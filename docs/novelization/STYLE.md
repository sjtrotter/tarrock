# TARROCK: THE FOOL'S READING — shared style guide (carried verbatim in every chapter brief)

## What this book is

A novelization of one player's complete playthrough of Tarrock — the True Shuffle run.
Register target: the warm, propulsive, emotionally direct crossover-fantasy register of
Harry Potter, His Dark Materials, and Twilight — wonder and intimacy over literary
opacity. Sentences that a twelve-year-old can ride and an adult can reread. The tone bar
underneath is the game's own: Fable/MediEvil storybook Britain — warm, dry, gently
mournful. Whimsy yes; snark and grimdark never.

## The narrator

The book is narrated by **the Querent** — the warm, wry, evasive voice that guides the
Fool in the game. The novel is framed as the Querent telling the whole Reading back,
afterward. Rules:

1. Third person limited on the Fool, in the Querent's voice. The narrator is fond,
   knowing, a little evasive about where it's all going — exactly the Fable-narrator
   energy of `characters.md` §The Querent. The narrator may address the reader briefly
   ("you would have liked her, I think") but sparingly — at most once or twice a chapter.
2. **The narrator knows the ending and is afraid of it.** Foreshadowing is allowed and
   encouraged, but always deflected with dry warmth ("but that comes later, and I am in
   no hurry to get there").
3. The narrator's identity (the world itself) is the book's buried twist, exactly as in
   the game. Nothing before the final chapter may give it away — but on reread, every
   evasion must be honest. The Querent never lies; they withhold.
4. The Querent's *spoken* lines inside scenes (dialogue in the Fool's ear) are rendered
   as dialogue like any character's, set off as speech "in the Fool's ear." The
   narration-voice and the in-scene voice are the same voice at different distances;
   let the reader half-notice that.
5. **Formal exceptions (do not deviate):**
   - Chapter 14 (the Stillmarsh) — the narrator withdraws. Plain, close third person,
     no narratorial address, no wit above the scene. The Querent speaks in-scene only
     where the quest script has them speak. The reader should feel the book's voice go
     quiet without being told why.
   - Chapter 22 (the Axis) — the narration cuts off mid-sentence at the wreath line,
     exactly as the Querent's voice does in MQ21, and the chapter continues in bare,
     close, present-tense third person until the Reading scene, where the narrator
     returns — in first person, "I", for the first time in the book.

## The Fool of this playthrough

The game's Fool is player-customizable; this book fixes one. She is a young woman —
brown-skinned, wiry, hair cropped rough with her own knife, dressed in traveler's
motley gone soft with weather. She is never named; she is "the Fool," and the Querent
calls her "little Excuse." She is not an amnesiac and not a mystery: she is *new*,
dealt into the world at the Cliff, and every wonder is a first wonder. Her default is
earnest — when the game offers dialogue choices, this player picked the earnest or
foolish option nearly every time (canonical: that option is "the character's soul").
Her wit, when it comes, is warm, never cutting. She asks questions because everything
is new, not because something was taken from her.

Render her chosen dialogue lines as the quest scripts give them (≤ 12 words, plain,
earnest); her interiority is the novel's addition and should be vivid — hunger, awe,
fear, stubbornness — but her *speech* stays modest. The gap between how much she feels
and how little she says is the character.

## Pip

A small scrappy white dog. He is never explained, never speaks, never performs concern,
and is never in real danger (single exception: the Mirrormarsh illusion beat in ch. 19,
owned by that chapter alone). People of the Stillmarsh bow to him; nobody says why; the
book never says why. Write him with the specific comic precision of a real dog —
businesslike, easily delighted, entirely unbothered by cosmic stakes. He is the story's
steady hand. Do not sentimentalize him; let him be funny and let the reader do the rest.

## Dialogue rules (from canon, non-negotiable)

- All quoted dialogue must be consistent with `docs/design/narrative.md` §Dialogue
  style guide: storybook British, contractions fine, no modern slang, anachronism only
  as rare deliberate comedy.
- Where the quest script gives a line, use it verbatim or trim it lightly — never
  contradict it. You may interleave new dialogue around scripted lines; new lines must
  sound like the same person on the same page.
- **Bound Arcana never say "I" with a personal name.** The name returns at the
  unbinding and only then. The unbinding always plays the same beats: the office cracks
  → the name returns (spoken by its owner, first person, for the first time) → the
  Trump is handed over personally. Nobody drops loot. Nobody is killed.
- The Fool's spoken lines stay ≤ 12 words, earnest-leaning.
- Every comic scene owns one honest beat; every sad scene owns one laugh.
- Common folk swear and bless by the deck: "by the Deal," "gods turn your card,"
  "a few pips short of a suit."
- The Querent's fourth-wall winks: in the game, one per quest. In the book, the
  narrator's asides absorb this license — but inside scenes, keep the Querent's spoken
  winks to at most one per chapter.

## World rules that show up in prose

- **Bound regions hold their breath.** No wind, no weather, no ambient motion; cloth
  posed mid-flutter; water glassy; dust motes hanging in light. "Wind-combed" grass is
  a *shape* the last wind left, not movement. But the world yields to touch: grass
  parts around the Fool and Pip and settles behind them; a brushed banner swings. This
  is the single most important piece of sensory canon — use it constantly.
- **Unbound regions gain motion.** Wind, weather, birdsong, loops that no longer loop.
  The Fool crossing from an unbound region into a bound one (or back) should always be
  felt in the prose.
- The White Rose at the Fool's belt: three petals to start, healing charges, regrows at
  Waystations and (slowly) in unbound regions only — in bound regions nothing grows,
  including her healing. Rose graftings found along the way raise the count (she ends
  the book at eight).
- The Bindle: quarterstaff and bundle, weapon and inventory both.
- Blanks: blank-faced humanoid soldiers bearing a suit-and-rank card as tabard. Felled,
  they slump "like a puppet set down kindly" and the card flutters off to raise a new
  bearer elsewhere — until the Fool carries Passage (ch. 14 on), after which what she
  fells stays ended. That change should be *felt* when it arrives.
- Fool's Chance: the perfect dodge — a held breath, the world going slow, an opening.
  Render it as instinct becoming art, not as a game mechanic. Never use game terms
  ("HP", "buff", "loadout", "spawn") in narration. Trumps, Fortune, petals, the Pocket
  Spread ARE in-world terms and may be used, in the way people in the world use them.
- The Trumps: each defeated Arcana hands over their card; the Fool wears three at a
  time in her Pocket Spread (Past worn-truth / Present willed-power / Future
  waiting-fate). Render slotting a card as something felt — a truth she carries, a
  power she can spend (it costs Fortune, which she earns by daring), a fate that will
  answer once when its condition comes.
- The Axis is visible from everywhere: a still silver point with a tiny turning figure.
  End-of-chapter glances toward it are a running motif — it should tug at the reader
  more each act.
- Money is Coins; the journal is the Almanack; rest points are Waystations (fast
  travel only after ch. 7's unbinding — before that she walks, after it the Chariot
  answers when called).

## Craft bar

- Chapters are substantial: hit the target word count in the brief (never under it by
  more than 10%). No summary-itis: dramatize, don't recap. Travel between regions is
  real page-time when the brief says so.
- Lively, specific dialogue. People interrupt, deflect, joke at the wrong moment.
  Nobody speaks in exposition. If a line could be printed on a tea towel, cut it.
- Concrete sensory anchors every scene: the quest docs are full of them (popcorn older
  than nations, wax-sealed books, a peach held out for three hundred years) — use the
  docs' own images first, invent more in their key.
- British spelling throughout (colour, honour, realise), matching the docs' dialogue.
- Chapter titles are the quest titles (given in each brief).
- Do not invent new canon: no new named NPCs beyond the docs (incidental unnamed folk
  are fine), no new place names, no new world rules. Where you need texture, pull from
  the region's canon NPCs and side-quest characters listed in the brief.

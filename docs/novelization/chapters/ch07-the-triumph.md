# Chapter Seven — The Triumph

The last of the Maw's living wind died against her back the moment her boot found the causeway stone, and after that there was no wind at all.

She had grown used to the wilds being loud about themselves — gorse hissing on the crags, a lion who now lay in her shadow of an afternoon and let her scratch the scarred place under his chin, because a Trump called Tame rode in her Past pocket, and beasts had stopped minding her the way the whole Maw, eventually, had. All of that fell away on the downward path, the way sound falls away behind a closing door, and what came up to meet her instead was a road so wide it looked built for something other than people.

Four wagons abreast, raised on dressed stone, curving away left and right until the curve became a ring, and the ring a rumour of distance she could not see the end of. To the inward side, past a low parapet, the land fell away in long green shelves toward the middle of everything, and there — closer than she had ever seen it — sat the Axis. A still, silver eye with no shadow in it, and at the heart of that stillness a single figure turned, and turned, and went on turning.

*You've been squinting at that thing since the Cliff*, said the Querent, not unkindly, in her ear. *Get used to it looking back. You're on its doorstep now, more or less. Every road on the Spread comes to this one eventually — it's the world's own spine, the Longroad. Rather appropriate you'd meet it walking on your own two feet, given how much use you've had of them.*

And then there was dust.

Not weather — she had learned better than to call anything on this road weather — but a long low banner of it, kicked up somewhere out of sight, moving fast and moving away. Under it, before she heard it at all, she felt it: a tramp of feet coming up through the stone into her boots, steady as a heartbeat, and a drum keeping time to a verse that had worn away and left only the beat.

Pip's ears went up together, which they did for almost nothing. He looked at the dust, and then at her, and something in his stance said, quite plainly, *that*.

*  *  *

The toll-fort straddled the road like a man standing with his legs too far apart: a squat gatehouse, a counterweighted arm, a brazier gone to cold ash. Nobody had paid a toll there in longer than anybody could count, and nobody had stopped there either, except the one soldier on the step with his boots off and his helmet upturned in his lap like a beggar's bowl. He looked up at her the way a man looks at rain he doesn't expect to amount to anything.

"New boots," he said. "New face. Neither of them marching. You're the strangest thing I've seen on this road in three centuries, and I've seen the same road three hundred times a year."

"Hello," she said, because it always seemed to work.

"Corporal Pike. Third of the line, or I was, when there was a first and a second worth counting." He turned the helmet over in his hands, not looking at it. "You'll be wanting to know about the parade. Everyone new wants to know about the parade. Then they stop being new, and they stop asking, and one day they're marching in it and can't remember why either."

"What are you all marching for?"

"A triumph. A great one — the General's triumph, the day the war was won and the Longroad was ours end to end." He said it crisply, out of drill, the way a man recites a thing he has stopped believing in but not stopped saying. Then something went out behind his eyes, the way a lamp goes out when the wick finally gives up. "I'll tell you the honest of it, since you're new enough to hear it. I don't recall which war. I don't recall the winning. I recall the *marching*. Three hundred years, and the marching's the only part that stuck."

She sat down on the step beside him, because there did not seem to be anything smarter to do with her legs just then.

"How do I get aboard?"

"On foot, poor devil. Nothing on this road's faster than the column, and there's no mount in the world yet." He nodded down the causeway, to where another fort's gate-arm stood raised against the sky. "You'd have to catch its tail and leap. Off a gate-arm's your best shot, if you've a mind to try something that stupid."

"I've a mind."

That got the smallest thing out of him — not quite a laugh, but its cousin. He cocked his head, listening to something in the stone, and jerked his chin down the road.

"There. Hear it? That's the tail of it, coming round again. If you're catching it, catch it now — it won't be back past here for a lap, and a lap out here is longer than you've been alive."

*  *  *

The dust arrived ahead of the column and then the column arrived inside the dust, and she ran.

She cut the bend the way Pike hadn't said to, because there hadn't been time: down the embankment in three strides mostly falling with intention, over the low toll-chain without breaking stride, taking the inside of the curve where the column had to take the long way round. Pip went ahead of her, low and delighted, born for exactly this, and for a few strides the gap held even, and then — grudgingly — began to close.

*You've crossed the entire Spread on those two feet, little Excuse, and never once complained to me about it — which I've noticed, and appreciated.* The Querent's voice had gone breathless on her behalf, which was new, and rather moving, in the two seconds she had to notice it. *Catch this and you'll not have to walk it again. Consider it the world's way of apologising for the mileage.*

The first gate-arm hung out over the road at exactly the wrong height to be sensible and the right height to be tried. She went up it hand over hand and threw herself at a roof already gone. Her hands found the running-board of the next carriage instead and held — held for three scraping yards that took most of the skin off her palms — and then didn't, and she was rolling clear across the road with the column thundering on without her, the specific fury of a near miss burning worse than the graze.

She was up and running for the second fort before the dust of the first attempt had settled.

This time the gate-arm gave her a rigging line instead — a trailing banner-rope, frayed, three centuries weathered, hanging low off a passing wagon like an afterthought. She jumped for rope rather than wood, and it held, cracking taut with a sound like a knuckle popping, and dragged her bodily up onto the roof of the war-train with her arms screaming and her heart somewhere it hadn't been before.

Pip was already there. He'd landed neat as a cat on the roof above, and sat looking back at her with the specific patience of a colleague who has been waiting slightly too long.

The world of solid, stopped ground dropped away behind her, and everything now was moving, and would not stop moving until somebody made it.

*  *  *

Up close, the triumph was a corpse that had forgotten to fall down.

The banners overhead — great regimental colours, once every shade a proud army owns — had bleached to a single uniform bone-white, the devices on them worn to ghosts of themselves. The gilt on the carriages had gone the green of old pennies. A trumpeter stood frozen mid-fanfare on a passing roof, cheeks puffed for a note three hundred years unblown, the bell of his instrument dented too deep to ever sound true again.

And the roofs were held. Parade-armoured Blanks — blank ovals where faces belonged, tabards bearing the column's higher ranks in colours faded to grey — wheeled and presented arms in perfect drill, inspecting a rank that would never be inspected, on a march with nowhere left on earth to arrive.

*There's your welcome party*, said the Querent, with the particular good cheer it kept for things about to be difficult. *Same as ever, dressed for the occasion. Fell one and its card blows straight off the back of the train, gone into the dust behind you. Nothing ends out here, remember. It only relocates.*

The first rank turned from its phantom inspection and levelled halberds at her, in step, because they had never learned to do anything any other way.

She fought forward, because forward was the only direction the roofs allowed. Each felled Blank folded at the knees and the waist, slumping the way a puppet slumps when the hand goes out of it, and each card peeled off the falling tabard and tumbled — not up, this time, but *backward*, snatched down the length of the moving train and off the tail into the road behind, already blowing toward some far stretch of causeway to raise a bearer where she was not. She stopped watching them go. There wasn't time, and there was always another rank.

The gaps between carriages had to be leapt outright, the roofs sliding and swaying underfoot with the whole column's gallop. Where a roof was barricaded she went up into the banner rigging instead, hand over hand through bleached canvas gone soft as old sailcloth, and from the top of one long climb the whole procession opened out beneath her at once: carriage after carriage after carriage, and at the very front of it, small with distance and wrapped in his own private cloud of dust, a single figure stood at the reins and did not look back.

*Forward*, said the Querent, mid-fight, easy as breathing. *Always forward — it's the only direction this whole army understands, so borrow it. Don't try to hold ground; there isn't any out here. There's just the next roof. And the man at the front who hasn't turned round in three hundred years.*

*  *  *

The head of the train was a high open platform, more throne than driver's box, and on it stood a man in tarnished ceremonial armour with a laurel gone black on his brow. One gauntleted fist was wrapped white-knuckled around a fistful of reins that ran forward into the dust to a team she could not see. His other hand held a cavalry sabre, loose and expert and entirely unbothered.

He heard her come up the last carriage. He did not turn his head — the reins would not permit it — but he spoke forward, into the wind of his own passage, in a voice built for carrying down a mile of marching men.

"A runner. Good. About time the column got fresh legs — the last recruit's been marching so long he's forgotten his own name, and so, if we're honest, has the man in front of him." The sabre came up, still without a backward glance. "You want a place in this triumph? Earn it. Come forward. Everyone in this column came forward once. It's the coming to a halt that none of us ever managed."

He fought one-handed, sabre only, disciplined and punishing, the rein-fist never loosening by so much as a finger. It was as much a fight against distance as steel — he gave ground along the traces, never toward her, always deeper into the procession's own momentum, so every clean parry that should have ended it only kept the reins where they were.

"Better," he said, mid-exchange, almost admiring. "Much better. But you're fighting the wrong war, runner — you keep striking for a finish, and there is no finish. There's only the road, and the road goes round."

She landed the blow that should have ended it — a full, committed strike, clean through his guard, the kind that had felled Blanks all morning — and it changed nothing. He rocked, recovered, drove forward another stride, sabre already back up, entirely undefeated, because a sabre had never been the thing holding any of this together.

The rose at her belt stirred then, turning of its own accord toward the fist on the reins — the one thing on the whole thundering train that was not, in fact, moving relative to itself.

*You can't cut him loose, little Excuse*, said the Querent, and there was nothing playful left in it now. *Nobody's holding* him *— he's holding the road, and he'll hold it till his arm comes off before he lets it stop on a sword-stroke. There's only one way this parade ever halts, and it's the same way it started. Somebody's hand on the reins.* Take them. *Take them.*

*  *  *

She dropped the Bindle across her back and went for his free hand instead of his blade, and closed her fingers over his on the leather, and the reins came alive under her grip the instant they did.

It was like taking hold of a river — a living, dragging weight, the pull of an entire army's forward want, three centuries deep and still accelerating — and for one long moment she was lifted onto her toes by it, hauled along, losing, boots skating on the platform boards.

Then Pip set his teeth in the hem of her coat and hauled too, all four paws braced, adding an ounce of furious dog to a war of centuries, and she dug her heels in beside his and pulled, and the Charioteer — for the first time in three hundred years — felt the reins go backward in his hand.

"No," he said, and his head came round at last to look at what was stopping him, appalled. "No — not yet — just a little *further*, we're nearly — we have always been nearly there —"

She won it by inches. Only by inches, and only barely, her whole body one long refusal, Pip a small furious anchor at her ankle, until the reins came back into her fist the way a held breath finally lets go.

And three hundred years of momentum began, at last, to die.

It did not stop all at once. There was too much of it, too long a run of it, and the halt went down the train like that same held breath, only slower: the lead carriage's wheels locked and *screamed*, a bright line scored down the causeway stone, and behind it carriage after carriage shuddered and slewed to a stop it had never once practised, the whole column telescoping down into one vast unstoppable stillness.

The dust that had hidden this procession for three centuries came forward, overtook it, and hung — and then began, softly, to settle. The banners overhead, deprived all at once of whatever had kept them flying, folded. One after another, down the whole visible mile of the train, they lost their shape and came down over the carriages, bone-white, gentle, like sheets drawn up over furniture in a house being closed for the last time.

The drum stopped. The cadence that had lived in Corporal Pike's own feet from a fort away simply ceased, and the silence it left behind was the loudest thing the Longroad had heard in three hundred years.

The train, at the very end, gave one last long groaning skid, and was still.

She stood in that stillness with the reins slack under her hands and the Charioteer's hands slack under hers, and it did not feel, for the length of one held breath, like a victory at all. It felt like something being laid out.

*  *  *

The office cracked first.

The black laurel, the tarnished armour, the whole hard carriage of *the General* — it came apart the way the halt had come down the train, front to back, and underneath it was only a soldier, old, appallingly tired, looking at his own halted triumph the way a man looks at something he had been certain could not exist.

"It stopped," he said. "By the Deal — it *stopped*. Three hundred years I held it forward because a commander does not halt a triumph, a commander does not —" He stopped himself, the way the train had. "It turns out all it ever wanted was for someone to pull the other way."

Something surfaced then, the way a name surfaces — not a wound, only a man coming up for air after a very long time under water. His grip on the reins went loose entirely.

"...*Cassian*." He said it the way a man says a word in a tongue he was born to and hasn't heard aloud in centuries. "That's — yes. That was the name before the rank ate it. Before I was *the General*, before I was *the Charioteer*, before I was three miles of dust going nowhere." He laughed once, nothing like the crisp drill-laugh of an hour before. "Gods, the *sound* of it."

He looked down the length of the halted train — the folded banners, the stilled marchers, a drummer's raised sticks come at last to rest — and the horror of it and the relief of it arrived in him at exactly the same moment.

"Do you understand what you've done? You've let it *arrive*. Three centuries I marched a victory with its destination cut clean out of it — momentum with nowhere left to be, which is the cruellest thing you can do to a man who only ever knew how to advance. And you simply stopped. Turned round. Looked at me." He shook his head slowly. "I'd forgotten a person could."

He unwrapped the reins from his hand — the leather had left three hundred years of itself pressed white into his palm — and where they had been was a card, warm, held out to her.

"Trump VII. Triumph, if anyone ever writes it down properly. Take it — and take this with it: it will answer when you call it, and carry you anywhere you please, at any speed you like." He folded her fingers over the card. "But it only ever learned one hard lesson, same as me. *Stopping is the difficult part.* Mind that, and you'll not end up a General."

*  *  *

Stopped ground was a different country than moving ground had ever been.

Where the dust had circled there was only a long settling haze and a mile of halted train, its marchers standing about in ones and twos, blinking, holding parade-armour with nowhere left to belong to. Some were Blanks, and simply stood, patient as furniture. Some were people — a musician turning a dented instrument over as if seeing it for the first time, a colour-bearer folding a bone-white banner with unpractised care — and those had begun, slowly, to walk off wherever pleased them.

Away up the slope toward the Maw's crags something tawny and enormous lay along a ledge, watching the stilled road the way a landlord watches a tenant finally settle a debt. She lifted a hand to it out of habit more than hope. Its tail went once against the stone, and that seemed answer enough from something that no longer had to answer to anybody.

Ahead, where the ring met a region road, a caravan sat stopped at the edge of the causeway — drivers who had waited three hundred years for a parade to clear a junction, unable quite to believe the road was theirs. The lead wagon eased out onto the stone, wheels loud and ordinary and wonderful in the new quiet, and she knew the painted sides of it before she knew anything else about it.

"OI!" Flick was hanging half off the tailboard, waving both arms as though she might miss an entire touring show trundling past at eight feet away. "It's HER! Tomkin, it's the *sky-woman*!"

"Seen her," said Old Tomkin comfortably, from the driver's bench, five clubs going round in a lazy figure without once dropping. "Told you the roads'd take us somewhere worth being." He caught all five in one hand, briefly, to touch two fingers to his brow at her, and went straight back to it.

"We're going *everywhere*!" Flick shouted, already turning to shout something else at Tansy Quill in the wagon behind. "Fresh eyes for miles — mind the wheels, sky-woman, and MIND THE WHEELS, PIP—"

Pip did not mind the wheels. He watched the wagon go with the satisfaction of a dog updating a list of acquaintances now travelling the world with him.

*Hear that?* said the Querent, gently. *Wheels, going somewhere, because somebody decided somewhere was a place you were allowed to reach. And every Waystation you've rested at just woke up properly — rest at one now and step from it to any other, quick as drawing a card.* A pause, drier. *You've made the world a great deal smaller. That's a kindness. Mostly.*

*  *  *

A little way round the ring stood a roadside inn, long and low and built wrong, she realised — built to serve a road going *past*, not a road going anywhere. Its whole front was a serving-counter open to the causeway, a hundred tin cups on hooks, a great cold range built to feed a marching band at the same bar of the same tune every evening, forever.

A woman stood behind the counter with a ladle in her hand and nothing in the pot, watching the mile of stopped train the way you watch weather doing something it has never once done before.

"Every evening for three hundred years the band came past at the same bar of the same tune," she said, without being asked, "and I had a hundred cups poured and waiting. They never stopped to drink them. But they came *past*. You could set your grief by it."

"I'm sorry," the Fool said, not sure yet what she was sorry for.

"Don't be. Not yet." Widow Tallow set the ladle down at last. "I built this place around a parade that would never once stop for me. Do you understand how much easier that is than the alternative? You can love a thing that never stops. It never leaves. It never dies. It never gets round to breaking your heart, because it never gets round to *anything*."

"The road's alive now." The Fool said it carefully, because it seemed to want carrying carefully. "That's a good thing."

"It is. It's the best thing." A beat, and then, despite herself, a wet laugh escaped her. "Listen to me — three hundred years pouring cups for men who couldn't drink them, and now the road's free, and my one complaint is nobody warned me the marching band had a *tune*, and I only ever heard the one bar of it, and now I'll never hear how it ended." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, briskly. "Grieving a parade. There's a fool in this exchange, and by the Deal it isn't you."

She waved the Fool off, not unkindly, and turned back to her cold range — and the Fool, going, saw her take one cup down off its hook, slowly, and pour it full, and set it on the counter for whoever the new road brought next.

*  *  *

Back at the near fort, Corporal Pike sat exactly where she had left him — boots off, helmet in his lap — but he was no longer watching the road for dust. There was no dust left to watch for.

"It's quiet," he said, looking at his own bare feet as if seeing them for the first time in three centuries. "I keep waiting for the cadence to come round and put my feet right, and it — doesn't. I've caught myself standing up to fall in three times since it stopped. Habit. Just habit, marching a man about with nobody left to give the order."

She sat down beside him again, and didn't tell him what to do with any of it. It didn't seem hers to tell.

He turned the boots over in his hands for a long while, the way a man considers a decision that has finally, after three hundred years, come round to being his.

"No, don't tell me to go," he said at last, though she hadn't. "I've had three centuries of being told where to put my feet, and I find I'd like to be the one who decides the last of it." He looked down the freed road, then back at his cold brazier, and stayed. "Maybe I'll keep the fort. Sweep it. Greet whoever the new road brings, the way somebody once greeted me and I've plumb forgotten who." He raised a hand toward the stilled train and held it there — not quite a salute, and then, deciding, exactly a salute. "When I march again it'll be because I chose the road. Not because the drum did. That'll do nicely."

*  *  *

She went out onto the open causeway to try the thing Cassian had told her would answer, half certain it wouldn't. She reached for Triumph the way she'd reached for Manifest that first night at the Prestige — not with her hand, with something behind it.

The sound came first: hooves striking causeway stone like a drumroll starting from nowhere. Then the Chariot arrived exactly as absurd and exactly as glorious as three hundred years of somebody's unbroken belief in it — a high gilded thing, laurel-worked, drawn by a team that gleamed like poured metal and cast no shadow, thundering up out of empty road with far more ceremony than any traveller had any right to. It stopped precisely where she stood, its wheels never quite settling into the stone the way an ordinary cart's would.

Pip was up on the platform before she'd got a foot on the step, sitting bolt upright at the rail with the satisfaction of a small dog claiming the finest chariot on the Spread as, essentially, his own idea.

"Oh, sit *down*," she said, laughing before she could stop herself — the first proper laugh the Longroad had had out of her — and took the reins that had cost so much, and let the ridiculous glorious thing carry her as fast as she liked, going nowhere in particular, because for once that was allowed.

*  *  *

She brought it up short — she had learned that part too, now, same as Cassian had — at a wayside shrine off the ring where the causeway met a lesser road: a low stone arch gone soft with ivy, a basin, offerings gone to moss. It could have been the very shrine from the Cliff-meadow's edge, or its cousin twenty times removed; she had stopped being surprised at how alike they all were.

The rose at her belt was already turning toward it before she'd sat down. Then, all down the ring in both directions, small lights began to wake — one after another, unhurried, as if something enormous and long asleep were counting itself awake finger by finger: a lantern here, a wayside flame there, a glow rising off a Waystation two regions distant that she had rested at only once, weeks and a whole different life ago. She felt each one land somewhere behind her ribs as it caught, faint and warm, like a hand laid gently on a shoulder she hadn't known was tired.

*There*, said the Querent, very quietly, for once without a joke standing ready behind it. *The whole network, awake at last. Rest here tonight and it's exactly as though you'd rested at every one of them. Step from any lit lamp to any other, quick as drawing a card. You've walked the whole Spread on your own two aching feet since the Cliff, and I don't think I ever once said how much I noticed it. I noticed it.*

She built a small fire out of habit more than need, because the Rose had already unfurled its fourth petal fully open the moment she sat down, healed and idle, and there was nothing left that evening for the flames to mend. Pip curled against her leg and was asleep before the second stick caught.

She took the cards out of her coat, one at a time, and laid them on her knee in the firelight, the way she had begun, without deciding to, to do at the end of every long day now. Manifest. Bloom. Union. Rite. Tame. Triumph.

Six.

She had never counted them all in a row before, not properly, not with nothing chasing her and nothing left to fight that evening. She looked at them a long while — a showman's warm hand round hers, a garden learning to grow again, a wedding finally crossed, a hymn finally sung true, a lion finally let go of gently, and now this: a parade, mercifully, made to stop.

Six endings, and every one of them, she saw now, laid out plain like a hand she hadn't asked to be dealt, had been put right by *stopping* something. The show had had to end. The garden had had to let its winter come. A whole life on one bank had had to be left behind. The lion had had to be let go of. And now three hundred years of marching, brought to a dead and mournful halt on a road built for nothing else.

She had thought, at the Cliff, that she was here to fix things.

She sat very still with that thought, the way you sit still with a stone in your boot you haven't decided yet whether to stop and remove.

"What's this all for?" she asked, into the fire, not really expecting an answer, which was generally the surest way of getting one from that particular voice.

There was a pause just a fraction too long to be nothing.

*For the world*, said the Querent at last, lightly, in the tone of a man setting a very full cup down very carefully so as not to spill a drop of it. *What else would any of this be for? Go to sleep, little Excuse. You've earned it six times over tonight, and there's a great deal of road left before we're through counting.*

She let it go. I will tell you, since she could not yet, that it was not quite an answer — and that I have never once lied to her. Only, on occasion, set the cup down very carefully.

Far off across the ringed width of the Spread, past the last of the waking lamps, the Axis sat closer than it had ever sat, a still silver eye with no shadow in it, and at the heart of it, patient beyond patience, something small went on turning. She watched it longer than she had let herself before. Then she banked the fire, and put six cards away against her heart, and slept.

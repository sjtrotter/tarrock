# Interlude — Wishes

*I read cards for a living, which is a peculiar trade to practise inside a world made of
them, and I have never once found that funny out loud, whatever Old Tomkin thinks. But
the wells have woken now, all down the roads we travel, and a well full of wishes reads
very like a spread full of cards — turn one over, and somebody's whole heart is sitting
there in front of you, wanting to be told what it means. So that is what I have taken to
doing of an evening, between shows, while the Troupe packs up and the last of the
audience finds their way home under a sky that still won't quite go dark. I do not charge
for it. Some things ought not to be sold twice. — M. Fen*

*  *  *

Left at the well by the crossroads shrine, weighted with a stone, in a child's careful
letters:

> *i wish my dad's leg would stop hurting when it rains. he says its an old soldiers
> ache and laughs about it but i have herd him at night when he thinks im asleep*

Answered. I know this because the coin was gone from beside it and a second slip had
been added underneath, in the same hand, only larger, as though the writer had grown an
inch doing it:

> *it stopped raining on him. thank you. i didnt know you could say thank you to a well
> but i am saying it anyway*

*  *  *

Pinned to a post at a Cups landing, in a hand gone loose with drink and feeling, both:

> To whoever's listening — a toast, not a wish, though I'm told the well doesn't much
> mind the difference. To my brother, three hundred years drowned and three hundred
> years missed just as hard on the last day as the first. If you can't bring him back,
> bring me one evening where the missing doesn't feel like drowning too. I'll take that.
> I'll take that and be grateful.

*  *  *

A Swords hand, small and exact, folded into quarters and tucked under a stone with
military precision:

> Request: that my brother-in-arms, lost at the Bastion siege before the Stall, be told
> — by whatever means the well finds proper — that the wall held. It held. He died
> thinking it hadn't, and he was wrong, and I have carried the correcting of that error
> for longer than is reasonable for one man to carry one fact. Please attend to this at
> your earliest convenience.

I do not know if wells have a sense of humour. I hope, for that soldier's sake, that
this one does.

*  *  *

Charcoal, on bark, at a Wands well deep enough in the woods that I had to be shown the
path:

> I WISH I COULD FINISH THE CHAIR. Three hundred years on the same four legs and never
> once got to see if it would hold a body's weight. Doesn't have to be a good chair.
> Just wants to be a FINISHED one for once in its miserable life. Same as the rest of us,
> I suppose.

*  *  *

And then — because it would not be a Coins wish otherwise, and because I laughed aloud
reading it, which is the honest laugh I promised myself I would let this whole page have
somewhere — an itemised slip, weighted not with a stone but with an actual half-coin,
filed at a well outside a grain exchange, ruled into three careful columns:

> WISH: modest prosperity, self and dependents (2)
> COST TO WELL: one (1) coin, as offered, herewith
> CONTINGENCY, should prosperity arrive IMMODEST: further coin remitted within the
> quarter, no argument entered into, terms binding upon both parties
>
> *Signed, in good faith and fair dealing, as befits the transaction —*

Withdrawn a week later, with a second note pinned atop the first, in the same careful
hand, considerably less careful this time:

> Amendment: cancel above in full. Have decided modest is plenty. Was greedy asking for
> more when the harvest already came in fair and my daughter is well and the roof
> doesn't leak. Apologies for the paperwork. Keep the coin anyway. Call it thanks.

*  *  *

One I nearly missed, tucked at the very back of a well I was reading purely for the
Troupe's own amusement, no ceremony to it, written by someone who plainly was not the
wisher:

> This one isn't mine. Met a young woman on the road — brown-skinned, cropped hair, a
> white dog that wouldn't leave her heel — and she looked so tired and so kind and so
> entirely unwilling to wish anything for herself that I have gone and done it for her,
> which I hope isn't against the rules. I don't know what she'd have asked. So I've
> asked instead that wherever she's walking, when she gets there, somebody is glad to
> see her. That's all. That's the whole of it. Seemed the least the road could do.

I read that one twice before I moved on. I will not pretend otherwise.

*  *  *

And a child's, left not at a well but simply spoken aloud to the sky, which a passing
Cups grandmother wrote down afterward because she said it seemed a shame to lose it:

> "I wish the stars had somewhere dark to stand. They're all up there already, I can
> feel them, but there's no dark for them to shine in yet, and that doesn't seem fair
> after they came all this way."

*(The grandmother's note beneath: The sky hasn't managed patience even a tenth so
graceful as hers. She says she doesn't mind waiting. Says the stars have waited longer
than she has, so it would be rude to complain first.)*

*  *  *

Near a shore I will not name, because he would not thank me for the naming, an old
lantern still burns at a well nobody expects to answer — a light kept, not a wish left,
for a wife three hundred years past keeping company with anyone. He knows exactly what
the flame means and cannot, will not, put it out. That well will never do what wells are
for. He tends it anyway. I have seen a great many wishes on this road since the water
woke, and that unanswerable one is the truest of the lot, and I only mention it here
because a page of wishes that pretends every light gets its wish would be a page of
lies, and I have told enough of those in this trade to know better.

*  *  *

Here, then, is my own reading of the whole spread of them, for what a fortune-teller's
reading is worth in a world that has started answering its own questions without my
help: every wish above is the same wish, said in four different accents and priced in
four different currencies. *Let it matter.* That the leg stopped aching, that the wall
held, that the chair finally takes a body's weight, that somebody far off is glad to see
a tired young woman when she arrives — none of it is really about the getting. It's
about it having counted for something, once, to someone, out loud, where the world could
hear.

I never made one myself. Never dropped a coin, never wrote a slip, though I have sat
beside more wells this last season than I could number. People ask why, expecting some
sad story, and there isn't one — I read the cards for a living and I have always known
better than to also be the client. I'll tell you the truth instead, since I'm apparently
in the business of it tonight: I did once want a table of my own, a proper reading-tent,
striped canvas, my name over the flap in gold. I don't have it, and travelling instead
turned out better than the tent ever would have. I don't regret the want. I only
stopped needing it answered, which a wishing-well, if you listen to what it's actually
for, will tell you is the finer trick anyway.

# Chapter Eleven — The Light on the Mountain

The Gallowwood let her down properly at last — feet under her instead of over her, the
canopy bridges giving way to ordinary uphill ground the way a held breath finally lets go
— and she climbed out of it still walking a little sideways, the habit of two days spent
upside-down. Something in her coat, a card worn close over the ribs, had made falling
into a thing that simply happened to her rather than a thing she had to survive; she'd
tested it twice more than the ordeal required, off ledges she hadn't needed to jump, just
to feel the world catch her. Pip watched both experiments with the weary tolerance of a
dog whose person has discovered a new way of showing off.

The treeline thinned, then stopped altogether, and the last stunted pine gave up its
argument with the slope and let the mountain have it plainly.

Above, the sky did not so much begin as fail to begin: a held breath of a sky, cloudless
and starless both, blue-grey and even from one horizon to the other, giving her eye
nothing at all to land on. Not evening. Not the deep blue before morning. Simply dusk,
the way a held note is simply a note, going nowhere, going on.

The cold met her a step past the last pine and did not let go again — not the sharp cold
of wind, for there was no wind here to carry it, but something patient, seeped into the
stone, the kind of cold that has never once had to hurry. Underfoot, frost furred every
rock in a rime that had never thawed and refrozen; it had simply always been this exact
frost, since before her grandmother's grandmother, if she'd had one.

Pip pressed in against her leg. Not frightened — she knew frightened Pip by now — just
cold, and glad of the company, his breath going up in two small ghosts that hung a moment
and were still hanging when she looked back a dozen paces on.

Far off, on a shoulder of rock she could barely separate from the dusk around it, a single
point of warm light moved. Amber, low, unhurried, walking a ridgeline at something very
like a person's pace. It went behind a fold of stone and did not come out the other side.

*There it is*, said the Querent, and there was something in the voice she hadn't heard
before — not quite fondness, not quite dread, some third thing that lived between the
two. *The famous light. Three hundred years it's walked those ridges, and three hundred
years nobody's laid a hand on it. Folk come up here meaning to. Folk go home again. The
mountain is very good at sending people home.*

She looked about her for a road sign, a scuffed path worn thin — the ordinary furniture
of every region she'd crossed so far. There was none of it. No trail. No waypost. Only
rock, and frost, and dusk, and, very far off, a light already gone.

"There's no path."

*No.* The Querent let that sit a moment, unadorned, which was its own kind of answer.
*This one doesn't hold your hand, little Excuse. This one asks you to read the ground.*

She settled the Bindle on her shoulder and started up.

*  *  *

She had gone perhaps a mile — hard to say, where the ground gave no landmark worth the
name and the sky gave none at all — when a second light showed itself. This one did not
move.

It sat low in a fold of the slope: a small stone shack, one window lit gold, a thread of
chimney-smoke standing straight up and staying there, the exact shape smoke keeps where
there is no wind to argue it out of that shape. Beside it, under a slate lean-to, stood a
stone basin swept clear of frost, and as she came near it the rose at her belt gave its
small private shiver — five petals now, stirring against a stillness they had no business
stirring against.

The door opened before her knuckles reached it. A woman stood in the gap: small, upright
as a fence-post, a shawl pinned close with a bone pin, a ladle still in one hand. She
looked the Fool over — boots, staff, the frost whitening her eyebrows — with the
thoroughness of someone who has looked over every cold and lost thing to climb this path
longer than she's troubled to count.

"Come in out of it, then," she said. "Kettle's on. Kettle's always on — no sense a
mountain this cold and no kettle." She looked down. "Dog first, you after. He knows where
the fire is better than you do."

Pip went past her ankles at a trot, entirely satisfied with this arrangement, and the
Fool followed him in out of the dusk.

*  *  *

Inside was a single room built, as far as the Fool could tell, entirely around the
business of being warm. Cots lined one wall — more than one traveller could ever need,
because more than one had always, at some point, needed them all at once. A long table,
its boards worn pale by decades of forearms. On a shelf above the hearth, a row of mugs,
no two alike — each of them, she understood without being told, left by a traveller who
had meant, at the time, to come back for it.

"Fenner," the woman said, setting down the ladle and taking up the tin mug instead,
filling it without asking. "I keep the waypoint. Have done since — well." A small
motion, taking in the mugs, the cots, the whole cold weight of *since*. "Since keeping it
was worth doing. You warm your hands, and I'll tell you the one thing everybody comes up
here to ask."

The mug came into the Fool's palms warm all the way through.

"The light," the Fool said.

"The light." Widow Fenner sat across from her, hands folded, the shawl-pin catching the
firelight. "It walks the far ridges every dusk — which is every hour, up here, dusk being
all we get. Never the same slope twice. Never close enough to hail. Folk set out after it
bright as buttons, and the mountain walks them in circles till the cold talks sense back
into them, and they come here, and I feed them, and they go down home."

"Has anyone ever caught it?"

"Not in my time, and my time's long. Plenty got close enough to smell the lamp-oil.
Nobody got close enough to be *offered a chair*." A small, dry pause. "There's a
difference, though I couldn't tell you what."

Pip, who had claimed the hearthstone as though it had been reserved for him personally,
sighed the enormous sigh of a small dog settling into the one good decision of his day.

"Why keep the kettle on for strangers?"

Something crossed Widow Fenner's face and was gone before it could be called an
expression. "Because somebody has to be the warm window on a cold mountain. Been my
whole purpose, love. A body likes a purpose." Her chin came up half an inch, daring the
Fool to make something of it. "Don't mind me. Drink up."

The Fool drank. It went down into her like the first honest thing that had happened to
her in a mile.

"That's the trick nobody credits," Widow Fenner went on, watching the fire rather than
her guest now. "The light doesn't run. It just keeps *one ridge* on. Always one ridge.
And one ridge is exactly as far as a body can bear to be from a warm fire before they
turn round." She turned the empty ladle over in her lap, an old habit with nothing left
to stir. "Clever, that. Cruel, maybe. I've stopped deciding which."

"Go on, then," she said at last, standing, brisk again, the moment folded away and put
back on its shelf, "since you've the look. Take the dog. Read the ground. And when the
cold starts making sense — and it will — you remember there's a window down here still
lit. Coming back isn't losing. Most folk never learn that." She pressed a heel of warm
bread into the Fool's hand at the door, the way you'd press a coin into a child's fist,
and made nothing of it whatsoever. "Off you go."

*  *  *

The ground tilted up past the shack into a long grey fan of loose scree, every stone
wanting to slide the moment weight came onto it, and here, for the first time, the
mountain had left tracks: cold fire-embers, long dead, half-buried in the rubble, and
between them boot-prints pressed into frost that had never once thawed to take them back.

"Seek, then," she told Pip; his nose went down and his tail went up and he cast forward
in the busy zigzag of a dog with proper work to do, pausing at each dead ember only long
enough to confirm it and move on.

She followed him up, reading the tilt of the boot-prints, the places where a hand had
gone down to steady a fall, the drift of old ash where it lay thickest in a boulder's
lee. Twice the scree gave way under her and sent her sliding with a clatter that went
nowhere in the windless air — and both times something in her simply refused to be
afraid of it, the fall ending with no more consequence than a bad step on level ground,
the card over her heart doing exactly what it had promised on the Gallowwood's bridges.

At the top of the fan, over a lip of broken rock, the light showed itself close for the
first time — a hundred yards off across a shallow gully, held low by a shape the dusk
would not quite resolve into a person. For one entire held second, it was *right there*.

She crested the lip at something close to a run.

The light was already on the next ridge over, going away at the same unhurried walk it
had kept the whole way up the mountain. Not fleeing. Simply keeping its one ridge.

Something in her chest did a small ugly lurch — hope arriving and leaving inside the same
breath — and she stood with her fists closed on nothing.

*Ah. You felt that, did you*, said the Querent, gently, into the quiet after. *The little
lurch of it being close and then not. Everyone feels that one. It's the mountain's
opening move, and it has never once failed to work.* A pause, then, more useful: *Don't
chase the light itself. You can't. Chase the ground it walked. That, you can read.*

*  *  *

Past the gully the land opened into a valley so wide and flat that its far walls stood
only as a darker line against a dusk barely darker still, the whole floor drowned to the
knee in fog that closed silent behind her boots. Overhead: nothing. No moon, no star, no
mark of any kind on the lid of the sky to say which way was which. Set down in the
middle of it and turned once, a traveller could not have told you which wall they'd come
in through.

She walked perhaps thirty steps toward where the light had last shown, and understood by
the thirty-first that she had already lost the line — the fog the same in every
direction, her own footprints swallowed the moment the mist closed over them.

*Nothing up there to help you tonight*, the Querent said. *Whoever's carrying that lamp
picked the one corner of the world where a light is the only thing worth looking at.
That's either despair or it's an invitation. I've a guess which.*

She crouched instead, hand flat to the frozen ground, and found — faint, but there — the
lean of old churned earth where other feet had gone before her, turning uncertainly,
doubling back, here and there going purposefully on. She followed the purposeful ones as
far as they held, then called Pip in low and let him cast ahead into the fog, nose
working the cold air for the one thing in all this white nothing that had a smell at
all: lamp oil, faint and sweet and wrong on the mountain's clean air.

Twice the light showed through the mist — once dead ahead, drawing her true, and once
down a false spur her own feet very nearly took before Pip planted himself square across
it and would not be moved, an immovable small object between her and a drop she could
not see and did not, in the end, need to.

"Good dog," she said, low, and meant it the way you mean a thing that has just kept you
alive.

She crossed the valley by dirt and by dog, and never once by sky, and came up at last
against a slope of standing rock she would not have found any other way.

*  *  *

The slope beyond was studded with cairns — dozens of them, stacked stone of every age,
some fresh, some slumped by centuries into shapeless heaps that had forgotten they were
ever meant to be a shape. Not grave markers, she understood, looking closer: turning-
points, each one built by somebody who had decided, on this exact step of this exact
climb, that this was as far as they were willing to go, and had raised a small monument
to the deciding before turning back toward Fenner's fire. Some had a scrap of cloth
knotted to the topmost stone, fluttering nowhere in the still air. One, low down where a
small hand could reach, had a child's shoe set atop it, laces tied in a bow that had not
come undone in longer than she wanted to think about.

Among the cairns, wrapped in three coats against a cold that plainly hadn't the first
idea what it was up against, sat a traveller adding a stone, with great cheerful care, to
a cairn of their own.

"Building mine up proper this time," they said, without turning round. "You after the
light? Don't. It's a ghost, that. Old lamplighter died on his round and never noticed,
still walks it, still lighting what's already lit. My gran said so. My gran was *rarely*
right, but she was right about that."

"How do you know it's a ghost?"

"Well, nobody's caught it, have they." The traveller settled the stone, patted it fondly.
"Living things get caught. Only a ghost keeps its distance three hundred years." A grin,
gap-toothed, entirely unbothered. "And a ghost you can stop chasing without feeling a
fool about it. Handy, a ghost. Very restful."

They looked at their own cairn, and the cheer thinned by exactly one shade, the way a
fire does when nobody's tending it.

"Built this the first time forty years back," they said, quieter. "Rebuild it every time
I come up and lose my nerve. Suppose the cairn's the thing I actually finish." A short,
surprised laugh, at their own expense. "That's *something*, though, isn't it. Catching a
thing you can finish."

The Fool looked at the shoe on its low cairn, and said the only honest thing she had.

"I hope you finish the climb one day."

"So do I, pet." They were already reaching for the next stone. "One more ridge. Always
tell myself one more ridge." A dry chuckle, not unhappy. "Mountain heard me the first
time, I think, and it's been laughing ever since."

She left them there, stacking stones with the patience of forty years, and went on up.

*  *  *

Beyond the highest cairn anyone had ever built — the ground past it unmarked, unclaimed
by three hundred years of anybody — there was only slope, and the light, closer now than
it had let her see it since the scree.

She followed it across three linked ridges, and somewhere on the second something
changed. The light was not gaining distance any more. She walked; it walked; the gap
held a beat, then closed a hand's width, then a stride, and did not open again.

*Look at that*, said the Querent, real astonishment under the dryness now, three hundred
years of watching finally catching up with itself. *It's slowing. No — you're not
gaining because you're faster. You're gaining because you didn't stop. That's the whole
difference. That was always the whole difference. Nobody ever failed to catch that
light. They only ever failed to keep walking toward it.*

She crested the third ridge with her breath tearing and her legs gone to water, and the
light was waiting for her at the top of the rise beyond — not receding, not moving at
all, simply standing, as though whoever carried it had turned, at last, and seen, for the
first time in three centuries, someone still coming.

*Three hundred years*, the Querent said, quiet now, almost to itself, *and the one who
followed all the way turns out to be the card that can't be told where to stop.* A dry,
private breath of a laugh. *I'd say I planned that, little Excuse. But we both know
who's actually been doing the walking, and it isn't me.*

She went up the last rise at a walk, because she found, when it came to it, that she no
longer had the strength or the wish to run.

*  *  *

Over the crest, in a hollow tucked out of a wind that had never once reached it, a small
fire burned, steady and low. Beside it, set down on a flat stone — set *down*, for the
first time in three hundred years — sat the lantern, its light close up warm and
entirely unremarkable, an ordinary good light doing an ordinary good job of pushing back
the dark.

And beside the lantern sat a man.

He was old the way the mountain was old — not worn by it, arrived at some far side of
time where age had stopped happening to him and become simply the shape he was. A
traveller's cloak gone thin from long use. A face gentle and remote, the face of someone
who has thought so long he has forgotten there was ever meant to be an end to it.

He did not startle. He did not rise. On the flat stone in front of him sat two cups of
tea, both steaming, both — she could see it even from here — just cool enough to have
been poured a little while ago, by someone who had known, at last, that company was
coming.

"There you are," he said, as she came into the firelight, his voice as unhurried as
everything else about him. "Sit — the second cup's gone cold about forty times over the
years, but I keep pouring it." He nodded at it, not quite smiling, not quite anything so
simple as sad. "A lantern's no use to a man alone. It's for being *followed* by. And in
three hundred years, do you know" — he said it without a grain of self-pity, the way
you'd remark on an odd fact of weather — "nobody once followed far enough to be worth the
pouring."

Pip walked past her legs without ceremony and sat himself down against the old man's
knee, entirely at ease, as though this were the seat he had come all this way to claim.
The old man rested a hand on his head, absently, like the most natural thing in the
world.

She sat. The stone was cold through her trousers and the fire was not, and between the
two of them she found, for the first time since the treeline, that she was properly
warm.

"Not that the light was ever hidden," he said. "Anyone could have caught it. It only
ever asked one thing — that a body keep walking after it when the fire behind them said
turn round." A small, private shake of the head. "Turns out that's the rarest thing
there is. Rarer than courage. Courage is loud. This is just — not stopping."

"Why keep walking away, then," she asked, "if you wanted catching?"

He was quiet a long moment, the kind of quiet that has room in it for three hundred years
to pass back and forth once, unhurried.

"Because the walking was the question," he said at last. "And I wanted to see who'd
answer it." He lifted the second cup and held it out to her across the fire, and there
was something almost formal in the gesture, an old rite performed for the first time in
longer than either of them could reckon. "Drink. I've earned a conversation, and so,
plainly, have you." A dry warmth came into his face, there and gone. "Only — before the
tea goes cold a forty-first time — let me ask you the one thing I climbed all the way up
here to ask, and never had anyone to ask it *of*."

She took the cup. It was warm in her two cold hands, and smelled of woodsmoke and
something bitter-green she had no name for, and she held it and did not drink, because
she understood, without being told, that this was not yet the moment for drinking.

He set his own cup down, folded his hands, and asked it plainly — the way a man asks a
thing he has been holding, unasked, for three hundred years.

"What have you been looking for," he said, "all this way?"

The fire cracked once, low, and settled. Somewhere behind her Pip's tail moved once
against the old man's boot and went still. The dark stood all round the hollow, patient,
unhurried, exactly as it had stood for three centuries, and the old man watched her the
way you watch something you have waited a very long time to see, careful not to hurry
it, careful not to breathe too loud in case it startled off.

She thought of the meadow she had woken in, and the tent full of people holding out
their arms to nobody, and a hundred small rooms full of people who had been waiting, one
way or another, for somebody to come and let them finish. She thought about all of it,
sitting on a cold stone with a hot cup in her hands and an old man's whole three hundred
years balanced on her answer, and found that the true thing, when it came, was smaller
than any of that and truer for being smaller.

"Whatever's still waiting to be found," she said.

He looked at her a long moment across the fire — not surprised, exactly; more the look
of a man hearing a tune he half-remembered, played correctly at last by somebody else's
hands.

"Ah," he said, softly. "Then you and I are the same animal." He picked his own cup back
up, turned it once in his fingers, did not yet drink from it either. "Only — mind you
don't stop as long as I did. Three hundred years is a great deal of *waiting to be
found*, and it is not the same as being found." He said it gently. He said it, she
thought, as a thing he wished very much somebody had said to him, once, a long time ago,
before the wanting had worn itself into a walking he could no longer stop.

Then he drank his tea, and it was, at last, warm, and something in his face — some old
patient tension she had not clocked as tension until it left — let go all at once.

"There," he said. "I've been asked after, and answered, and had my tea hot." He set the
cup down, gently, finally, the way you set down a thing you will not be picking up
again. "That's the whole of what a lantern was ever for. I don't think I need to keep
walking any more."

*  *  *

He reached for the lantern.

She felt it before she understood what she was seeing: as his hand closed round the
handle, the light did not change, not one flicker of it, and yet the air around him
altered, the way the air alters before a weight drops — the lantern growing, in his
grip, impossibly heavy, a small warm flame taking on the whole dark mountain it had
crossed and crossed and crossed, all at once, all in one hand. He lifted it anyway. Both
hands. His arms shook with it, and around him the office came apart: the endless
walking, the always-one-ridge-on, three centuries of being a light and never once a man,
cracking away with a sound like ice giving on a frozen pond, long bright fissures opening
in something she had taken, until that moment, for simply his face.

And underneath it, surfacing the way a held breath finally, finally lets go, came a name.

"...*Ellery*."

He said it carefully, turning the shape of it over on his tongue, the way you'd test
frost with one boot to see whether it would bear your weight.

"Ellery. Yes." He looked up at her, and he was, all at once, only an old man — tired,
and glad, and entirely unperformed. "That was — that *is* mine. I'd gone so far into
the thinking I forgot there was a thinker with a name." A short laugh, rusty, real.
"Well caught, my friend. Not the light. *Me.* You caught me, which is the harder thing,
and the only one that ever counted."

He held the lantern out across the fire — both hands, arms still trembling faintly with
the mountain-weight of it, though it burned in her own eyes as nothing more than an
ordinary lamp, warm and willing to be carried.

"Trump the Ninth," he said. "The Lantern. It's yours now — it was always going to be
whoever kept walking." He waited until her fingers had closed properly round the handle
before he let his own go. "Hold it high when the dark lies to you. It doesn't know how
to lie back." A small, mountain-old smile came and went at the corner of his mouth. "And
when you find the ridge you've been walking toward all this way — I hope there's
somebody sitting at it with the tea already poured."

In her hands, the lantern was only a lantern: warm, and light, and easy to carry, exactly
the size a good lamp ought to be. The weight, she understood, had never once been in the
lamp. It had been in the carrying of it alone.

*  *  *

The change came over the mountain the way a dawn would, if the Dim had ever once been
allowed one.

It began at the edge of things, where the dusk met the black shapes of the farthest
ridges, and did not so much brighten as *unlock*: the flat blue-grey lid of the sky
thinning, and pricking, one small hard point of white at a time, then a dozen, then more
than either of them could count standing still and trying. Stars. Hundreds of them. Then,
as the last of the lid gave way, thousands — laid out across a sky that had shown nothing
at all, not a mark, for three hundred years, arriving all at once the way an answer
arrives when a question has finally, finally been allowed to finish being asked.

She stood with her head tipped back until her neck ached and did not care. There was no
day and night anywhere else in the wide Spread yet — she did not know, standing there, to
miss what she'd have of that later — but here, in this one high cold place, three
centuries of held breath let go all at once into a night of its very own: cold, clear,
and unlike anything the rest of the world had been permitted to keep. She had not known,
until that moment, that a sky could hold so much and still leave room for more.

Ellery stood beside her, no longer needing the lantern to see by, and looked up with her,
and said nothing at all for a good long while, which for him, she was beginning to
understand, was a very great deal.

Down every trail, small warm lights began to move — ordinary lamps and rushlights and the
odd bare candle cupped in an ordinary hand, the lost coming down out of the hills at
last, following an actual sky home the way they had never once, in forty years or four
hundred, been able to follow a single wandering lamp. They passed her without quite
understanding what had changed, only that the mountain had, for the first time in living
memory, let them go.

High on the far eastern face, one small window stayed dark to everything but its own
single lamp, trained down a trail none of the returning lights were using — kept lit for
somebody who was not, tonight, among the ones coming home. She could not fix that. Not
tonight. The thought of it went into her the way the cold had, quiet and unhurried, and
stayed.

*  *  *

The shack, when she came back down to it, was emptying. Cots were being stripped by
their own occupants — travellers she recognised from the cairn field and the valley and
the long grey scree, shrugging into coats, ducking out the low door with a nod or a
murmured thanks, headed for a path down the mountain that had, an hour before, not
existed for any of them. By the time she reached the door, the long table sat all but
bare.

Widow Fenner stood among the mismatched mugs, drying one that no longer had anybody left
to come back and claim it.

"Well," she said, not turning round straight away. "There they go. All of them, near
enough. Down the mountain like water that's finally found its slope." She set the mug
back on its shelf, carefully, in the space it had always occupied. "Three hundred years
I've been the warm window on a cold mountain, love." A breath, level, entirely without
self-pity. "Turns out the mountain's not so cold any more. Turns out it doesn't much
need a window."

She said it without bitterness, and that, the Fool thought, was somehow the worst part
of it — there was no one to be angry at, nothing to set right that hadn't already been
set right by the very thing that had emptied the room.

"I'm sorry," the Fool said.

"Don't be, love." Widow Fenner's chin came up, sharp. "Don't you *dare* be. You gave a
mountain of people their way home. If the price of that's one old woman with too many
clean mugs — that's the cheapest thing I ever heard tell of." She set down the cloth. "Go
on. Don't stand there looking stricken on my account. I'll not have it."

The Fool did not know what else there was to say, so she said nothing, and watched
Widow Fenner fill the kettle from the pail by the door and set it back over the fire —
though the shack was emptying, and the mountain was emptying, and there was, by any
sensible reckoning, nobody left up here to warm.

She caught the Fool watching, and lifted her chin again, half a dare and half something
much softer than a dare.

"Old habit," she said. "Well — in case someone's still finding their way. You never do
know." The kettle ticked once, settling onto the iron. "And a body likes a purpose. Even
a made-up one." A small, unguarded smile, gone almost before it arrived. "*Especially* a
made-up one."

The kettle began, very quietly, to sing. Outside the one small window, the new stars
held steady and cold and countless over a mountain that had finally, after three hundred
years, arrived somewhere.

*  *  *

She left before the singing finished, because there was nowhere in that shack for her to
be of use any longer, and because the road, she had learned by now, did not wait
politely for a person to feel finished with a place before it asked to be walked.

At the treeline she stopped and opened the near pocket of her coat — the one that had
carried, since a carnival ring three hundred years less lonely than this mountain, a
card with a showman's steady patter worn into every line of it — and drew it out, and
looked at it a moment in the light of the new stars, and put it away deeper in the coat,
into keeping rather than use. In its place she slotted the lantern, and felt it settle
into the pocket the way the first card ever had, as though the seam had been cut for it.
A truth she wore now, over her heart, for whatever dark still lay ahead: hold it high
when the dark lies to you. It doesn't know how to lie back.

Pip fell in at her side, unbothered as ever by cosmic weather, nose already down and
working the cold air of the descent.

Away to the east, past the last dark shoulder of the Dim, lay fog-grey courts and
adjourned trials and a queue three centuries deep, knitting. She did not know that yet,
not the particular shape of it — only that the road bent that way, and that something in
her, some small stubborn thing that had followed a light up a whole cold mountain rather
than turn back at the first cairn, was already walking toward it before her feet had
properly decided to.

She looked back once, at the shack's window and its small steady lamp, and then she
whistled up the dog, and turned east, and went on.

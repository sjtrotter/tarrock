# Interlude — The Letters That Cross Anyway

*Carried by messenger-bird across the Divide, except where noted.*

*  *  *

**PERPETUA, EAST BANK, TO OSRIC, WEST BANK**

My dear brother-in-honour (I shall go on calling you that; you have never once told me
to stop, in three hundred years, so I take your silence for consent, which is the only
kind of consent a west-bank man ever gives anybody),

Word has come up from the water-stair and I am writing before I have properly finished
being astonished by it. A stranger came. A stranger with a small white dog, if you can
credit it, and stood the pair of them on the broken span, and did the one thing three
hundred years and both our whole grieving towns could not do between us. Said a bank
out loud. Ours, Osric. Our chapel. The bell that has waited so long it forgot it was
allowed to make a sound is to ring, and ring properly, for our girl.

I do not know how to tell you this and be glad in front of you at the same time, so I
am simply going to be glad in the letter and trust you to forgive the letter for it. You
know I have never once forgiven your hall its swept, empty floor without also loving it
a little for staying swept. Write back and tell me you are not too wretched. Then, when
you have finished being noble about it, tell me you are a *little* wretched, because I
shall not believe you otherwise, and I have known you far too long to be lied to gently.

Your Perpetua

*  *  *

**OSRIC, WEST BANK, TO PERPETUA, EAST BANK**

Dear Perpetua,

I read your letter three times before I let myself feel anything about it at all, which
you will recognise as exactly the sort of thing a west-bank man does, so I shan't
apologise for it.

Here is the truth, offered once, so we needn't revisit it: it aches. This hall has been
swept every morning of my whole remembered life toward a wedding that has just, very
definitely, decided to happen somewhere else. I stood in the doorway this afternoon and
looked at the floor and thought, well. That's that, then. Three generations of polish for
nothing.

And here is the rest of the truth, offered in the same breath, because it is also true:
I am glad. He loves her. I have watched that boy love her, uselessly, hopelessly, for as
long as I have been old enough to notice such things, and if the price of his happiness
is that my floor stays a floor and never a dance, I find I can meet the price. Grief and
gladness, it turns out, fit in the same chest without much crowding. I don't know why
nobody warned me.

Tell your girl her uncle-in-honour sends his blessing, and — since you will only ask if
I don't say it first — no, I have not touched a drop, I am simply an old fool with
something in my eye. We will be sending a family across directly the bridge takes
weight; my second cousin's lot, packing already, gone to live where the town is going.
I shall miss having them under this roof more than I expected to.

And before you say it: yes. Your chapel will do a *finer* wedding breakfast than we ever
managed here, because your coast has the fish for it and we have only ever had stone and
stubbornness. I concede the point in advance, so you needn't spend a whole letter making
it.

Osric

*  *  *

**PERPETUA TO OSRIC**

You concede nothing, you old fraud, you have never once conceded anything in your life,
you have simply run out of time to argue before the wedding arrives, which is not the
same thing and you know it. Your hall's game pie is the one dish on either bank I have
genuinely missed the smell of these three hundred years, and I shall say so to no one but
you, so mind you don't repeat it to the whole west terrace, or I shall have to deny it
under oath.

The bridge is finished, Osric. Finished in a single night, they say, the last plank
seating itself like a lock turning while decent people slept. I mean to walk it. I have
never once in my whole long life stood on the same ground as you, did you realise that?
Three hundred years of your handwriting and I could not tell you the colour of your eyes.
I find, now that I could simply walk across and find out, that my nerve has rather gone
out of me. Isn't that a foolish thing, at our age.

Come to the wedding. Come and stand where I can see you do it.

P.

*  *  *

**OSRIC TO PERPETUA — carried by hand, folded small, given to a west-bank family
crossing to their new home, who were asked most particularly not to read it**

I have stood at my own end of the bridge twice today and turned back twice, which is a
poor showing for a man who spent thirty years insisting the crossing was only ever a
matter of a choice being made. Turns out knowing a thing and doing it are not, after
all, the same trade.

I will come. I will stand at the back where the light is bad, in case my face does
something my letters never had to account for. Look for the tallest fool in a coat too
warm for the weather. That will be your correspondent of three hundred years, finally, in
person, and appallingly nervous about it.

O.

*  *  *

**PERPETUA TO OSRIC — written the evening of the wedding, sent by bird out of pure old
habit though the messenger is by now entirely redundant and knows it, and sulks
accordingly**

Osric. Osric, the bell *rang*. I have heard that bell only ever silent, my whole life,
and tonight it rang and rang and did not know how to stop, and neither, I confess, did
I. Our girl walked out under it with the sea behind her doing that show-off thing it
does at a wedding, and I looked across the chapel for the tallest fool in the wrong coat
and there you were, at the back, exactly as promised, and you did not look wretched at
all. You looked like a man who had crossed a bridge.

I have never embraced you. I realised it halfway down the aisle and nearly missed my
own cue for thinking about it. We shall have to remedy that, I think, before either of
us grows too proud to manage it.

Your Perpetua, still, and now also — at last — your neighbour.

*  *  *

**OSRIC TO PERPETUA — the last letter for some while, or so he claims**

My dear Perpetua,

I write this knowing full well I could simply walk it across in the morning, that the
bird sitting on my sill is looking at me with an expression I can only call reproachful,
as though she too has worked out that her trade is finished, and that we are both, bird
and man, rather too old and too fond of our habits to let a perfectly good bridge talk us
out of them.

So: no. I shall not be walking it across in the morning. I shall be writing to you, as I
have written to you for three hundred years, and having it flown, because the flying was
never really the point of the letters and I find I am not ready to discover what was left
over once the flying stops. We embraced at the wedding, briefly, both of us laughing to
cover it, and I have thought of very little else since, which at my age is either a
tender thing or an alarming one, and I have decided not to enquire too closely which.

Keep writing to me, Perpetua. By bird. Slowly, the old way, the way that let two old fools
love each other's towns without either town noticing until it was too late to mind. We
have a bridge now. I find I would rather keep the habit than the shortcut.

Your Osric, across the gap, exactly where he has always been glad to be found.

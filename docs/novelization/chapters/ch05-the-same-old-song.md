# Chapter Five — The Same Old Song

She heard the Chantry a full day before she saw it.

It came across the fields the way smoke does, thinning and thickening on nothing, four
beats over and over — a low note, a higher one, a held one, a rest — arriving out of the
haze at the edge of hearing long before there was a tower to hang it on. By the second day
of walking it had stopped sounding like distance and started sounding like weather, a
thing the whole sky seemed to be doing rather than a bell.

"That's not stopped," she said, "since yesterday."

*It's not stopped since a good deal longer than yesterday, little Excuse.* The Querent, in
her ear, sounded almost apologetic about it. *You'll want to brace yourself for the
particular flavour of company that keeps.*

She had left the Divide three days back with a wedding ring of laughter still going in her
ears — the east bank's whole green turned out for it, fiddles and a bonfire and Wystan
crossing the new bridge in his good coat, hair combed flat and immediately ruined by the
wind, because there was wind now, real wind, all along that canyon, now that the Lovers
had let each other go and chosen. A new card rode folded in the Bindle that she hadn't
found anywhere to put — Union, she'd taken to calling it in her head — loose, unslotted,
alongside Manifest at her heart and Bloom worn close as a promise, because her small sewn
spread had only ever had the two pockets filled. The White Rose at her belt had gone from
three petals to three petals and stayed there the whole three days, opening by the barest,
grudging fraction with every camp she made on unbound ground — slow, the Querent had
warned her, terribly slow out here where nothing but a Waystation gave it back all at
once — but opening, which was new, and which she checked on more often than she'd admit to
Pip.

The road climbed a low rise, and there below her, sunk into a green fold of hills like a
coin dropped in grass, lay the Chantry.

It rose in tiers the way a choir does, low roofs climbing to a cloister, the cloister
climbing to a great pierced drum of stone that stood over the whole town like a held
breath made architecture — every window in it a throat, every throat singing the same
four beats. Golden light stood behind stained glass the colour of held jewels, ruby and
sea-glass and a deep unmoving amber, and did not flicker, because nothing in the Chantry
had been permitted to flicker in three hundred years.

She came down through the lychgate with Pip trotting a half-step ahead, nose up, and felt
the particular held stillness of a bound place close over her like water: no wind here,
though the fields behind had had plenty; no dust drifting, though the light lay thick
enough with it to have been powder; a banner over the gate hung in a fold that had chosen
its shape once, three centuries ago, and had never once reconsidered it. Grass parted
around her boots and settled behind her, the only motion for a hundred yards in any
direction, and Pip's passage cut a second, smaller wake beside her own.

The hymn was everywhere and came from nowhere. It rose off the flagstones under her boots
and the gravestones worn round as river stones and the gutters and the very stone of the
lychgate arch, so that walking through it felt less like entering a town where people sang
than walking into a town that had, itself, over three hundred years, quietly become the
singing — the hymn rising out of the Chantry rather than being sung by it.

A young woman broke from a procession that never quite arrived anywhere — choir-shift,
sheet music rolled tight in one fist though she plainly had not needed to read it in her
whole life — and crossed to her at something close to a run.

"A new face!" She said it the way other people say *fire*. "Do you hear it the way I do,
I wonder — the hymn, I mean. You'll not have heard it before. Nobody's not-heard-it-before
in three hundred years but you."

"It's very loud," the Fool said, which was true, and also the only thing she could think
to say.

"Isn't it just." The young woman laughed, delighted rather than embarrassed. "I'm Linnet.
Chorister, third row, second voice — that's me, that's the whole of me." She fell into
step without being asked, the way you fall into step with something you've been hoping to
fall into step with. "I've sung this hymn every day I've been alive, which is every day
there's ever been, and I love it. Truly I do."

"But?" said the Fool.

Linnet's stride hitched, very slightly.

"But nothing. There's no *but*. It's perfect. It's the same perfect thing it was
yesterday, and the thousand yesterdays before." She looked down at her own rolled music as
if it might, this once, have a different answer folded into it than the one she'd sung
ten thousand times. "...I should like to hear one new note before I die. Just the one. I
don't even know what it would sound like." She flushed, all at once, badly. "Isn't that a
wicked thing to want."

"It isn't wicked to want it," the Fool said.

Linnet stopped walking entirely.

"...No? You say that so plainly." She turned the sheet music over in her hands as if it
had grown heavier. "Nobody's ever said it plainly. Everyone here would tell me to pray it
down. You just — let me keep it."

She asked her other questions on the walk in — who led the hymn (*the Hierophant, up in
the colosseum, hands on the great organ; you can't reach him, nobody reaches the middle*);
why the song couldn't change (*because it's* right, *and a right thing doesn't change, or
so we're taught, every day, in the hymn that says so*) — and by the time they reached the
nave doors Linnet had tucked her music back under her arm and was walking a half-step
ahead, glancing back to be sure the Fool followed: the first time in three centuries she
had led anyone anywhere.

*Listen to it properly, little Excuse,* said the Querent, low, as they went in. *The whole
town keyed to one note like a nail keyed to one board. You'll not fight your way to the
middle of that. You'll have to learn the tune, and then ruin it. Cheerful work.* A dry
pause. *My favourite sort.*

*  *  *

Inside, the service was always in progress and never in conclusion.

Rows of choristers held a chord that resolved and re-began without a seam between the two,
a congregation sat shoulder to shoulder in the long patience of people who had stopped
waiting for an ending so long ago they'd forgotten one had ever been promised. It should
have felt like a prison. It felt, instead, like being tucked in — which was so very much
worse.

Linnet drew her to a place at the aisle and kept the measure with two fingers tapping soft
against her own palm.

"There — feel it? One, two, three, four, and around." She was not performing this; she was
teaching it, and something in her had gone quietly serious to do so. "What matters for you
isn't the beat, though. It's the *space* between — the off-beat, the little gap where
nobody's singing yet. That gap's the only unguarded moment in the whole Chantry."

Pip sat, and thumped his tail against the flagstones, and — she noticed it a full measure
before she believed it — thumped it *on the gap*, not the note, steady as a second
metronome nobody had asked for.

"Good," Linnet breathed, when the Fool found it too, a held breath let loose sideways into
a rest nobody else in the whole nave had ever thought to use. "*Good.* You've the ear for
it. Most here couldn't find the off-beat with both hands and a lantern — they've been on
the beat so long they think the beat's the only place there is."

An elder chorister nearby — robe gone to a shine at both elbows, eyes shut, face lifted
into the chord like a man warming his hands at a fire — spoke without opening them, in the
easy certainty of long doctrine.

"Do not let the child teach you to hear the gaps, pilgrim." His voice was kind, and that
was the worst part of it. "The gaps are where doubt gets in. The hour holds because we
sing the sound and not the silence. Three hundred years it has held. It will hold three
hundred more."

He believed it entirely. It was the most comforting thing anyone had said to her all week,
and it was a cage with the door painted to look like a window.

"Is he happy?" the Fool asked Linnet, low.

"He's *certain*," Linnet said. "He'd tell you that's the same thing. Some mornings I even
believe him."

She turned back to her music, and the Fool watched her turn it over once more, and thought
of what it had cost her to say *isn't that a wicked thing to want* out loud to a stranger,
and made up her mind about something small and, as it would turn out, not small at all.

"Sing it," she said. "Just the one. I'll listen."

Linnet went very still.

"Now?"

"Now."

For a moment Linnet's mouth opened and shut on nothing, the way a person's does at the top
of a jump they've already committed to. Then she sang — one note, off the hymn entirely,
flat and wobbling and hers, cutting straight across the chord that had not once in three
hundred years admitted a stranger into it.

Nothing fell. No window broke. Down the aisle, an old woman's head turned a fraction and
turned back, and that was the whole of the catastrophe.

Linnet clapped both hands over her own mouth.

"Oh," she said, from behind them. "*Oh.* The sky's still up. The bells didn't stop. I did
a wrong thing and the world just — let me." She lowered her hands slowly, staring at them
as if they belonged to someone braver. "I think I've been more frightened of that one note
than of dying."

Three rows off, the elder chorister's certainty faltered for the length of a breath.

"...The third measure wandered," he murmured, to no one, eyes still shut, deeply troubled.
"It never wanders. I must have miscounted. I have never once miscounted."

*  *  *

Above the nave, the town's music was fed from copper throats — great choir-pipes mounted
along the ridgelines and the bell-towers, each one piping a single held voice up into the
endless hymn. Three of them, the Querent told her, could be reached and turned; turn a pipe
and its voice bent out of true, thinning the harmony that kept the middle of the colosseum
untouchable.

Each was guarded. Not by a soldier exactly — by something built along the same lines as
one, blank-faced, tabard-plain, but wearing for a head a cracked handbell that swung and
tolled with every turn of its neck, ringing the beat back into true if it ever caught her
moving on it.

*Turn the pipe on the gap, not the note,* said the Querent, at the first one, *same as the
child told you. Get greedy and clang about on the beat, and that bell-headed fellow rings
the whole town down on you. Patience, little Excuse. The song's teaching you its own weak
spot; rude not to listen.*

She learned the trick of it on the roof-ridge above the almonry: watch the bell-Blank's
slow patrol, count the four beats in her own chest the way Linnet had taught her, and on
the gap — only ever on the gap — reach with the third hand Manifest had given her, the one
that went out fifteen feet past her fingers and needed no crossing of open tile to get
there, and wrench the pipe's copper throat a half-turn off true. The held note soured. Far
below, faint but unmistakable, the perfect chord wobbled, and a single distant voice
faltered and found its place again a half-beat late.

The second pipe she took at a dead crouch along a gutter with Pip sent ahead to worry at a
bell-Blank's flank on the beat, drawing its slow turn away just long enough; the third from
inside the belfry itself, breath held, boots soundless on a floor she'd swept clear of
loose slate with her sleeve first, because a slipped foot on the beat would have brought
the whole tower down on her in cracked-bell alarm.

*Three down,* said the Querent, as the last pipe's note bent and soured. *Hear that? The
whole town's a hair off true now and hasn't the first idea why. That wobble is every door
you're about to walk through. Go and prove it in the square before you climb to the
middle — no sense learning to swim in the deep end.*

*  *  *

In the square below the colosseum's shadow, something had come loose from the hymn
entirely.

A bell-Blank stood at the centre of it, staggering in slow circles, its cracked handbell
head tolling a half-beat behind everything else — ringing an alarm nobody answered,
because the note it rang was simply *wrong*, and the Chantry had no word ready-made for
wrong. Passers-by drifted around it, uneasy, unable to name what troubled them, the way
you cannot name a held breath until somebody lets it go.

Linnet was already there, drawn like everyone else, but unlike everyone else she knew
exactly what she was looking at.

"That's — that shouldn't be possible." She had gone very pale and very bright at once, the
two things sitting oddly together on one face. "A bell out of true, ringing on and on and
nothing struck down for it." She caught the Fool's sleeve. "Go on. *Please.* Silence it. I
have to see it silenced and the sky stay up. I have to see it with my own eyes or I'll
never believe the middle can break."

She fought it in the square's centre, and it was, in its small way, the most naked fight
she had yet had — no rooftop, no shadows, every soul in the square watching a thing they
did not have words for get quietly undone, on the offbeat, over and over, until the
staggering bell-headed figure simply stopped moving. It did not shatter. It sat down where
it stood, like a marionette with its strings gone slack all at once, and its cracked
handbell rolled off across the flagstones, ringing nothing at all.

The square held its breath. The sky stayed exactly where it had always been. The other
bells tolled on overhead, entirely untroubled, over a crowd that had just watched a piece
of its eternal music switch off — and survive it.

"Nothing ended," Linnet whispered, barely a whisper at all, radiant with it. "Something
*stopped*, and nothing ended. Those aren't the same. Nobody ever told me those weren't the
same."

"Now you tell everyone else," the Fool said.

"...I might." Linnet's eyes had not left the fallen bell. "When the song's free, I might
tell the whole square. Go up, then. Go and stop the biggest bell of the lot."

*  *  *

The colosseum's doors stood open, and had always stood open, because no one who
understood the hymn had ever once thought to walk through them meaning to end it.

Inside was a great round of tiered stone with no audience in it at all, ringing a floor
inlaid with staves like a page of music laid flat, and at the centre of it — raised on a
console grown into the very walls, mitre and worn cope and both hands laid on the
manuals — sat the Hierophant, conducting the eternal hymn without ever once looking up,
because in three hundred years nothing had arrived worth looking up for.

She walked the whole ring. Every strike she tried slid off the held harmony like rain off
slate; nothing landed; nothing so much as marked him.

Then he lifted his head — the way a shepherd counts a flock and finds the number wrong by
one.

"A newcomer." His voice filled the drum without effort, the way water fills a bowl. "On
the staves themselves. You have walked into the middle of the Rite, child, which cannot be
done, and so you have not done it. Stand in the seats. Be sung to. That is what the middle
is *for*."

"The song's already wobbling," she said. "Did you hear?"

"The song does not wobble." It came a fraction too quickly. "The song is the one thing
that has never wobbled. You are new, and the new mishear — that is not a sin, it is merely
a lack, and the hour is patient with lacks. Come. Let it correct you."

He said it with the practiced patience of a man who had corrected ten thousand novices and
feared not one of them — over the smallest flicker of something that was not patience at
all, gone before it could properly be named.

Then the wobble she'd bought him, pipe by pipe, roof by roof, opened its first true gap in
his guard, and she stepped into it.

The fight kept the hymn's own time. His attacks came in strict meter, organ-stabs that
struck the floor's inlaid staves like struck strings, a sweeping chord that crossed the
whole drum in a wave she had to duck under on the beat and rise from on the gap; twice
more bell-headed figures rose out of the tiered stone at his call and had to be silenced —
off-beat, always off-beat, exactly as the square had rehearsed her — before their ringing
closed his windows again.

"Back to the seats," he told her, mid-chord, patter worn smooth as a psalm. "It is not a
punishment, child, it is a *place* for you — everyone has a place, that is the whole
comfort of the hour."

"You strike in the gaps," he said, later, breath shorter. "Who taught you the gaps? There
are no gaps. There is only the measure and the measure and the measure."

She struck him on the third beat's rest, exactly as the boarded, syrupy-slow half-second
Manifest's power lent her told her to — the world going obedient and thick the way it had
above a haywain and beneath a striped tent, only now the syrup had a metre to it, four
counts and a rest, and she rode the rest in like a held breath finally let out — and his
playing grew harder, faster, the patience visibly thinning under it like ice going to
water.

"Stop," he said. "*Stop.* You are unpicking three hundred years with a — with a
*tune-deaf child's* trick, you cannot, the hour is not *yours* to—"

He heard himself. The doctrine cracked for the length of one bar, and the fear beneath it
stood plain in his voice for the first time in three centuries.

"...What if the child is right," he said, very quietly, hands faltering on the manuals for
the first time she had seen. "What if there was always a gap. What if I have been singing
over the gap so long because I could not bear to hear what was in it."

She reached the master pipe at the colosseum's heart on that falter — the organ's own
great voice, the one all the others answered to, fourth and last and never once meant to
be touched by anyone but him — and turned it, with both hands and every ounce of Manifest's
borrowed reach, on the final off-beat of the whole broken measure.

The great voice bent. Cracked. Guttered.

And the whole hymn, town-wide, three centuries deep, broke off mid-note.

Silence.

Not a rest. Not a held breath waiting on its resolution. Genuine, total silence — the
first the Chantry had known since the Stall — and somewhere far below, a congregation that
had not heard silence in three hundred years made a sound like an entire town flinching.

*  *  *

The Hierophant's hands lifted off the manuals as though the keys beneath them had gone
suddenly, unbearably cold.

He looked at the office around him — mitre, cope, the whole architecture of certainty —
and it split, top to bottom, with a sound exactly like an organ pipe rupturing: one long
tearing overtone, and then nothing at all.

"There is a gap," he said, wonderingly. "There was always a gap. And in the gap there is—"

He drew breath to name it, and the breath brought something up with it that was not
doctrine's to give: a name surfacing mid-inhale, the way a drowning man's head breaks
water.

"...*Bede.*"

He said it as if he'd caught it before it could slip away again. Tried it a second time,
gentler, to be sure it would hold.

"Bede. That's — that was mine. Before the hour. Before I was a *when* instead of a *who*.
I had a name and I set it down to carry the office, and I never — nobody told me I could
pick it back up."

He stepped down off the console a smaller man than had sat at it, not beaten so much as
*unburdened*, the mitre gone crooked and no longer minded, and looked at her without a
scrap of doctrine left in his face.

"First new note in three hundred years," he said, "and it turns out to be a *name.* Mine.
You gave an old shepherd back the one thing his own hour took off him. That's — that is a
better sermon than I ever preached."

He lifted a card from the folds of his cope. No flourish, no rite — just a man handing over
what was, at long last, simply his to give.

"Trump V. Rite, if anyone writes it down kindly. Blessings, and a circle nothing unwelcome
may cross, and one litany for when your last petal's spent." He set it into her hand and
folded her fingers closed over it. "Take it. A rite's only ever been a kindness people
forgot to stop performing. Perform it better than I did."

She opened her coat and slid it into the pocket the Chantry's own hour had somehow always
been waiting to fill, and felt the small, exact click of a truth she would now be able to
carry on purpose.

*  *  *

She came down out of the colosseum into a town that had never once needed to know what to
do with quiet, and was finding out — and into a town that had started, ever so slightly,
to *move*. A votive candle sent up a thin curl of smoke that leaned, actually leaned,
sideways, the first uncommitted thing it had done in three hundred years. Wax frozen
halfway down its own candle let go and ran. A banner folded stiff around one corner since
before her grandmother's grandmother gave a single soft snap and hung differently.

For a long moment the bells hung still. Then, tentatively, badly, gloriously, a
bell-ringer's mate somewhere up in the nearest tower tried a peal that was not the old
one: three notes, a fumble, a laugh, and three notes again. The town flinched at it. Then,
one by one, it leaned toward it.

Overhead, the light that had lain nailed to one unmoving instant of afternoon for three
hundred years let go by the smallest possible degree — not the sun; the sun over the wide
Spread would keep its own long counsel for a good while yet, and had no part in this — only
the local, narrow mercy of shadows across the flagstones finally allowed to lengthen a
finger's width, because the one held hour that had owned them was, at last, over.

Linnet stood in the square where the bell-Blank had fallen, sheet music unrolled across
her knee and, for the first time in her life, being written *on* rather than read from.

"Listen to us," she said, laughing, wet-eyed. "We're *terrible*. Nobody's ever been
terrible here before — you have to be allowed to change to be allowed to be terrible, and
we were never allowed either. It's the best sound I've ever heard."

She sang it then, out loud, unafraid — the one wobbling note she'd stolen in the nave grown
up into a whole ragged phrase of her own, nothing like the hymn at all.

"I've had that one saved up since you let me keep it," she said, breathless. "Turns out it
had friends."

"It's a beautiful note," the Fool said.

"It's a *wrong* note, is what it is." Linnet was laughing properly now, both hands over
her own heart as if to keep it in. "And it's *mine*. And those turn out to be the same
wonderful thing."

*  *  *

She found Brother Tolliver alone in the emptied nave, hands lifted to warm at a fire that
had gone out, the fumbling new peal reaching him faintly through the stone and doing
nothing at all to his face.

"I knew the hour," he said, without turning. "Do you understand me — I *knew* it. When to
rise, when to kneel, when to hope and when to let hoping be. Sixty years, and then three
hundred more, and every one of them I knew exactly where I was in the song."

He listened to the peal a while. It was joyful. It was plainly, entirely, not his.

"Listen to that," he said. "Flat as a dropped plate, and off the beat besides. Sixty years
I'd have had a novice scrubbing the crypt for that." A small, helpless, wet laugh got out
of him before he could stop it. "And now it's the sound of everyone being happy, and me
being wrong to mind. There's a joke in there. I was never any good at the jokes."

He wiped his face with the back of a robed wrist, and the certainty did not come back.

"And now there's a new song, and everyone's so *pleased*," he said, "and I don't know when
to kneel any more. What do I pray *by*, if the hour won't tell me? How does a man keep
faith with a clock that's allowed to be wrong?"

She had no answer that would have fit in twelve words or fewer, and did not try to find
one. He did not curse her for it. He was not owed a resolution, and she had none to give
him; he only lifted his hands again to the cold air where the chord used to be, and held
them there, and let her go without another word.

*Not every door opens onto somebody glad you knocked,* said the Querent, quiet in her ear
as she crossed the empty square. *You'll get used to the shape of that. I never quite did,
and I've had three hundred years longer than you to practise.*

*  *  *

She left by the south-western road with a new card warm against her ribs, three petals
still closed at her belt but, she'd have sworn it, a fraction less sulky about staying
that way, and the last of the wrong, wonderful new peal chasing her out past the lychgate
in fits and starts.

Along the way she passed a wedding party assembling on a green that had plainly waited a
very long time to be allowed to — banns finally read to an hour of their own choosing,
somebody's aunt already crying happily into a handkerchief that had clearly seen this duty
before — and, further on, another, and further still a third, because word travels faster
than a bride's nerve, and the whole settled country between here and the Maw seemed to
have decided, all in the same handful of days, that today was finally, actually, allowed
to be the day.

*One shepherd, one name, back where it belongs,* the Querent said, comfortably, somewhere
around the fifth mile. *On to the next stuck card. There's always a next card.* A pause,
warmer than the words strictly needed. *There's always one more.*

Far off across the whole width of the Spread, small and silver and perfectly still, the
Axis sat at the centre of everything, and the tiny turning figure at its heart went on
turning, unhurried, as if it had all the time there ever was.

She did not look at it for very long. Ahead, the land was already climbing toward pale
limestone and a country of teeth-white crags, and somewhere in it, so she'd heard, there
was a woman who had held a lion's mouth shut with her bare hands for three hundred years
and had not once, in all that time, let it bite.

She whistled up the dog, and walked on south-west, into the Maw.

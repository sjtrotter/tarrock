# Chapter Thirteen — Between the Pillars

Eleven cards rode in her coat by then, and she had stopped feeling the weight of them,
which the Querent said was either a good sign or a very bad one, and declined, when
pressed, to say which.

The Longroad took her south-west, and the Longroad these days remembered how to have
weather: wind at her shoulder in long unhurried pushes, grass leaning all one way and then
the other, birds overhead about their own business. Its procession still marched the ring,
banners road-worn now rather than bleached. She rested at a Waystation where four roads
met, and the White Rose at her belt opened all six of its petals in one breath, the small
private sound it always made. When she thought, deliberately, of the western spur, the
road gave itself up the way roads had begun to lately, when she asked properly — four days
folded down into a good stride.

*Here we are, then*, said the Querent, and the voice had gone quiet in a way it kept for
nothing else on the whole Spread — the quiet of walking past a room where somebody is
sleeping. *Mind your feet and mind your voice, little Excuse. This is the Veil — the whole
world's secrets, shelved and sealed and sat on for three hundred years. They don't so much
keep quiet here as make a religion of it.*

The wind gave out first, leaning and thinning and finally simply stopping, the way a hand
stops waving once the person it waved at has turned the corner. Then the birds. Then,
within a dozen strides, the road behind her, the sky behind her, the whole ordinary
daylight she'd been walking under since the Longroad — all of it gone into a white with no
grain to it at all.

Mist, not fog: something closer kin to the wax on a letter, laid down once and never
disturbed. It came up to meet her the way water comes up round a step, curling where she
and Pip walked and settling again the instant they'd passed, exactly the width of two
bodies and no more. Nothing in it drifted. Far ahead, two shapes resolved out of the
white — pillars, enormous, their tops lost the way a spire is lost in low cloud, except
that above *this* was no cloud, only a moon hung fixed at one angle, as if someone had
stopped the sky mid-breath and walked away from the job.

*I'll not chatter, for once*, said the Querent. *Wouldn't want to be the loudest thing in
a library. Go on. Someone's already noticed you — someone always has, in there.*

Pip's trot dropped, unbidden, to something that was, for a dog built entirely along the
lines of enthusiasm, an almost unbearable effort at a whisper.

*  *  *

The cloister was strung between the pillars like washing hung to dry — sealed doors,
shuttered windows, a low gatehouse arch with a crescent worn nearly smooth in its
keystone. No guard stood in it. But something shifted in the doorway's dark before she'd
got within twenty feet, and a small figure in a grey habit stopped there, plainly
undecided whether to be delighted or terrified, and settled, in the end, on both.

"*Shh* — oh, you're real, you're actually — no. No, quiet, quiet, or they'll all look, and
then I'll have to *explain* you, and there's no word for you, I checked."

She crouched, gave Pip one silent, delighted pat — accepted with the gravity of a knight
accepting a medal — and straightened into the barest thread of a voice, the voice of
someone who had whispered her whole short life and was still, somehow, afraid of being
caught at it.

"Right. The rule. There's only the one, so I can manage it even in here. Nothing is asked
twice, and nothing is told at all. Don't expect an answer out of anyone but me, because
I'm the only one daft enough to give one." A glance back at the dark, as if it might have
heard that. "You want the Keeper. Everyone who comes wants the Keeper, all two of you in
three centuries. But she won't see just anyone. You'll have to be *quiet* enough to
deserve asking, first. Come on. And keep the dog to a whisper, if he's got one."

Months back she'd have asked everything at once, simply because the questions were there.
She had learned since the Prestige that a person who hands you a secret has usually paid
for the having of it. So she spent the small safe questions instead. "Why does no one
speak?"

"Vow. Three hundred years deep." Sister Amity gave the name over shyly, as if it too might
be more than she was strictly permitted to spend. "The Keeper says a secret spoken stops
being a secret, and then what's a library *for*. I speak because nobody ever got round to
swearing *me* in."

"Are you allowed to like visitors?" she asked — the kindest question left to her.

A whole conflicted breath went by. "No. Yes. I've never had to find out." A small
astonished laugh escaped before she could catch it. "You're the first thing to *happen* to
me, and I took vows against things happening. Ask me later whether I'm glad."

The question about the Keeper's reading sat right there, close enough to reach for, and
Amity's eyes had that flicker of someone half wanting to be asked. She let it go by, and
found that passing over a question could be its own kindness — offered to a stranger who
had already given away more than her order allowed.

Amity turned, visibly relieved not to have been pressed, and beckoned her through the
arch, walking on the balls of her feet out of a habit three centuries deep.

*  *  *

Beyond the arch, a hall of writing-desks ran off into the mist, and at every desk sat a
nun bent over a page, quill in hand, entirely still. Not resting. She understood that
within three steps, the way you understand cold water the moment your boot goes in. At the
nearest desk the quill had dried fast to the parchment mid-word, grey dust settled soft
along the writer's knuckles, the sentence trailing off where, three hundred years ago, the
world had simply stopped listening to what she meant to say next. Desk after desk, the
same — a hall of half-finished thoughts, held open at the exact place they broke off.

A few awake among the sleepers moved between the rows soft as tide-pool crabs, re-inking a
dried nib, straightening a slumped shoulder, keeping vigil over work that would never,
whatever they did, advance a single word.

"Careful," Amity breathed, barely more than shaped air. "Wake a sleeper — startle her out
of her sentence — and every Sister in the hall will turn and look, and you'll not get
within a mile of the Keeper. Softly. Like you mean it."

She put two fingers to Pip's collar, and he dropped, without being told twice, into a low,
exaggerated tiptoe, tail rigid with the effort — the most serious she had ever seen him
about anything, food included, each paw placed as though the floor itself might report
him. It was one of the funniest and gentlest things she'd seen him do, and she bit the
inside of her cheek not to laugh in a room built on the wrongness of laughing.

They threaded the gaps between desks — a held breath, a shaft of frozen moonlight where
the dust hung so still it might have been painted — and not one head came up, not one
sentence finished. At the far end a small door stood ajar on a spiral stair into the dark.

*Three hundred years, every one of them mid-thought*, said the Querent, very low. *Whatever
she was writing, she never got to find out how it ended. None of them did. That's the
Veil's particular cruelty — not that it's quiet, but that it's quiet right in the middle of
a sentence.*

*  *  *

The stair climbed to an open gallery strung between the pillars — a dozen cloister bells
along a rail, each the size of a cauldron, each utterly silent. Mist poured through like a
slow tide with nowhere to be. Below: the whole white nothing. Above: the moon, exactly
where it had been all night, all century.

Amity waited at the rail with her hands clasped so tight the knuckles had gone pale. "This
is as far as *asking* takes you," she whispered. "To be granted, you ring the summoning
bell. Only — look at them. Three hundred years in the damp. They've all gone."

She was right. A hand near the nearest bell found the crack at once, a hairline seam with
frozen moonlight caught inside it like something pressed between the pages of a book — and
the next the same, eleven in all, each wearing its own thin scar of light where the bronze
had failed. The twelfth hung dark all the way through, kept, all this while, apparently for
no reason but this one. Pip sat himself directly beneath it and would not be moved.

She took the striker in both hands and swung it, and the bell rang.

One note. Enormous, whole, unbroken — the first true bell-note the Veil had heard in three
hundred years — and it rolled out over the gallery and down into the mist, unhurried about
ever stopping. Amity clapped both hands over her ears and grinned in a way that plainly
hurt her.

"*Oh* — oh, that's — I've never — that's what a bell is *supposed* to — quick, quick,
before I cry about a *bell*, she'll have heard it, she'll be—"

The note faded, unwillingly. Far down the gallery, where the white hung thickest,
something that had been part of the stone stopped being part of the stone — a shape,
robed and still, that she'd have sworn had not been standing there a moment before.

*  *  *

The inner cloister lay open to the frozen sky, floored in a great pale dial of stone,
walled by shelves sealed under a skin of grey wax poured three centuries ago and never
broken since. Ten thousand volumes no hand had opened since the Stall. Between the
pillars, at the centre of the dial, stood the Keeper.

She did not move to greet them. Her eyes found the Fool the instant they crossed the
threshold and simply stayed there, unhurried, thorough, faintly rude — the precise
sensation of being *shelved* while still standing on her own two feet.

"A rose. A dog. A staff, and no name worth the shelf-space." The voice was low, even,
built for rooms where nothing was said twice. "I have read pilgrims, kings, liars, and the
occasional god, and I could tell you the ending of every one of them from the door." She
tilted her head a fraction, which was, the Fool understood, the most she had moved in some
while. "You, I have read to the last page, and the last page is *blank*. I know precisely
what you are and precisely nothing about what you will do. That is intolerable." A pause,
faint as the mist settling. "That is, possibly, the most interesting thing to walk in here
since the sky stopped."

"There is no fight here for you to win. There is only an examination, and it cannot be
passed with words, because words are the one currency this house does not accept. Three
things. Do them, and we shall see what the blank page does when it is *trusted*."

"Why build a test to be failed?" she asked — the one question that had been sitting in her
chest since the gatehouse, and the only one, of the few she was allowed, that seemed worth
the spending.

Something in her face became, for a moment, readable. "Because in three centuries no one
has passed it, and a thing that cannot fail cannot be trusted either. You noticed it is
built to be failed. That is your first honest mark. Do not spend it."

*  *  *

A tighter, older stair climbed the near pillar to a broken belfry where the mist thinned
and the moon hung nearer than anywhere else. An owl sat unmoving over a nest wedged into
the rafters — a magpie's midden of bright useless things: a silver thimble, a green-gone
coin, and, torn and shredded half into the bedding, a single leaf of a catalogue-page — the
finding-key, she'd have staked her last coin on it, to some whole sealed wing, put here to
better use than anyone downstairs had ever found for it.

Pip went in with his particular scholarly delicacy, past the thimble and the coin as
beneath his notice, and drew the torn leaf free without disturbing a feather, head high,
entirely satisfied with his own judgement.

The leaf was plainly incomplete, a third of it gone to nesting, its numbers running off a
ragged edge into nothing.

She carried it down and set it, unasked, at the Keeper's feet — not handed over, only
returned. "The owl took part of it," she said. "This is the rest."

The Keeper took it, eyes not leaving the Fool. "Torn," she said, "and you say so. A small
thing to be honest about." A pause that felt like being weighed on a very fine scale.
"Small things are where it starts. Both ways." She set it on the dial — not shelved, only
left there, catalogued at last by the simple fact of being *returned* — and the Fool
understood she had just been asked the whole examination in miniature, and answered
without knowing it.

*  *  *

The Keeper turned her face, barely, toward the dial underfoot. Every flagstone lay drowned
in moonlight, edge to edge — except at the foot of the second pillar, where one stone sat
locked forever in the pillar's own shadow, the single square foot in this whole watching
place the moon had never once touched and never would.

She found it by its cold, its numeral worn soft in a way that had nothing to do with
bleaching, since none had ever reached it. Stepping onto it, the pillar swallowed the moon
whole from where she stood, and for the length of one held breath, in a cloister built
entirely out of being read, nothing in the Veil could see her at all — a quiet with
nobody's eyes anywhere in it, not even her own.

She stepped back into the light. The Keeper had watched without appearing to move a single
joint, and spoke now in a voice pitched somewhere lower, less entirely the office's.

"What did you see, standing there?"

"Nothing sees me here," she said. "Not even you."

"Correct," said the Keeper, "and rare. Most who reach that stone cannot bear it — to stand
one moment unread, unwatched, unaccounted." Something almost like warmth crossed the still
water of her face. "You bore it. Noted."

*  *  *

She was quiet longer than she had been quiet yet, and when she spoke again it was in a
voice pitched somewhere new — under the reading-voice, under the office, worn thin from
disuse.

"The last thing is the only hard one," she said, "and it is hard because it is easy. I am
going to tell you a secret. A true one. Mine. And then we shall see what your hands are
like for holding what is not yours."

Around the chamber, the wax on ten thousand books seemed, impossibly, to lean inward to
listen.

She told it. I am not going to tell it to you.

I know that's strange, from someone who has promised, this whole book through, to deal
honestly — and I have; I have not lied to you once, tonight or any night before it. But
there is a difference between a thing withheld and a thing untrue, and the Keeper of the
Veil taught it to the Fool with more patience than most of us are ever offered, so I mean
to honour the lesson rather than report it. What I will give you is its shape, because the
shape is mine to give and the words are not: it was old, it had cost three centuries to
carry and had never once been set down, and it was the kind of true that does not get less
true for being kept — only heavier, the longer nobody is trusted with the weight of it.

The Fool listened without a sound, and felt the vertigo of being handed something far too
large for a stranger — and understood, in the same breath, that she was not quite a
stranger any longer.

The wanting to ask more came first, sudden as thirst, right behind her teeth. She held it
there and said, when she trusted herself to say anything: "That must have been lonely."

"It was the office," said the Keeper. "The office is always lonely." A stillness, then,
almost gently: "You did not ask me to say more of it. I notice that."

The wanting to say it back came second — the itch to prove she'd truly heard, to hand the
secret home dressed in her own words, as though that made the holding of it safer. She let
the reach go slack. "Tell me about Pip instead," she said.

Something in the Keeper's face came as near to relief as it had come all night. "The dog.
Yes. Let us speak of the dog, who has never repeated a thing in his life." Her eyes went,
for the first time, to Pip, patient at the Fool's heel like a man sitting out a sermon he
approved of. "A better keeper than most vowed here."

The third temptation was the quietest, and came in a long silence the Keeper let stretch
on, the way a person tests a floorboard by standing on it. It would have been so easy to
fill it, to prove her own good faith by saying more than was asked for. She set the easier
answer down instead, the way you set down a thing you have decided, after all, not to
need.

"Whatever it is," she said, "it's yours. It stays that way."

The Keeper closed her eyes for the length of one breath. "Yes," she said, very quietly.
"It stays that way." And then, so low the Fool nearly missed it: "...You strange, blank,
kept-mouthed thing. It stays that way."

*  *  *

Nothing happened. That was the whole of it, and the strangeness: no fanfare, no shadow
rising off the pillars to be fought — only a woman who had built a test to be failed,
watching a stranger fail to fail it, plainly not knowing what to do with her own face as a
result. No staff had left the Fool's hand all evening. It would not, tonight, need to.

"You did not press," said the Keeper. "You did not trade. You did not so much as *repeat*
it back to be certain. Three centuries I have offered that secret to the silence itself,
and the silence at least had the decency to be empty." She looked at the Fool the way you
look at a sum come out even, against expectation. "You are not empty. You simply chose to
be quiet."

She took a step — possibly the first in three hundred years not part of some fixed,
practised motion — unsteady, a foot testing ground it did not yet trust. "I built this to
keep everyone out. It never occurred to me that the way through it was to want nothing on
the other side." She almost smiled, and the almost cost her something visible. "You have
unmade my whole cloister by being *trustworthy* at it, and I do not know whether to thank
you or bar the gates."

Then, all at once, the grey wax sealing ten thousand shelves began to crack — a long,
spreading, cloister-wide sound like ice going out on a river at the first true thaw in
three hundred years. The frozen moonlight wavered overhead, once, as though remembering it
was permitted to move.

The office cracked with the wax. That patiently-rude mask came apart at some seam the Fool
could not have pointed to, and underneath was only a tired woman who had been holding her
breath since before anyone now living had drawn their first one.

"There is one secret I have kept longest," she said. "Longer than the fear. Longer than
the silence. I have held it so close, so many years, that I stopped trusting I still
remembered how it —"

She stopped. Something surfaced in her the way the true bell had rung: whole, unbroken,
the first of its kind in three hundred years. Her own name, come home to her before it
came home to anyone else in the world.

"...*Vesper.*"

She turned it over, testing the shape of it, the way a woman just off a long silence
weighs the first word she means to keep. "Vesper. Yes. That was the name I was keeping." A
laugh escaped her, rusty and astonished, borrowed from no office at all. "Of course it
was. The one secret worth the whole shelf, and I sealed it in with all the rest, and very
nearly forgot which drawer."

She looked at the Fool properly then — not reading her any longer, only *seeing* her,
which turned out to be a different and much gentler thing. She reached into the
wax-cracked dark of the nearest shelf and drew out a single card. No flourish, no
truesight — only a keeper handing over, at long last, the one thing that had finally,
simply, become hers to give.

"Trump the Second. Secrets, if it must be written down, and I suppose now it must." She
folded the Fool's fingers over it. "Sealed script will open to you. You will hear what the
Blanks whisper, and see what the world hides, and slip behind the veil when struck. I kept
these powers safe for three hundred years by refusing to use them." A pause, almost wry.
"Do the opposite. It suits you better."

The card went into the second pocket behind her heart, the willed one, and settled as
though the seam had been waiting for it.

*  *  *

Beyond the pillars the change rolled outward like a tide going out for the first time in
three hundred years. The mist thinned, the near shelves resolving, then the far, then the
whole shape of a cloister the Fool had never once seen entire, and everywhere the wax had
cracked, doors the Veil had never advertised stood plainly open — visible now for no
better reason than that the secret of them was simply over.

Below, the sleepers were stirring — not waking with a jolt but surfacing, the way a diver
comes up from deep water: quills lifting off old sentences, heads coming up one after
another into a hall gone soft with ordinary lamplight. And everywhere, low and astonished,
they were beginning, for the first time, to *murmur*.

"I dreamed," said one, wondering at her own quill as though it had grown a new opinion
overnight. "Of a sea, I think. It was lovely."

"I never wondered how my sentence ended," said another, reading her own page as if for the
first time. "It ends, *and so the moon came down at last.* Fancy that."

Sister Amity stood at the head of the stair with both hands pressed to her mouth,
watching her whole silent order come apart into sound.

"They're — listen," she managed. "That one's *dreaming out loud*. And that one's asking a
question, an actual out-loud question, and someone's going to answer it, aren't they.
Twice, even. As many times as she likes."

She was crying and laughing both, plainly undecided which she was permitted. "I took my
vows into the quiet. I *loved* the quiet. It was the one thing here that was always the
same, and now it's — going. Softening. In a day it'll be an ordinary library full of
ordinary noise, and I don't know if a cloister that dreams and gossips is still the
cloister I—" She caught herself. Wiped her face hard with the heel of one hand, the way
you wipe away a thing you're cross with yourself for letting show. Managed, wobbling, one
honest laugh, because even a sad thing is owed that much.

"Listen to me. Mourning a *silence*. Three hundred years I wanted something to happen, and
the moment it did, I miss the nothing." She looked at the Fool, and at Pip, something
almost fond and almost helpless both. "There's no shelf for me either, is there. You two
are a matched set."

Nobody answered that, because there was no honest answer that would have made it smaller.
She let it stand.

*  *  *

The causeway ran clear now, mist lifting in long slow banners, the pillars standing whole
against a sky that was, for the first time in three hundred years, only *waiting* to move
rather than forbidden to. Far back between them, small at this distance, Vesper — she
reminded herself, and felt the rightness of the new word settle — lifted one hand, palm
out. Not a farewell, quite. An acknowledgement. Keeper to Excuse. She let it fall and did
not wave again, the very last thing this particular woman still had a habit of not saying
twice.

*Hear that?* said the Querent, warm and easy again, the register it kept for the road.
*Neither do I. That's not the old silence — that's just quiet, the ordinary kind, the kind
that comes after a thing is finished instead of before it starts. First genuinely quiet
moment the Veil's had in three centuries. Rather nice, isn't it.*

Behind them, very faint, a bell that was whole now rang once more, for no reason at all
except the joy of being able to.

*You kept her secret*, the Querent went on, something under the lightness of it, the way
there is sometimes something under a floorboard. *Never so much as repeated it back.
You're good at that — holding onto what you're told and giving nothing away.* A pause,
drier than the mist had ever managed to be. *Takes one to know one, little Excuse. I've
been keeping a rather large one from *you* since the first card. Ask me sometime. Twice,
even. There's no rule against it out here.*

She smiled, and did not ask. Not yet. She had learned that much tonight: some things are
worth being asked for properly, and she was willing, for the moment, to wait to be the one
doing the asking.

*  *  *

The causeway ran on ahead, clear and pale, back toward the Longroad's ring. But south of
it — off to her left, where the land had been climbing gently the whole while without her
noticing — the ground did something she had not seen it do anywhere else on the journey.
It fell. Not steeply. Almost gently, the way a voice falls when a person decides,
mid-sentence, not to finish what they were saying — down and down, out of the moon's
borrowed light, into a country the colour of a candle held too near a window: flat,
silver, uncannily still.

There was a road down into it — had always been one, running alongside the Longroad's
whole southern arc the entire while, patient as a held breath, waiting to be noticed. Its
signs had names on them for a while, and then simply stopped. Not broken, not weathered
blank — merely finished, the way a sentence is finished, with nothing added after the full
stop.

She asked a carter where the road went, and something crossed his face that was not quite
fear and not quite grief, and he found, with sudden conviction, urgent business with his
horse's off-side shoe. She asked a woman with a basket, who smiled the smile people keep
for questions they've decided not to have heard, wished her safe travels, and went the
other way.

"Where does it go?" she asked, finally, of no one in particular — of the voice that had an
answer for nearly everything, that had told her the truth every single time even when it
would not tell her all of it.

Nobody answered.

She waited, the way you wait through a silence you have learned, this very evening, to
recognise as its own kind of honesty. Pip sat down at the edge of the drop and looked
south with his ears up, not moving — the stillness of a dog who has been somewhere before,
and is choosing, for once, not to say so.

They stood together at the top of that lowering road, looking down into country the
colour of a guttering candle, under a sky that had forgotten how to be moonlit and not yet
learned how to be anything else.

Somewhere down there — past the last named signpost, past the last person willing to say
the word out loud — was the Stillmarsh.

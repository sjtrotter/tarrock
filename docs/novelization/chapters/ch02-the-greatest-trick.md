# Chapter Two — The Greatest Trick

She got out of the haywain the undignified way, which is the only way there is, and stood
in the lane picking straw out of her collar, and that was when she noticed the smoke.

A fry-stall stood at the top of the lane with a griddle going under it, and the smoke went
straight up out of the pipe for about the height of a man and then stopped going anywhere
at all. It did not thin. It did not lean. It hung there in a crooked grey column — the
exact shape of a breeze that had happened once, long ago, and had never been followed up.

Behind her, over the hedge, the birds were still at it in the last of the light. Ahead of
her they were not. The line between ran along the hedge as neat as a seam, and when she
walked through it the sound stopped, the way a door shuts.

Then the banners: strung up the poles in their dozens, striped red and cream, every one
standing out stiff and full as though a good wind were in it — held mid-snap, corner
turned over and never turning back. Nothing anywhere moved but a great quantity of dust
hanging in the gaslight in slow golden constellations, which parted politely round her as
she walked and swirled shut again behind.

*There*, said the Querent in her ear, comfortably. *You've felt it. I'll not spoil it by
naming it.*

"There's no wind," she said.

*Isn't there. Well spotted. Keep hold of that.*

Then the smell: popcorn, hot and buttered and overwhelming, and old the way a cellar is
old. It did not drift to her on any current. It was simply there, and had been for three
hundred years.

Over an arch of painted board, in letters gone soft at the edges from being retouched by
loving hands too many times, it said:

**WELCOME, WELCOME, ALWAYS WELCOME.**

And a young man in a striped waistcoat two sizes too enthusiastic for him abandoned his
post at the rope line and came across the green at a dead sprint.

"Fresh face!" he shouted, from about forty feet out. "Oh, we do *love* a fresh face round
here — sit anywhere, the good seats are all the seats!" He arrived, out of breath, radiant.
"Also you fell out of the *sky*, friend, and I still haven't decided if that's the best
ticket I've ever sold or the worst."

He put out his hand. She looked at it, worked out what it was for, and shook it.

"Flick," he said. "Page of Wands, and personally — *personally* — the first friendly face
you'll meet on the whole Spread. I checked. Welcome to the Prestige! Show's been running
three hundred years and it has never, not once, had a night this good."

"Every night's the best night?"

"Every single one! That's showbusiness for you."

Pip sat down on Flick's foot. Flick did not appear to notice.

"What is this place?"

"The Prestige! Finest show on the whole Spread — and before you ask, yes, the *only* show
on the whole Spread. We don't get much competition out here."

She looked past him at the tents: striped canvas rising in naves and aisles like something
built to be prayed in, gaslit gold from within, the shadows of people printed on the walls
of them and not moving.

"Why hasn't the show ended?"

"*Ended*?" Flick laughed. It was a real laugh; that was the awful part. "Why would it end?
It's *good*. Best crowd, best acts, best —" and there it was, a half-second where his face
went somewhere else and came back, "— best everything. Every night."

"You sound unsure."

"Do I? Must be the popcorn talking." He said it fast, and he said it kindly, and he did
not look at her while he said it. "Go on, go and enjoy yourself. That's an order from
Management, and I *am* Management, technically, on Tuesdays."

"Who's the Magician?"

"Ah!" He came back to himself all at once, the way a lamp comes back when the wick's
trimmed. "The Bataleur. Star of the whole thing. And — get this — first volunteer in three
hundred years is going up there tonight, matter of fact. Lucky soul, whoever it turns out
to be."

"Can I see the show?"

"Course you can!" He was already digging in his waistcoat. "Three little acts first, mind.
House of Mirrors, the Menagerie, the High Wire — earn your stub at each and the big tent
opens right up." He pressed a folded paper programme into her hand, printed in three
colours, each act with a small empty box beside it waiting for a stamp. "Three acts, three
stubs, one ticket to the big show. Go on — Pip looks like he knows his way around a
carnival already."

She did not ask how he knew the dog's name. It did not occur to her until much later, by
which time there was nobody sensible left to ask.

*  *  *

The main tent lay across her path to everything else, so she went in at the back of the
audience, and that is where the Prestige stopped being charming.

There were nine hundred people in there, give or take, shoulder to shoulder on board
benches in three-hundred-year-old Sunday best — bonnets, watch chains, a boy in a collar
that had plainly been the worst hour of his life — and every one of them facing front, at
a stage with nothing on it.

Nobody turned to look at her. Nobody had turned to look at anything in a very long time.

Halfway down the nearest row a woman was holding out a toffee apple to the child beside
her, arm extended, wrist gone soft under the weight of the gesture. The child's hand was
six inches from the apple and had been for three centuries. The toffee shone like new. Of
course it did. Nothing here was allowed to go off.

She walked down the aisle very quietly, which was ridiculous, and she knew it was
ridiculous, and went on doing it.

"We're in the *good* seats," a man remarked, to nobody, as she passed. "Three hundred
years, the good seats. Worth the wait, I expect."

"My father queued all night for these tickets," said the woman beside him, comfortably.
"Wonderful man. Haven't the faintest idea what became of him."

"Ferridge there's been in the front row longer than any of us. Says he doesn't remember
what he did before the show." A pause. "Neither do the rest of us, come to that."

In the third row, feet not quite reaching the ground, sat a girl of about eight with a
ribbon in her hair, swinging her legs to a rhythm only she could hear — the one thing in
the tent moving under its own steam. She turned right round on the bench to look.

"Are you new?" she said. "We don't get new very often. Actually we don't get new *ever*.
You're the newest thing in three hundred years, I should think, other than the popcorn,
and even that's mostly the same popcorn."

"How long have you been sitting here?"

"Since before my mum was born, I think." The girl beamed. "Isn't that funny."

She said it exactly the way you say a funny thing. It was not, quite, and the tent seemed
to know it: nine hundred people held a beat of quiet nobody had asked them for. Then Wren
went back to swinging her feet and watching the empty stage, patient as stone.

I intend to be honest with you throughout, which is not the same as being forthcoming, so:
standing in that aisle, the Fool did a sum. She had no numbers for it — she was one day
old — but she had the shape. *These people are waiting. The waiting is what's wrong. To
mend it, somebody must take the waiting away.* She could not have told you what that would
cost. Neither could I, at the time, though I had a much better idea.

She went out the side of the tent, and Pip went with her, and the peach stayed where it
was.

*  *  *

The House of Mirrors was a long canvas gullet of angled looking-glass, lit from below, and
the moment she stepped into it there were forty of her.

They did not behave. She raised a hand and thirty-nine hands came up, but the fortieth
came up a heartbeat late, and by the time she'd found it in the glass it was doing
something else entirely. She turned a corner and met herself coming back, wearing an
expression she was fairly sure she had never made in her life.

Pip's opinion of the House of Mirrors was that it contained a great many other white dogs
and he did not care for a single one of them. He inspected the nearest, found it insolent,
and thereafter walked pressed to her boot with his nose forward, ready.

At the centre of the first chamber stood a woman in a coat sewn all over with mirror
shards, so that she came apart into fragments of herself whenever she moved.

"Everybody thinks the trick's in the glass," she said. "It's never in the glass. It's in
whichever one of us is fool enough to look real while we're doing it."

"How do I tell which one's you?"

"Watch for the one who's tired." Tansy Quill smiled — a lovely smile, worn down to the
canvas at the corners. "Glass doesn't get tired. People do. Even people who've been doing
this same trick for three hundred years." A beat. "*Especially* those people."

Then there were nine of her, and eight of them were perfect.

It took a long while. She was wrong twice, and being wrong meant walking into a mirror
hard enough to make Pip sneeze with disapproval. But by the third chamber she had stopped
looking at the coats and the faces and started looking for the one small thing that did
not match — and there it was. One Tansy Quill, of nine, held her shoulders a fraction
lower than the rest. Just tired. Nothing magical about it at all.

She walked up and stood in front of her, and the other eight went out like blown candles.

"There," said Tansy, pleased, and stamped the first stub with a little brass press she
kept in a pocket. She held on to the programme half a second longer than she needed to.
"If the show ever stops, I go where nobody's looked at the same trick twice. Fresh eyes
are the only currency that matters to me." She let go. "Not that it stops. Only if."

*  *  *

The Menagerie was a wagon-yard behind the tents, and everything in it was made of paper.

Folded, hinged, painted, jointed with wire and pinned with brass — a lion the size of a
pony, three ostriches, an elephant, and a peacock of such structural ambition that she
stopped walking. They moved. Not much, not smoothly, and with a sound like a book being
riffled, but they moved: the lion's ribs went in and out, the peacock's tail opened a
hand's-breadth and shut again.

A very large man sat on an upturned barrel with the elephant's back leg across his knees,
doing something delicate to a hinge with fingers like sausages.

"Wind gets in the paper," he said, without looking up. "They go a bit wild. Nothing worse."

There has not been a wind in the Prestige for three hundred years. He said it anyway,
because it was what you said, and because you do not stop saying a thing merely on account
of its no longer being true. I have watched an entire world do that. He was only a man.

Three of the beasts had wandered out of the pens — an ostrich picking about in the guy
ropes, a paper hare, and the lion, which had wedged itself between two wagons and was
riffling gently to itself.

"Send the dog at the stray ones," said Perrin Loom. "He'll herd better than I ever could,
and I lift *bridges*."

She looked down at Pip. Pip looked up at her. She had never sent him anywhere in her life,
and not knowing how lasted about as long as not knowing how to hold the staff had: she
made a sound in her throat and flicked two fingers, and he was gone like a thrown stone.

What followed was, by some distance, the best thing that had happened to her since she
woke up. Pip did not bark at the paper beasts. He *worked* them — head low, shoulders
down, cutting the angles, turning the ostrich with a look and steering it into the pen at
a brisk trot. The lion he simply leaned on. The hare put up a fight and lost.

"There," said Perrin, folding the elephant's leg shut. "Good dog. Good — whatever you are,
too."

He stamped the second stub with a hand the size of a dinner plate. The stamp disappeared
entirely inside it and he had to fish about for it afterwards.

"They'll ask you, before it's over. Whether we ought to go on the road or stop somewhere.
Everyone's got a view." He shrugged, which was an event, geologically. "I go where they
go. Not much point being strong on your own."

*  *  *

The High Wire was strung between two poles at an altitude that could only be called a
joke, lit the whole way with little lanterns, and there was no net.

There was, in fact, a net. It lay folded on the grass with a sign on it reading *NET
(AVAILABLE)*, and the sign had been there so long the paint had lifted, and nobody had
ever once asked, which was the joke.

Beneath the near pole a woman sat at a card table with a lamp and a worn deck laid out in
a cross.

"Shall I read your cards before you risk your neck?" she said. Then, before the Fool could
open her mouth: "...No. Don't answer, I already know. I always already know. It's the least
funny thing about this job."

"Will you read mine?"

"I already have, dear." Marigold Fen turned a card over, looked at it, and put it back
without comment. "You're the sort of card that doesn't sit still for a reading. Comes with
the title, I expect."

"What do you see for the show?"

"An ending, eventually." She said it lightly, the way you'd mention rain. "A good one, if
I'm reading it right. And I am always reading it right."

The Fool thought about the peach, and the woman's arm held out for three hundred years.

"I'd rather not know."

Marigold Fen laughed out loud — one short surprised bark of it — and put the back of her
hand to her mouth as though the laugh had escaped.

"Wisest answer anyone's given me all century. Off you go." And, as the Fool started up the
ladder: "I read cards for a living in a world made of them. No, I've never once found that
funny. Ask me again in three hundred years."

The wire was a finger's width and the whole world was underneath it.

She went out in three stretches, and here is what nearly killed her: on the second, the
wind came up. A steady warm press of it from below and behind, smelling of oiled canvas
and lamp black, lifting her coat and putting a long shiver down the wire. She leaned into
it, because you lean into a wind. Then it dropped, exactly, all at once, the way weather
never does — and she went over sideways with one arm windmilling and only came back
because her feet had already learned more than her head had.

She stood shaking, halfway across the Prestige, in air that smelled of machinery, and
understood that she had just been lied to by something. She did not yet know by what.

*Keep hold of that as well*, said the Querent, very quietly.

At the far pole a wiry old man was juggling five clubs. He was not watching them. He was
watching her come, and while he watched, the clubs went round and round in a lazy figure
like something a river does.

"Three hundred years," he said, "five clubs, not one dropped." He caught all five in one
hand without appearing to notice he had finished. "Some nights I think about letting just
the one go. Just to see." He looked at the clubs in his fist. "Never quite manage it.
Suppose that's its own kind of trick."

He stamped the third stub, and pressed the programme back into her hands, and held on to
it for a moment the way Tansy had.

"If the tent ever folds for good," said Old Tomkin, "I'd not mind new roads under it. Same
act, new faces watching." The corner of his mouth went. "Might even drop a club for them."

*  *  *

Three stubs. The main tent's flap glowed gold ahead of her, and from inside came a noise
she had not heard from that tent before: a crowd, awake, leaning forward. It swelled as
she came, and it was — unmistakably, for the first time in three hundred years — *for her*.

She put the programme into the gatekeeper's hand and went in.

The ring was enormous: gilt and red velvet worn soft to the weave, sawdust raked in
perfect circles, and above it, tier on tier, every seat full and every face turned. Nine
hundred people looked at her at once. It went through her like cold water.

The spotlights swung down, and out of the dark came a man in a top hat and tails, arms
spreading, smile arriving before he did.

"Ladies! Gentlemen! And whatever climbed out of that haywain earlier tonight —" the crowd
found this hilarious, in unison, on cue "— a *volunteer!* Our first new volunteer in three
hundred years!"

The gasp came up off the benches in a single perfect wave, the way the tide comes in, and
you could hear three centuries of rehearsal in it. The Bataleur's smile was a wonderful
thing, and worn absolutely through: the smile of a man who has made this exact gesture ten
thousand times because he has made it ten thousand times.

"Now then. Watch closely." He turned once on the spot, and the sawdust did not stir behind
him. "This is the greatest trick this ring has ever held, and it is about to be performed
on *you*."

Three spotlights snapped on. There were three of him.

They came round her at a walk, evenly spaced, identical down to the shine on their shoes,
tipping their hats in perfect time — and then all three came in at once, and the trick
turned out to be that only one of them could hurt her and all three could try.

She was badly outmatched; she had owned a quarterstaff for one day. But she had spent the
evening being taught, by a tired woman in a coat of broken glass, to stop looking at the
big obvious thing and start looking for the small thing that did not match — and it was in
the buttonhole. Each of them wore a red rose in the lapel. Two were flawless, waxwork,
eternal. The third was wilting: only a little, and a little more with every exchange, a
petal browning at the edge and curling. By the time she understood what she was seeing she
understood something with it — that the wilting was the only honest thing in the tent, and
that it was happening to the one doing the work.

She hit the man with the dying rose.

He staggered. The crowd screamed with delight. She went for a decoy by mistake and it
burst into confetti — and out of the confetti a stagehand straightened up, brushed itself
off, and came at her with a mallet: blank-faced, tabarded, a Page of Wands on its chest.

"Which one, which one!" the Bataleur cried, from somewhere, from everywhere. "A showman
never tells his own secrets — good policy, that! Keeps a man employed for three centuries
running!"

"What's your name?" she shouted.

There was the smallest hitch in the patter — a quarter of a beat; you would have missed it
if you were enjoying yourself.

"The Bataleur!" he sang out. "The Magician! Master of the Manifest, the Marvellous, the—"

Which is a list of things, she thought, ducking a mallet, and not one of them is a name.

Then the world went slow and syrupy in her chest — she had begun to be able to *ask* for
it — and she stepped inside a swing that had already committed and put the Bindle into the
ribs of the Bataleur with the dying rose, and the whole ring flipped over.

Not a figure of speech. The floor rotated on its axis and dumped the lot of them into the
dark beneath.

She landed rolling on packed earth among the machinery of every trick she had seen that
night: ropes and pulleys and sandbag counterweights, trapdoor springs, a rack of identical
top hats, a rail of identical tail-coats. And at the end of it, big as a barn door, two
enormous canvas lungs on a crank, wheezing air into a pipe as thick as a man — the wind:
the great unstopping wind of the Prestige, the one that had nearly had her off the wire,
going up through the boards to fill the banners overhead. Not weather. Never weather. A
machine, breathing a dead thing's chest in and out for three hundred years.

"*As above, so below!*" The Bataleur was up already, hat gone, hair on end, and he sounded
almost glad. "Every good trick's got machinery holding it up! Mine's just been holding the
*same* trick up a touch longer than most!"

The doubles came on again, and down here they were shabby — strings visible at the
shoulders, paint thin, mouths not quite finished. It was easier, and much, much worse.

She fought him the length of the under-stage, between the counterweights, in the smell of
oil and old rope, and at the end of it he was leaning on a prop crate with his chest going
and his rose down to two brown petals. She brought the staff round one last time and
stopped it a hand's width from his head, because he had put his palm up.

He sat down heavily on the crate.

And the office cracked.

I have watched this happen twenty-one times and it has never once stopped frightening me.
It is not a wound. It is the sound a plaster mask makes coming apart at the seam — the top
hat and the patter and three hundred years of the same smile all going at once, in long
bright fissures, off the face of a man who has worn them so long that what is underneath
has gone soft for lack of weather.

"That's — that's rather the finish, isn't it." He blinked at his own hands. "Funny. I don't
believe I ever actually reached the finish before—"

He stopped.

Something came up under the crack. Not blood. A name, surfacing the way a held breath
finally lets go.

"...*Wicke*."

He said it slowly, tasting the shape of it, the way a man off a long desert road tastes
water and has to stop a moment to believe in it.

"Wicke. That's — yes. That's me." He laughed once, and it was nothing like the laugh he'd
been using all night. "I'd almost forgotten the *sound* of it."

He got up off that crate a different man from the one who had sat down on it: looser,
older about the eyes, entirely unperformed. He looked at her properly for the first time
all evening — one performer clocking another.

"First new volunteer in three centuries," he said, "and you turn out to be the one act I
never saw coming. That's — that's rather good, actually." He shook his head, with enormous
professional respect. "That's a *good* trick."

Then he reached into the air beside his ear and took a card out of it. No flourish, no
patter: just a man handing over a thing that was, at long last, simply his to give.

"Trump the First. Manifest, they'll call it, if anyone ever writes it down properly." He
put it into her hand and folded her fingers over it. "Take it. Hands were always the
trick, you know. Mine. Yours." He shrugged. "Everybody's, really."

And then the world ended.

Not the world — I am getting ahead of myself, and I have promised not to do that. The
*show* ended. But it was the first thing in three hundred years that had been permitted to
end, and it went off under that carnival like a rope parting.

The bellows stopped. Both lungs sighed out and did not fill again, and every banner
overhead went slack at once with a sound like a great flock settling.

And then, into the silence where the machine had been, came a draught.

It came down the under-stage stairs from the open air: cold, damp, smelling of hay and cut
grass and rain three fields off. It moved her hair. It went along the racks of hanging
coats and stirred every one of them, and up in the tent above their heads three hundred
years of dust came down out of the lamplight all at once, softly, in a slow bright fall
like the smallest possible snow.

Somewhere above, a rope began to swing. Nothing had swung here since before anyone was
born.

Wicke stood in the middle of it with his face turned up and said nothing at all, which for
him was a very great deal.

She looked at the card in her hand. Then she opened her coat and put it into the first of
the three sewn pockets — the near one, over the heart, the *now* one — and it went in as
though the pocket had been cut for it, which it had been.

Her hands came alive. That is the only way I can put it and be truthful. She had two
hands, and then, without gaining anything, she had the *idea* of a third: a reach that
went out fifteen feet past her fingers, closed on a top hat across the under-stage, and
brought it to her through the air. She dropped it at once and made a small undignified
noise.

*Three little pockets*, said the Querent, *and twenty cards still out there to fill them.
You'll have opinions about that arrangement before we're through.* A dry pause. *Everybody
does.*

*  *  *

Above them, the audience got up.

Not all at once, and not neatly. It came in a long ragged wave from the front rows
backward — nine hundred people standing after three hundred years of sitting — and the
sound of it was knees. Knees all up and down the tent, cracking like a fire taking hold,
and then a rolling groan of pure sensation, the noise a person makes when a limb they had
forgotten about comes back.

The woman in the fourth row lowered her arm. The child took the toffee apple. Nobody could
persuade her to eat it, but she carried it about for a week, and I count that a success.

And somewhere at the back, an old man finished a laugh he had plainly begun a very long
time ago. It came out of him in two halves, arriving in two different centuries, and the
second half turned into a coughing fit and then into something else his wife had to hold
his arm through.

"I don't recall my legs doing that before," somebody said, wonderingly. "Cracking like
that. I don't recall my *legs* at all, come to that."

"Good seats, they were," said another. "Still are, I suppose. Just — not the only seats
any more."

Wren stood up on her bench to see over the heads, and tugged her mother's sleeve, and
shouted across the tent to the man in the ring with the cracked-open face:

"What happens now? What does everybody *do* now?"

And Wicke — who for three hundred years had not had a single honest answer for any of the
nine hundred people in front of him — spread his hands.

"Anything," he said. "Anything at all, poppet. That's rather the whole idea."

*  *  *

The office door backstage had been jammed shut so long that it came off its hinge when
they opened it, and the room behind was small and cold and knee-deep in mail.

Three hundred years of it: letters, bills, playbills, invitations, a bird's nest in the
window frame that had never had a bird in it. A desk. A single chair nobody had sat in
since before the Stall, and Wicke sat down in it as if it still fitted, which it did not,
quite.

The Troupe crowded the doorway — Old Tomkin with his clubs, Marigold Fen with her deck
under one arm, Tansy Quill throwing little wandering fragments of lamplight round the
walls, and Perrin Loom, who did not so much stand in the doorway as wear it.

Wicke picked an envelope off the top of the drift and opened it with his thumb.

"Lamp oil," he said. "Forty gallons. Delivered, unpaid." He turned it over. "Three hundred
and six years overdue, at four per centum. Compounding." He looked up. "I want you all to
know that I would rather have fought her again."

Flick, from somewhere out in the passage: "I *told* you there was post!"

And at the edge of the group, not quite able to leave and not quite able to come in, stood
an old man in a good coat with a programme rolled in one fist. He had been in the front
row since before any of them. His face was wet and he had not noticed.

"I don't rightly know," said Ferridge, "what I'm meant to do with an evening that hasn't
got a show in it." He looked at the programme in his hand as if it had been handed to him
by a stranger. "Silly thing to grieve, I know."

Nobody said anything.

"Grieving it all the same," he said.

They all turned, then, and looked at her — which she had not expected and did not want,
and which would go on happening to her for the rest of the journey.

"Well," said Tomkin, into the silence. "There's a question in it, isn't there. Whether we
go, or whether we stop."

"Fresh eyes," said Tansy, to the ceiling.

"Same table," said Marigold Fen, to nobody. "Twice in a row. Just the once. A woman likes
to lay her cards on the *same* table twice in a while." She was shuffling as she said it,
fast and beautifully, the way other people breathe.

Perrin said nothing, having already said it in the yard over a paper elephant's leg.

So the Fool stood in a dead man's office with a card in her coat and straw still in her
collar, one day old, being asked what a hundred-odd people ought to do with the rest of
their lives. She thought about a tired woman who wanted fresh eyes, and an old man who
wanted, more than he could say, to be somewhere he was allowed to drop a club.

"Go," she said. "Be seen by people who've never seen you."

Tomkin's whole face came up like a lamp. Tansy Quill shut her eyes. Perrin Loom nodded
once, slowly, and that was that, because he went where they went.

Marigold Fen kept shuffling. She did not stop and she did not look up, and after a moment
she said, quite pleasantly, "There. I did say I was reading it right."

"Marigold—"

"No, no." She cut the deck one-handed. "It's a good ending, dear. I told you it was a good
one. I only ever said it was good." She put the cards away, and stood, and patted the
Fool's arm on the way past with real warmth, and did not stay to be thanked for it.

And Ferridge, who got no choice at all because nobody had thought to offer him one, stood
in the doorway and watched them decide, and rolled his programme tighter, and said nothing
whatsoever.

*  *  *

It took four days to bring the Prestige down, and she stayed for all of it — the only time
in the whole long journey she watched a region change at walking pace.

The canvas came down in enormous slow breaths, panel by panel, folded six at a time into
bales like loaves. The gaslamps were crated in straw. The benches went onto wagons. The
WELCOME arch was taken apart and its soft-worn letters stacked in a barrow, face to face,
like plates. And the ring was raked flat, and stopped being a ring, and became a circle of
paler grass, and then a field.

The weather came in, which nobody there had a word for. It rained on the second day —
properly, sideways, out of a sky that had not changed its colour in three centuries and
did not change it now, because the sun of the Spread was nailed where it was and would be
for a long while yet; the rain simply arrived anyway, like a guest who has stopped
waiting to be announced — and Flick stood out in it with his arms spread and his mouth
open until somebody made him come inside. The paper lion went soft in the wet and folded up on
itself, and Perrin Loom carried it to the fire himself and looked at the fire a long while
afterwards.

On the fourth day the wagons stood at the crossroads with their wheels greased, and
Old Tomkin juggled to pass the time — five clubs, round and round, because a habit is not
a chain and there is no hurry.

"Best show we ever ran," Flick told her, hauling on a rope, "and the very last one! Bit of
a mixed bag, feelings-wise, if I'm honest — which I try never to be, in this line of work."
He tied off badly and looked down the eastern road with an expression she would come to
know very well. "Road's calling, apparently. Never been much further than the crossroads
myself." He grinned, and it wobbled, and he let it. "Exciting, that."

Ferridge stood on the empty green with his programme.

She went over. She had nothing to give him, and there was nothing to be given: every one
of these was going to cost somebody, and this was the cheapest bill the journey ever sent,
and it was already too much.

"You'll be off, then," he said. "You've the look."

"I'm sorry about the evenings."

"Ah." He waved the programme at her, gently, and put it away in his inside pocket, over
the heart, where a man keeps a thing he means to keep. "It was a *good* show, mind.
Whatever else. It was a good show."

*  *  *

The eastern road out of the Prestige had been shut behind the tents for three hundred
years. Now it lay open: a chalk-white lane running out between hedges that had begun,
tentatively, to move.

She went down it while the lamps were still lit, with straw in her collar, a card in her
coat, a rose at her belt with three petals on it, and a small white dog trotting well out
ahead in the manner of a professional who has been to all these places before and does not
wish to make a fuss about it.

Behind her, wagon wheels. Ahead, hedgerows learning to move again, and weather loose in a
sky whose sun still sat exactly where it had sat for three hundred years — the days here
would be kept by candles and appetites for a while yet, and nobody minded, having known no
other way of keeping them. And far off across the whole width of the world, small and silver and perfectly
still, the Axis sat at the centre of everything and glittered like a coin at the bottom of
a well — and in the middle of it, so far away it was barely a flaw in the light, something
turned, and turned, and went on turning.

She looked at it a while. Then she whistled up the dog and walked on east, and did not
look at it again for eleven miles, which for her was a very long time indeed.

# Interlude — The Rim-Watchers

The dark came down over the Axis rim the way nothing had come down over anything in
three hundred years — all at once, entire, and real. Not a fixed dusk. Not a stasis-sky
holding one patient hour forever. An actual, moving dark, with actual stars standing up
out of it in their thousands, sharp as pins, and every soul at the rim who had waited
long enough to see it stopped whatever they were doing and simply looked up for a while,
because nobody living had ever had the chance to before.

*  *  *

**I. THE LENS**

The Loach workshop had four generations crowded into it that night, and all four were
arguing, which was the family's oldest tradition and the one most likely to outlast the
world itself.

"Longer barrel," said the eldest, not for the first time in forty years. "You want
distance, you want length. I've said so since before your mother could hold a chisel."

"You've said so and you've been wrong every single time," said her son, not looking up
from the grinding-wheel. "It's the curve that wants attending, not the tube. A longer
barrel just gives you a longer *wrong*."

His own daughter, arms folded, siding — as she always did — with whichever argument let
her get back to work fastest: "It's neither. It's the polish. It was always going to be
the polish. I've told you both since I was eight."

And the youngest Loach, who was not arguing at all, because she had her eye already down
against the finished glass and had gone very still in the way a person goes still when
something has actually, finally, worked.

"It's a person," she said.

The workshop stopped.

"It's a *person*," she said again, and stepped back so her grandmother could look, and
her grandmother looked, and went the particular pale of somebody handed, at last, after
a lifetime of asking, the exact thing she had asked for. Not a shape any more. Not a
silver rumour turning at the world's centre. A person, dancing, close enough now — with
the truest lens four generations of Loach hands had ever ground, on the one night the
sky had finally gone dark enough to let them — to be unmistakably, entirely a person.

Nobody in the workshop said anything for a long moment. Then the eldest, very quietly,
for once not arguing at all:

"Well. There it is, then. Four generations, and there it is."

She did not sound triumphant. She sounded like someone who had spent a lifetime certain
that the answer, once it arrived, would matter enormously, and had just discovered — on
the one night it could no longer matter in the way she'd meant — that it was already too
late to be the thing she'd wanted it for. The dancer had waited three centuries to be
seen clearly. The Loaches had only managed it on the very evening the dancing was, by
every sign at the rim, about to end.

The youngest set the lens down, gently, as if it might still bruise. "Do we tell
anyone?"

"Tell them what," said her father. "That we finally built the glass, and it only
answered a question nobody's still asking?"

They left the lens on its stand, pointed at the centre, and went out, all four of them
together, to watch the rest of it happen with their own unaided eyes.

*  *  *

**II. THE LINE**

Corvin Rathe stood where he always stood, on the worn patch of ground his own boots had
made over more years than he could number, and did not cross it, because he never had
and did not know, this close to the end of not-knowing, how to begin.

He had heard she was coming. Everyone on the rim had heard. He had told himself, walking
out to his spot that evening same as every evening, that tonight would be no different —
that he would watch, as he always watched, from exactly as far back as fear had ever let
him stand.

But the dark came down, and the dance went on inside it, small and steady and
unmistakably still there, and something in Rathe's chest that had held its breath for
longer than he could account for let a little of it out.

He did not ask her to stop. He never had; that was never the ask. When the small figure
and the smaller dog passed close enough along the approach road for a voice to carry, he
found his own voice arriving before he had entirely decided to use it.

"You'll go all the way in, I expect," he said. Not a question really. "I never have. I
don't say that with any pride in it."

She paused. He saw her look toward him — kindly, unhurried, the way, he thought, a
person looks at someone they have already decided not to argue with.

"When it's done," Rathe said, and his voice did not quite hold steady on the word, "will
you tell me — gently, mind, I'm asking gently — what it looked like. Up close. I've
stood out here the best part of my life not going to see for myself. I'd like, just the
once, for somebody to have gone and looked, and come back, and told an old fool who
never dared it."

She said something back to him then, low — and there was no wind out there to carry it,
nor had there ever been in his hearing, so it went no further than the two of them and
reached nobody else's ears to record. Rathe nodded, once, as though it had been enough,
and stepped back to his worn patch of earth, and did not follow her one pace further.

*  *  *

**III. THE STONE**

Inside the wreath, the Silent Order kept their silence exactly as they had kept it for
three hundred years — except for Elder Sister Loveday, who broke hers, quietly, to
nobody at all, which was, she had decided, the only way she could bear to say it.

"We never held it up," she said, to the dark, to the clean swept stone, to the three
hundred years of unbroken tending that had eaten her whole life and every life before
hers in the order without once being asked whether it wanted to be spent that way. "All
this sweeping. All this reverence. I used to lie awake certain that one missed gesture
would end the world, and do you know — I don't believe that any more. I don't think I've
believed it in years. It was never us. It was only ever *them*, out there, dancing, and
we tended stone."

Nobody answered her, which was, for the first time in three hundred years, exactly what
she wanted.

She should have grieved that — decades handed to an office that had never once been
needed — and some part of her did, a small cold ache under the breastbone for all the
silent hours she would not get back. But mostly, standing there with the dark actually
dark for once and the stars actually up, she felt only relief, enormous and unfamiliar,
like setting down a yoke she had carried so long she had forgotten it could be set down
at all.

"Best-kept amphitheatre in the whole Spread, mind," she added, drier now, allowing
herself the one small blasphemy of the evening. "Say what you like about three hundred
years of vows — the acoustics in here are *appalling*, and I have wanted to say so since
before your grandmother's grandmother, and I find I no longer care who hears me say it."

*  *  *

They all saw her cross, in the end — the Loaches from their workshop door, Rathe from
his worn patch of ground, Loveday from the wreath's own edge — a small figure and a
smaller white dog, moving unhurried through the first true dark any of them had ever
stood in, stars overhead in their thousands with, at last, somewhere properly dark to
shine.

It was into that same dark, some while after, that Ferridge came walking up the last
stretch of road on foot, a packed bag over one shoulder, his knees audibly unhappy about
the distance and his face set with the particular determination of a man who has walked
a very long way and does not intend to discuss it.

"Evening," he said, to whoever was nearest, which happened to be the youngest Loach.
"Don't suppose any of you could point a fellow to the best spot for watching a goodbye?
Not fussy. Just — the best one. I've come rather a long way for a decent view."

Nobody laughed, though it was, in its way, exactly the kind of thing that should have
raised one; and nobody corrected him, either, when he settled at the rim beside the
rest of them, bag still over his shoulder, and looked in the same direction they were
all already looking.

None of them stepped past the wreath line. Not the Loaches, whose four generations of
looking had finally, uselessly, seen. Not Rathe, who had asked for gentleness instead of
company. Not Loveday, whose office had never mattered and whose relief was, for once,
entirely her own. Not Ferridge, who had walked further than any of them that night and
still knew, without needing to be told, exactly where his own two feet were meant to
stop.

That ground, past the wreath, belonged to the Fool alone. Everyone standing at the rim
that night had always known it, without one of them ever having had to say so aloud.

# Interlude — The Lantern Ledger

*Being pages from the book kept by Tarn Loach, ferryman's mate, at the landing of the
Stillmarsh — copied here exactly as he wrote them, blots, crossings-out, and the odd
argument with himself left in.*

*  *  *

**A note, first, on how this book is to be kept** (written some years back, and copied
fair into every new volume since, because a standard, once set, ought to be honoured):
*One name per line. Waiting-time in years, estimated where not known exactly, and marked
"est." so nobody mistakes a guess for a fact. One small true thing about the person,
where I can learn it, so they are not only a name to whoever reads this after me — if
anybody ever does. No opinions of my own. This is a record, not a diary, and I will
thank myself to remember the difference, which I frequently do not.*

**GOODWIFE HALLOW'S GREAT-GRANDMOTHER** — waiting 300 years (est.). Asks after the
weather every visit, though the weather has not changed in living memory. Waits anyway.
Small true thing: she was, by all accounts, a terror at cards, and has not lost the look
of a woman who is still winning.

**THE SAILOR'S WIFE, sixth window along** — waiting 300 years (est.). Watches the
crossing and says nothing. I have tried three times to learn her name and been told,
gently, that it isn't mine to have yet. Small true thing: her candle has never once
guttered, not in three hundred years, and the man who tends it will not hear a word
against tallow merchants in her hearing.

*(A run of some four hundred further entries follows in the same hand, omitted here for
space. They are none of them less important than the two above. I want that written down
somewhere, even if only in my own book, even if only I ever read it.)*

*  *  *

**Note to self, undated:** *A new column has gone in today, at the right-hand margin,
where there has never before been the smallest need of one. I have ruled the line twice
to be sure it is straight. I do not know yet what will go under it, only that something
will, which is a stranger feeling than I have words ready for.*

**Heading, right-hand margin, new column, ruled twice:** CROSSED.

**THE SAILOR'S WIFE, sixth window along.** Crossed today. First true crossing the
landing has ever recorded — mine or my two predecessors' books, and I have checked both,
carefully, standing up, because it did not seem a thing to check sitting down. She went
watching the water, same as always, and then she simply was not watching it any more,
and the ones who loved her closed her window and did not need me for anything except to
write it here, which I have done, and my hand shook doing it, and I am not ashamed to
say so in my own book.

**Note to self:** *Ledger-keeping standard amended. Under CROSSED, record only the day
counted from today, called the First Day, there being no better word for it and no
sense waiting for a finer one. I am aware "day" is a habit of speech more than a fact of
the sky in this marsh. I am keeping the habit. A man has to count from somewhere.*

**OLD BARROW.** Waited 300 years (est.). Crossed, First Day, some hours after the
window. Won a wager laid the night before the Stall, the stakes a loaf of bread neither
baker now living is able to bake — Gammer Thist has instructed me, at some length and
volume, that this makes her the winner and him the loser, on grounds I did not follow
and was not permitted to question. Small true thing: he went out saying it was the
sweetest part of losing, that he wouldn't be about to hear her gloat, and grinning like
the winning hand himself. I have recorded the grin. I stand by the grin.

**GAMMER THIST.** Still waiting, and in tremendous spirits about it, which I did not
expect to be the honest note here and am recording anyway, standards being standards.
Small true thing: she has asked me twice today whether I intend to put "I WON" in the
ledger in letters a hand high. I have explained that this is not how the ledger works.
She has explained that she does not care how the ledger works. We have reached no
agreement. I record the disagreement, in the interest of completeness, and because it
was the first thing all week that made me laugh out loud at the landing, which felt
almost indecent, and then did not.

**SEDGE FENNER.** Crossed. First grave dug in the marsh in three hundred years — I did
not attend, Sallow needed the ferry and someone has to keep it, but I wrote the name in
by lantern-light the same evening, in the new column, with the day counted beside it,
and it seemed to me that mattered nearly as much as standing at the graveside would
have, though I would not say that to a mourner's face.

**Ambient note, several days on:** *The willows have dropped leaf. I am told this is
called autumn, by those old enough to remember the word, and that it happens every year,
somewhere, to people who have never once had to be told what it was. I have added
"AUTUMN (first)" to the top of this page as a heading, the way other books mark a new
chapter. It felt like the kind of thing a ledger ought to notice.*

**GAFFER CORLIN.** Crossed, some days after the window, gentle in his sleep, three
nights before his household knew it. Bettony Marsh sent word down to the landing by a
neighbour, for the book, in her own words exactly, which I set down here unaltered
because they did not want improving: *"Tell the ferry he's not late for once in his
life, and tell it I said that, because he would have wanted the joke on record before
the sadness was."* I have recorded both. I could not, in honesty, have separated them if
asked.

*  *  *

Sallow's own words, this evening, poling in with the lanterns doubled on the water
behind him, recorded as near verbatim as I am able, my memory for his dry remarks being
tolerably good after some years of practising it:

*"Marsh is emptying out around me by the day, Tarn, and do you know — I find I'm rather
looking forward to my own turn, when it comes round fair at the back of the queue, where
it belongs. Not rushing at it. Somebody's to carry the last of them over first, and I'll
not leave that to you on your own. But I'll take it gladly, when it's mine. I've earned
a good long nothing."*

I have written this down twice, once as he said it and once underneath, smaller, so that
if the ink of the first ever fades there will still be the second. I do not entirely
trust my own hand to hold something that important only the once.

*  *  *

**Last entry this book, for now.**

**THE ONE WHO OPENED THE DOOR.** No name given, none asked for, none, I think, offered
lightly to anyone. Came through the landing quiet, did what needed doing at the heart of
the marsh, and went on again before a soul had properly gathered to thank her for it —
I am told she looked faintly alarmed at the idea of being thanked, as though gratitude
were a large dog that might jump up. The small white dog with her was bowed to at every
door she passed, as is proper, though not one person doing the bowing could have told
you why, myself very much included. Small true thing, the truest I have: she is the
reason every entry above this one has a right-hand column at all.

**Note to self, and I mean to keep this one, standards or no standards:** *The book is
very near finishing what it was for. There will be a last name in it eventually, and
then no more waiting-names to add, only crossed ones, and then, I expect, not even
those, once there is nobody left at the landing who has not already gone over. A ledger
that has run out of names to keep is a ledger that has done its whole job.*

*I find I am going to keep writing in it anyway. Somebody ought to remember they were
here. That was always the whole of the reason, and it has not stopped being true just
because the waiting has.*

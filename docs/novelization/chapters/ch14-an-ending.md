# Chapter Fourteen — An Ending

The road out of the Veil went down, and kept going down, and did not at any point suggest an alternative.

For the first half of it she still had the moving air with her. The Veil had woken three days behind her and taken to motion the way a sleeper takes to stretching: mist rolling now instead of standing, the great pillars streaming with it, the cloister's paper stirring under its stones. The wind came off the high ground and travelled with her down the switchbacks, tugging her coat, combing the wet grass into long silver runs.

Then the ground flattened, and the wind did not come down with her.

She noticed it the way a person notices a clock stopping in another room — a small subtraction, felt before it is named. She stopped walking. Behind and above, a stand of alders was still shifting its leaves. Ahead, nothing shifted at all. The line between the two ran across the road as clean as a seam, and when she stepped over it the sound went out of the world: no rustle, no hiss of grass, only her own boots on wet stone and the ordinary business of a dog's feet beside them.

She had crossed twelve of these lines now. It had not stopped being a thing that happened in her chest.

The Stillmarsh came up to meet her without a horizon to announce it. The road lowered and lowered until it was less a road than a habit of firmer ground; then the firmer ground gave out and it was a plank jetty, and the jetty went out over water so black and so flat that at first she took it for a floor.

The sky above it was a low, even, colourless dusk — not going anywhere, not becoming anything, the fixed grey hour this place had been dealt and kept. Reed-beds stood in it to the edge of sight. Standing pools lay between them like coins spilled on a table. And everywhere, on posts and sills and the prows of tied-up punts, small candle-lanterns burned: dozens of them, then hundreds, going away into the flat distance in strings and scatters, every flame low and level and perfectly upright, not one of them guttering, not one of them so much as leaning. Each had its double lying in the water beneath it, exact and untroubled, on a surface that had not been asked to ripple in three hundred years.

She had been hearing about this place since the carnival.

That was the strangest part of arriving: how much of it she already had. Not facts — nobody had ever given her facts. Only a shape made out of other people's swerving. A stallholder in the Bower who had begun to say *marsh* and finished the word as *market*. The Assize's clerks, who had a form for everything, going oddly quiet about where the dead ones' files went. A soldier on the Longroad naming the regions for her on his fingers and simply skipping one, and then, when she pointed at the gap, saying *ah — well* — and offering her a boiled sweet. All of them fond. All of them lowering their voices as though the place were a room with somebody ill in it.

Standing at the head of the jetty, she understood the lowered voices, and thought that everybody had got them slightly wrong. The hush here was not the held breath of the Prestige, waiting for a thing to happen. It was not the frozen roar of the Spire. It was older and gentler than either: the quiet of a house where somebody is sleeping, and everyone else has agreed, without discussion, to let them.

Pip went out along the jetty ahead of her, businesslike, claws ticking on the planks.

At its end lay a long flat-bottomed ferry, and leaning on the pole of it, in no hurry whatsoever, stood a thin, weathered man of no describable age, with the look of somebody who had been just about to sit down for a very long time.

*  *  *

"Mind the step," he said. "Not that a step's ever hurt anyone here. Nothing does. That's rather the trouble with the place."

The step did not hurt her. The boat took her weight without rocking, which was its own small wrongness; she put a hand on the gunwale and felt the water refuse to argue.

Pip did not wait to be invited. He padded up the length of the ferry and sat at the prow with his ears up and his chest out, facing the way they would be going, like a small paid pilot.

The ferryman looked down at him.

And bowed.

It was not much of a bow — an inclination of the head and a fractional bend at the neck, the sort a man gives a magistrate or a well-liked aunt. What made her stare was that he did not appear to know he had done it. His face never changed. His hands never left the pole. He gave a scrappy white dog a small, sincere courtesy on a wet plank in the middle of nowhere, as naturally as breathing, and then straightened up and shoved off.

"You'll be the Fool, then," he said. "No, don't look surprised. You're the only thing that's come down this road on *purpose* in three hundred years."

The pole went down and made no splash. The ferry slid out onto the black water, and behind it there was no wake at all — the surface simply closed and forgot, the way a page closes on a finger.

She dug in the Bindle for coins, because that was what happened on ferries; she had learned that on the Divide, from a man who had counted hers twice.

"Put it away." He waved it off with the back of one hand, without looking. "I've not taken a coin for this crossing in — oh, long enough that I've forgotten the weight of one. What would I spend it on? There's nothing to buy here, and nowhere it'd get to before I did."

"Don't I owe you a coin?"

"You owe me nothing." The pole came up, went down. "First honest fare I've had in three centuries is you, sitting still long enough to be carried. That'll do."

The lanterns went by on either side of them, doubled in the water, silent. Somewhere off in the reeds a punt was moored with a coat folded on its seat. There were no insects. That was the thing her skin kept reaching for and not finding: a marsh at dusk with nothing whining over the water, nothing skating on it, nothing at all in the air but the smell of wet reed and tallow.

"Old Sallow," said the ferryman, to the middle distance, "since you're too polite to ask, and since the young lad who keeps the names will only want it written down proper." He poled on. "I've been nearly done dying for three hundred years, friend. Nearly. Right on the lip of it. Longest goodbye anyone ever drew out, and I never even got to the good part."

She turned round on the seat to look at him.

"You get so you can make a joke of it," he said. "You have to. It's that or the other thing."

"Nearly done dying — how long is *nearly*?"

"Three hundred years and counting, and every one of them I've thought, *this'll be the one.*" He smiled at the water. "You get fond of the nearly, in the end. Almost fond enough to keep it."

She thought about that for the length of a dozen lanterns going past.

"You must be so tired."

The pole paused. Not long — a beat and a half, hardly anything, the sort of hitch a man could pretend was a snag on the bottom if he wanted to.

"...Aye." He said it quietly. "That's the first anyone's said it plain in a long while. Tired's the word. Not sad, mind. Just — ready." He set the pole again and leaned on it. "There's a difference, and it took me a hundred years to learn it."

The far bank came up slowly out of the flatness: low houses on stilts, board-walks, a scatter of light.

"Twelve, they say you've turned loose out there," Old Sallow said. "Before us. Which makes ours the thirteenth card you've come for, and it's the thirteenth card in the deck." He considered this with no visible excitement whatsoever. "I'd call that tidy. Mind, we've had three hundred years to get superstitious in, and precious little else to spend the time on, so we're none of us the best judges."

At the prow, Pip had not moved. Old Sallow's eyes went to him again, and again something in his neck gave a fraction, and again he did not explain it, because he could not.

"Mortimer's expecting you," he said. "Been expecting you longer than the water's been still, if you want the truth of it. Go on up to him when you land. He'll not make you fight to reach him — that's not his way." The bank slid nearer. "He'll make the tea."

The ferry noised against the far bank with a soft wooden knock, the first sound the crossing had made.

*  *  *

The lanes on the far side were plank causeways running out over the reeds, strung between houses raised up on stilts, and every joint and post of them held a light.

People sat in the doorways. People stood at the windows. Some of them were tending flames that did not need tending — trimming a wick that had not moved in a century, cupping a hand round a flame that had never once been in danger — and doing it with the mild unembarrassed care of somebody stirring a pot that is already stirred.

None of them looked at her the way the rest of the Spread had looked at her.

She had got used to that look without noticing: the double-take, the hesitation while a stranger was fitted into some box or other and found not to fit any of them. The Assize had tried to file her. The Bastion had wanted to know her business. Here, nobody's face did any work at all. They nodded as she came along the boards — the small, unsurprised nod given to family who were expected on Tuesday and have arrived on Tuesday.

And every one of them, before they nodded at her — every single one, without exception, as reliably as the boards creaked — looked first at the dog.

A woman in a doorway with a child on her hip bowed from the waist. An old man mending a net bowed his head over the net. Two lads sitting with their legs over the edge of a walkway stopped their argument, got up, bowed, and sat down again to go on arguing. Nobody spoke to Pip. Nobody spoke about him. Pip accepted each one with the mild good grace of a dog who is used to it, and went on ahead with his nose down, checking the causeway for anything that might have improved since the last dog came this way.

"Bless the little dog," said the woman with the child, to the dog. "Bless him and the one who walks with him."

She said it to Pip. She did not, at any point, say it to the Fool.

"You've come, then," said somebody else, from a window. "We heard you would. We hear everything, out here — there's not a lot else to do but listen for footsteps."

"Don't mind us." An old fellow in a chair, both hands on a stick, entirely comfortable. "We're only waiting. We're rather good at it by now."

She kept walking, because there was nothing else to do with a welcome like that, and somewhere on the third causeway she caught herself waiting for a dry remark in her ear that did not come.

It had been quiet since the road. Not sulking-quiet; she knew the difference by now. Just quiet, the way a hand goes still on a table.

*  *  *

At the heart of the marsh stood a broad low house, open on every side to the reeds, with a fire in a stone ring under its roof and a kettle already on the fire.

There were chairs. That was the thing she saw before she saw the man: a great many chairs, none of them matching, ranged loose around the hearth in the numbers a household would need if it had spent three centuries expecting company and laying places for it — a good carved one, a wicker one gone soft, a milking stool, a bench with a folded blanket left on it in case anyone came in cold.

A tall figure in plain dark robes was crouched at the fire, feeding it. He rose the moment her boot touched the boards under the eaves, and turned, and his whole weathered face opened into a smile of such uncomplicated gladness that it took her a full second to remember what he was.

There was a scythe leaning against the doorpost, arm's reach from his hand.

He reached past it for another cup.

"There you are. *There* you are." He was already crossing the floor. "Oh, come in, come in — sit down, you've come such a long way — and Pip, you old marvel, look at you, not a grey hair on him after all this time—"

He went down on his knees on the boards with the cup still in his hand, and Pip trotted straight to him and shoved his whole head into the offered palm and submitted to having his ears fussed, tail going in slow heavy swings, entirely at ease.

From down there Mortimer looked up at her, and the gladness in his face turned out to have a floor under it. Something older. A tiredness so far past being a mood that it had become the shape of the man — in the hang of his shoulders, in the skin under his eyes, in the way he had lowered himself to the boards like somebody who has learned to take every offered opportunity to be nearer the ground.

"Forgive me. I don't get to be glad very often." He rubbed Pip's chest with his thumb. "I've been the last thing a great many people were ever glad to see. And then not allowed to be even that — do you understand? They come here to finish, and they cannot, and I have to sit with them in the not-finishing, year on year on year, and I cannot give them the one thing this whole office exists to give."

He got up. He poured the tea: three cups, out of a habit so old it did not notice itself, though there were two of them in the house and a dog. She saw him see the third cup after he had filled it, and set it down at the edge of the hearth without comment, where it steamed gently to nobody.

He handed hers over with both hands, steady as a table.

"But you've come now. So. Sit. Drink that. And when you're rested — not before — you and I are going to have a talk about what happens next." He lowered himself into the carved chair. "There's no hurry. There has never, in the history of this marsh, been a hurry."

She sat. The tea was scalding and very slightly stewed, and it was the best thing she had drunk in a week.

"You're not going to stop me?"

"*Stop* you?" He looked genuinely startled, and then he laughed — a real one, warm, badly out of practice. "Friend, I have been on my knees for three hundred years *praying* for you. You're not the thief at the door." He shook his head. "You're the one who's finally come to open it."

She turned the cup round in her hands.

"Why is everyone here so kind?"

"Because we've had nothing to do but practise it." He said it as a plain fact about the weather. "When there's no ending, there's no rush, and no rush is where kindness grows. It's the one crop this marsh has ever raised."

Outside, a long way off across the reeds, a woman was walking a causeway with a covered basin held against her hip, going somewhere with something warm.

"You've waited a long time."

"Longer than the words for it." He looked into the fire. "I stopped counting when the counting started to hurt. But you're here, and that's — that's the count over, isn't it. That's the sum done at last."

He drank his tea. She drank hers. The fire cracked, which was the only thing in the whole marsh permitted to be sudden.

"Go and walk the lanes first, if you like," he said. "Meet the folk. See what it is I've been keeping. I'd have you know what you're setting right before you set about it — that's only fair." He settled back. "I'll be here. I'm always here. It's the whole of the job."

*  *  *

Down one of the longer causeways stood a great raftered house with its shutters open to the reeds, and it was full of beds, and the beds were full.

It should have been dreadful. In a way it was: forty or fifty of them under one roof, in rows, some sitting up, some lying flat with their eyes on the rafters, and every single one of them dying, and none of them getting anywhere. There were card games in progress that had been in progress since before the Stall, played with hands that could not be dealt a fresh one. There was a chamber pot under a bed and a jug of water beside it and a child's drawing pinned to a beam. There was a woman at the far window who did not turn round.

And it was warm. That was the thing that undid her. Somebody was always holding somebody's hand — she counted four pairs from the doorway without trying — and the low talk under the rafters was the talk of a long night in a kitchen, and when a knot of the sprightlier ones caught sight of the white dog at her knee, the whole nearest corner of the room came alight.

"You'll be the one, then." A very old woman, propped on three pillows, bright as a robin. "Come here, let me look at you."

She came. She was looked at, thoroughly, by a pair of eyes that were unimpressed and delighted in exactly equal proportions.

"Hm. Smaller than I pictured the end of the world."

"Everything's smaller than she pictured it," said a man across the aisle, not looking up from his cards. "Been telling her two hundred years the ceiling's low, and she keeps cracking her head on the beam getting up. You'd think she'd have learned." He laid a card down. "You'd think any of us would have learned anything, all this time."

"Barrow and I have a wager going." The old woman said it with enormous relish, as one producing the family silver. "Which of us goes first, when the going finally starts. Laid it the night before the Stall. Two hundred and — how long, Barrow?"

"Long enough that the stakes are a loaf of bread neither baker's alive to bake."

"Longest game of nerves in the whole Spread, and the old goat still won't blink."

The Fool sat down on the edge of a bed that was offered to her by its occupant with a wave, and found that the room had made space around her in the manner of a room that has been hoping for a visitor.

"Doesn't the waiting frighten you?"

"Frighten?" Gammer Thist looked scandalised. "Bless you, no. It's not the ending that frightens a body. It's the *never* — the going-on and going-on with the door locked." She patted the Fool's wrist. "You're the key, duck. Keys don't frighten anyone."

"What will you do, after?"

"Same as anyone does after." Old Barrow considered his cards. "Nothing. Blessed, ordinary nothing. Do you know I have not been *nothing* in three hundred years?" He put down a knave with great deliberation. "I can't wait. First rest in an age."

The laugh went round the corner of the room and out along the rows: three beds down, then six, then somebody in the far dark who could not have heard the joke and laughed anyway on general principle. It was a real laugh, and it landed in the middle of all that patient dying like a candle set down in a dark room.

Then Gammer Thist's eyes went, for just a second, to the far window.

The woman there had not moved the whole time. She sat with her chair turned three-quarters from the room and her hands in her lap, watching the water where the ferry crossed. She was not asleep. She was watching very hard.

The room quieted. Nobody had signalled it; the noise simply came down, the way a household lowers itself around a sleeper.

"She's been ready the longest of any of us," said Gammer Thist, low. "Won't play cards. Won't take the wager. Just watches the crossing." She smoothed the blanket over her knees. "Some of us make jokes to pass a wait like this. Some of us haven't the jokes left." She looked up. "You'll be kind to her, when it comes. I know you will."

The Fool looked at the woman at the window for a while. Then she looked back at Gammer Thist and said the plainest thing she had.

"I'll settle the wager. I promise."

The old woman's face went off like a struck match.

"There's a good Fool. Written down and witnessed — Barrow, you heard it." She flapped a hand at the door. "Now off you go and make an honest woman of me before I lose my nerve, which at my age is a genuine risk."

On her way out she passed a door frame with a list of names chalked on it in four different hands, days of the week ruled off in columns, the older entries worn to a ghost and gone over again by somebody who had wanted them kept. A family rota. Four generations of one family, keeping watch in shifts over a great-grandmother who could not finish, and never once dropping a night between them.

And through the west window, far off across the reeds, a single candle burned in a distant sill, level and unhurried and consuming nothing — Corse Millbank's window, they told her, without her asking; his wife was in the long room among the waiting; he had lit it the night before the Stall and had been tending it ever since.

She could not mend one of them. Not the rota, not the candle, not the woman at the window. That was not what she had come to do, and knowing it did not help nearly as much as she had hoped.

*  *  *

On the causeway back toward the heart of the marsh a young man stepped out in front of her with a battered book under one arm.

He bowed to Pip first. Then he held out an unlit lantern.

"You're going back up to Mortimer? Good." He had the slightly breathless air of somebody who has rehearsed an interception. "Then do us a kindness on the way and take his round for him — just the one."

She took the lantern. It was tin, dented, warm at the handle from being carried under an arm.

"He'll have told you there's no hurry," said Tarn Loach, "and he'll be right. But there's a difference between no hurry and no *rest*, and that man has not sat down properly in three centuries. He'll not stop while there's a lantern to be carried." He shrugged, and it came out as almost an apology. "So carry it. And let him."

She lit it at the hearth-fire under Mortimer's eaves, which took some doing, because the wick was cold and there was no draught at all to help it along, and Mortimer watched her do it from his chair and did not offer to take over, which she understood was the largest thing he had done for himself in a long while.

Then she went out along the board-walk with the light swinging in her fist and Pip trotting at her hip, past a dozen doors that bowed to the dog, to the last house on the north line, where an old man lay too weak to keep his own flame.

He did not want conversation. She worked that out inside a minute; his eyes went to the lantern and stayed there, and when she set it in his sill he made a sound that was not a word. So she sat down on the stool beside the bed and let the wick take properly, and watched the little flame stand up and steady, and the old man watched it too, and after a while his breathing eased into something longer and quieter, and neither of them said anything at all.

That was the whole of the task.

It was, she suspected, sitting there with her hands loose between her knees, the whole of the office too.

When she came back along the boards, the hearth-house had changed shape. It took her a moment to see how. Mortimer was sitting all the way down — not perched forward with his forearms on his knees the way he had been all afternoon, ready to rise, but back in the carved chair with his head against the wood and his boots out in front of him and his hands loose in his lap, and his eyes closed.

Tarn Loach was at the causeway's foot to take the lantern back, and he had seen it too.

"There," he said. "He rested. First time in — I don't have a number for it, and I keep the numbers." He tapped the battered book under his arm; it was half a joke, and it was half not. "Thank you. He'd never have asked. None of them ever ask." He tucked the lantern into the crook of his elbow. "That's why somebody has to notice."

*  *  *

The last house before the heart of the marsh had its door open and its lamp trimmed low.

Inside, a woman sat at a bedside in a chair that had been pulled up to it so long ago that the boards were worn pale under its legs. She was hollow-eyed and bolt upright and she was holding the hand of the old man in the bed with both of hers.

"You're here." She did not get up. She did not let go, either — did not so much as loosen. "Grandda, look — the Fool's come. It's really the Fool." Her voice went up and thinned. "Didn't I say? Didn't I keep telling you to hold on just a little more?"

The old man in the bed was worn to almost nothing: a whisper of a man under a blanket, his wrists like reed stems. But his eyes came open and swung round, and they were sharp and dry as flint.

"You did, girl." Every word was chosen and set down separately, because he had breath for no more than that. "You've been telling me to hold on for a hundred years and more."

His eyes found the Fool, and there was something in them that was very nearly laughter.

"She's a wonderful girl. She'd hold the whole marsh here by the coat-tails if the marsh would let her." A pause for air. "That's rather the trouble."

Bettony Marsh's grip on his hand tightened. Not gently. She did not appear to know that she had done it.

"How long have you kept this vigil?"

"Since before I knew it was one." She smoothed the blanket flat over his chest with her free hand, and smoothed it again where it was already flat. "My mother sat here, and hers. I don't remember choosing it." She said it without self-pity, as a plain report. "You don't choose the ones you love. You just don't leave."

"Is he in pain?"

"Pain?" Gaffer Corlin's mouth moved. "No, lad. Worse than pain. *Boredom.*" He gathered himself. "Do you know how long three hundred years is when you're too tired to sit up and too stubborn to stop?" A rattle of a breath that was, unmistakably, amusement. "Don't answer. You'll find out soon enough, if you botch this."

The Fool looked at the two of them: the man who had been ready for a century, and the woman who had held him here, and the space between their hands where the thing that should have settled it had been locked shut since before either of them was born.

"You've held on so long. Both of you."

Bettony's face did something complicated and expensive.

"...I know. I know we have." She was quiet for a moment. "Everyone keeps looking at me like I ought to be glad you're here. And I am. I *am.*" Her thumb moved over the back of her grandfather's hand. "It's only — when it comes, it'll be him gone. Glad and gone are a hard pair to hold at once."

At the door, the old man's eyes followed her out.

"Go on, then. Go and do the thing." A long breath, spent carefully. "And don't you mind her when it's done. She'll be cross with you, and cross with me, and cross with the whole business." Something like satisfaction moved across the ruin of his face. "It's how she loves. Always has been. Let her."

*  *  *

Mortimer was on his feet when she came back under the eaves, and rested, and different for it.

Not grimmer. Readier. A man who had let his guest see the whole of what was at stake and would not now insult her by pretending it was small.

"You've seen them. Good." He crossed the floor. "Then you know what I keep, and why I've prayed for you, and why what I'm about to ask isn't cruelty. It's the opposite. It's respect."

He took the scythe down from the doorpost.

She had been half expecting the room to change when he did it, and it did not. He held it the way a farmer holds a tool: one hand at the balance, the other loose on the snath, the whole thing familiar and unfrightening and almost fond, a thing he had swung ten thousand times and set down ten thousand times and knew the weight of asleep.

"I'll not simply hand you the card." He turned it over, looking at the blade rather than at her. "I've thought about it — the gods know I've had the time to think about it — and I can't. An ending has to be *earned*, friend. It's the one dignity the office has left to give." He looked up. "If I laid down and let you walk over me, I'd be handing you a death, and death isn't a thing you're handed. It's a thing you meet."

"Anything less would be disrespect to the office," he said. "To them." A glance out at the lanes, at the long strings of level flames. "To me, if I'm honest, and I am, now, at the end."

He stepped out from under the eaves.

"I want this fought. I want it *proper.*" He looked back once, and the smile was there again, gentle and immense and entirely without menace. "Come and meet me on the shore when you're ready. There's no clock. There's never been a clock." A beat. "But when you come — come to *win*."

*  *  *

They had ringed the shore with candles.

It was the flattest place in the marsh, where the black water went out to nothing and the low grey sky came down to meet it with no join she could find, and somebody had carried the lights out there and set them in a wide circle on the wet sand: dozens of them, low and level and unguttering, every one of them doubled in the water below, so that the ring seemed to hang in the middle of the dark with nothing holding it up.

There were no benches. There was no crowd. There were no stakes driven into the sand for Blanks to rise from.

There was Mortimer, standing easy at the centre of it. There was the Fool, at the edge, with six petals at her belt and twelve cards in her coat and a rose that would not regrow one leaf of itself anywhere in this region. And there was Pip, who trotted out to the candlelight's edge, turned round twice, and sat, facing in, ears up, watching with the frank interest of a dog at a village fair — performing not one scrap of concern, because he never did.

Mortimer lowered himself and sat cross-legged on the sand. He laid the scythe across his knees and folded his hands over it.

"On your time. Only yours." He settled his shoulders. "I've all of mine, and always have."

She stood there a long while.

She could have gone back. The Waystation was four causeways behind her; she could have slept, and eaten, and taken the cards out of her coat and laid them on a rail and thought about them, and come back tomorrow, and he would have been sitting exactly there. That was the worst mercy anybody had ever done her. Nobody had ever made her *choose* the moment before.

She thought about the rota chalked on the door frame. She thought about a woman at a window watching a ferry she was not allowed to take.

Then she walked into the ring.

*  *  *

He rose in one unhurried motion, and the scythe came up with him, and the kindest man in the world became the hardest thing she had ever faced.

She learned the reach in the first three seconds, which is to say she learned it by being hit. He did not lunge. He simply turned at the hip and let the blade go out along its own arc, low and long and unhurried, and the tip of it caught her across the ribs at a distance she had already decided was safe. She went down on one knee in the wet sand with her whole side singing and got up, and he waited for her to get up, and then did it again from the other side, and she was not there for it.

That was the first thing she got right.

The scythe's reach was the whole language of the fight, and everything he did was a sentence in it. The long horizontal sweeps punished standing still; there was no such thing as ground you could simply *hold*. The overhead reap came down slowly enough to see and fast enough to hurt, and buried its point a foot into the sand, and if she moved the instant she saw it start she had one clean strike at his shoulder on the recovery, and if she moved a heartbeat later she had nothing at all.

And then, the third time she read a sweep and closed on it, he took a half-step backwards and *pulled* — drew the blade in toward himself along the arc instead of out through it — and the whole reach turned inside out, and the inside edge came back through the space she had just moved into, and it took her off her feet and dumped her flat in the shallows outside the ring.

She lay in four inches of black water and looked up at a sky with nothing in it.

"That's the one," Mortimer called, not unkindly, not moving from where he stood. "That's the one to live by. Learn it now, friend. I'll not stop showing it to you."

She got up. The water ran off her and the rings it should have made were not there.

She spent a petal — took the rose from her belt and closed her fist round it and felt the white leaf go, and the deep ache in her ribs go with it, and five left where six had been and nothing in this region that would ever give them back — and then she went at him again, and this time when he half-stepped she was not there to be pulled.

It went on a long time.

There were no tricks in it. That was what she kept finding, over and over, in the way a hand keeps finding a step that isn't there: there was no gimmick to spot, no rose wilting in a buttonhole, no tired one among nine, no lie in the machinery underneath. Twice she reached for the Hermit's lantern in the near pocket, because a raised light had solved four regions for her, and twice she got it up and blazing and it did nothing whatsoever except throw her own enormous shadow across the sand, because there was nothing hidden anywhere on that shore to reveal. The second time, she caught the look on Mortimer's face — a flicker of pure sympathy, as at a child trying a door that was never locked — and let the light go out, and did not reach for it again.

So it was hands and feet and the length of a stick against a farmer with a farmer's tool, and there was nothing else in the world.

And her breath began to come in the right places.

Every window she got she made. She dodged a sweep late and shallow, on purpose, and the world went slow and syrupy in her chest the way it had first gone on a plateau above the clouds, and she stepped through the arc as through a doorway and got two clean strikes into his flank before it closed. She baited the pull twice and slipped it, and on the second the point of the blade came round through her coat's hem and did nothing at all, and she was inside his measure with the Bindle already travelling.

He grunted. He gave ground. He came back.

And somewhere in the third or fourth exchange, wringing wet, with her arms full of sand and her heart going like a mill-race, she noticed the thing that changed the whole afternoon for her.

He was enjoying it.

It was not cruelty and it was not pride. It was in the set of his shoulders and in the almost-smile that never entirely left him, and in the small satisfied noise he made when she read the pull correctly for the fourth time and made him work for the fifth. Here was a man doing, well, and with his whole attention, the single thing his office had ever been for — doing it against somebody who could actually receive it, for the first and last time, at the very end of an unbearably long working life.

He fought her the way a great baker bakes a last loaf.

She stopped being frightened of him somewhere in there. She did not stop being frightened.

*  *  *

He stepped back out of measure, planted the scythe butt-down in the wet sand, and rested one hand on the top of the snath.

He was not staggering. He was not hurt in any way she could see, and he was certainly not out of breath, because he never had been. He had simply chosen to stop.

The candlelight steadied. The water went glass-still.

"Now," he said. "While we've breath between us. Because you've earned the *whole* of it, and I'll not send you back into that second half half-blind."

She stood in the middle of the ring with her chest heaving and her hair stuck to her forehead and knew, with an awful and perfect clarity, what was coming — the way a person knows a letter's contents from the handwriting on the outside.

"You know what I am," Mortimer said. "You've worked out, I think, most of what the rest are. Every card you've turned loose so far, you did it by letting something *end* — a show, a harvest, a verdict, a long march." He said it gently. "That's the shape of the whole journey. That's not a trick being played on you. That's just what it is."

She had done that sum. She had done it in a fog-grey courtroom four regions back and got as far as the last line of it and stopped, deliberately, and gone to sleep instead.

"The last card in the Great Reading is The World," he said. "When it turns, the Reading is done. And when the Reading is done, friend, the world is gathered up and dealt again. The Shuffle." He held her eyes across the candles. "Finishing the journey doesn't *save* the world. Finishing the journey *ends* it. That's what completing the Reading means."

The reeds did not move. Nothing on that shore moved at all.

"I'll not pretend otherwise. Nobody has ever loved a thing enough to pretend otherwise to it, and I love you too much to start."

He bent and picked the scythe up off the sand, gently, and held it across both hands, level, the way a thing is offered rather than raised.

"You are the ending, little Excuse. I am only the door."

The candles burned without moving. Far off, small and silver and perfectly still, the Axis sat at the middle of everything and the tiny figure at its heart went on turning.

And then, in her ear, the voice that had gone quiet on the road came back.

*He's right.*

There was no music in it. None of the dry lift, none of the little swerve, none of the sideways brightness it put on things when it did not wish to be asked a question. It came out flat and plain and unhidden, the way a voice comes out when its owner has stopped arranging it.

*Everything he just said is true, and I've known it was true since the cliff, and I let you find it out slow because I couldn't bear to be the one to say it. But he can say it, because he's Death, and Death doesn't flinch.*

A pause. She stood very still.

*So — there. No more sliding round it. The journey ends the world. It always did.* Quietly. *You've a right to know that standing up, and now you do.*

She was standing up. She had not, until that moment, understood that this had been arranged for her.

She looked at the candles, and at the water holding every one of them twice, and at a small white dog sitting at the edge of the light with his ears up, entirely unchanged, waiting for whatever came next in the way he had waited on the lip of a cliff. She thought about a stallholder who had swerved the word. She thought about nine hundred people standing up after three hundred years, and the sound their knees had made, and how she had laughed at it because it had been funny and because it had been the best thing she had ever heard.

Then she looked across the ring at the man who had told her, and not at the world, and not at anything else.

"Then I'll walk it with my eyes open."

Mortimer's whole face eased.

There is no better word for it. Three centuries came off it, all at once, the way a held weight comes off a shoulder.

"That's all I wanted for you." His voice had gone rough. "Not that you'd go on — that you'd go on *seeing*." He settled his grip on the scythe, and the almost-smile came back, and it was proud. "There's the difference between a mercy and a murder, and you just chose the right side of it."

He came up to guard.

"Then let's finish it. Both of us. Nothing hidden now, on either side." He nodded at her, once, colleague to colleague. "Come on, friend. Come and do it *right.*"

*  *  *

The second half was the same duel and an entirely different fight.

He was faster. The sweeps came harder and closer together; the pull tightened until the half-step warning was barely a shift of weight; the overhead reap stopped being an ending and became a beginning, chained into follow-ups that asked her for everything the first half had taught her and then a little more that it had not. He gave her nothing. He forgave her nothing. He had never once been trying to lose.

And it was — she would think about this for a long time afterwards, on roads and in inn beds and once, badly, at three in the morning in the Confluence — *easier*, in the only way that mattered. Not simpler. Lighter. There was nothing standing anywhere on that shore now that was not true, and it turned out that a great deal of what had been tiring her was the carrying of a thing she had refused to look at.

She was not tricking her way to an ending any more. She was choosing one, blow by blow, having been told to the last penny what it cost.

She spent three more petals. She took the reap on the Bindle and felt her arms go numb to the shoulder and gave ground for eleven paces before she found her feet again. She got the pull wrong once more, late in it, and paid for it in the shallows.

And then the opening came, and it was hers, and it was not given.

He had chained a sweep into the reap, and the sand under his forward boot was wetter than he had judged, and for a quarter of a heartbeat the blade hung at the top of its arc with all his weight committed under it. The world slowed. She saw the grain of the snath and a bead of water coming off the blade and hanging there with nothing to do. She stepped inside the arc, and put the Bindle in under his ribs with everything she had left in her, and the whole shore rang with it.

Mortimer did not fall like a man struck down.

He sank like a man setting down a weight he had carried past all reason: knees to the sand, and then the scythe laid flat beside him with a care that was almost tenderness, and then a breath going out of him, long and slow and complete — and at the very end of it, something that had been locked in his chest for three hundred years going out with it.

*  *  *

The office cracked.

It did not shatter. It came apart the way frost comes off a window in the first honest warmth: not a break, a *thawing* — the dark of the robes going ordinary, the authority draining out of the scythe until it was a farm tool lying on a beach, the great weight of being *Death* rather than a man lifting off him and away in the space of two breaths.

He knelt there in the candlelight, smaller and plainer and unbearably relieved, and turned his own hands over in front of his face and looked at them as though somebody had just given them to him.

Everyone in that marsh had called him Mortimer for three hundred years. He had answered to it, gently, a thousand thousand times, on a thousand thousand thresholds. He had never once been able to say it of himself.

Now he could.

"...Mortimer."

It came out barely aloud. He tested it, the way a man tests a word in a language he was born to and never permitted to speak.

"*I'm* Mortimer."

He said it again, steadier, and his eyes came up, and they were wide and quietly astonished at the sound of it in his own mouth.

"Do you know, in three hundred years — that's the first time I've ever got to say it." He laughed, or something in that country. "They all called me it. Every soul I ever sat with. And I never once got to say it *back.* Mortimer. My name is Mortimer."

The breath he took then was almost a laugh and almost a sob and landed as neither.

"Oh, that's — that's a good sound. That's a *good* sound."

He reached into the front of the robe, over the heart, and drew out the card.

Trump XIII. Passage: plain as a gravestone, no gilt, no border, warm from three hundred years of being carried against a man's chest. He did not flourish it. He took her hand in both of his, and folded the card into it, and closed her fingers over it, and held them there — both his hands around her one, on his knees in the sand, entirely sincere.

"Don't waste it."

That was all. He let go, and got to his feet — steady, whole, unhurried in a way that was suddenly a choice and not an office — and stood looking out at the lantern lanes he had kept for three centuries with the face of a man about to go home at last.

She stood there with the card in her fist. Then she opened her coat and took the Assize's Verdict out of the third pocket, the deep one, the *not yet* one, and put Passage in its place, and it went in as though the pocket had been cut for it.

She did not feel powerful. She felt like somebody carrying a full cup down a flight of stairs.

*  *  *

The change came the way the office had left: not with spectacle, but with a hush going out of the air.

She felt it on the causeway before she saw it — a pressure lifting off the whole flat country, the way a room changes when a held breath is let go somewhere in it. Nothing collapsed. Nothing cried out. Across the marsh, all at once and very quietly, the ones who had been ready began to be able to go.

In the great raftered house down the long causeway, the woman at the far window stopped watching.

That was all it was. Her hands stayed in her lap. Her chair stayed three-quarters turned from the room. Her watching simply, gently, ended, and the two who loved her came and closed her eyes, and one of them put a hand flat on her hair for a while and did not say anything.

It was the first true ending the Stillmarsh had held since before the Stall, and every person under that roof knew it, and it was right.

Nearer the door, Gammer Thist was waving both arms.

"There you are, my duck — quick, come here and *witness it*, the old goat's finally going first, I win, I *win* —" she was pink to the ears "— three hundred years I've held that wager and I've got him at last!"

Old Barrow lay back on his pillows with his cards squared on the blanket and an expression of enormous dignity and not one shred of hurry.

"Won fair," he said. "Loaf of bread, when either baker's reborn to bake it." He turned his head to the Fool, dry as dust. "Tell her she can gloat all she likes. I'll not be about to hear it, and that, madam, is the sweetest part of losing."

Gammer Thist laughed until she had to hold her own ribs, and Old Barrow's shoulders went, and half the room went with them.

It was the last laugh either of them got, and both of them plainly knew it, and they spent every penny of it on each other anyway.

*  *  *

The marsh emptied the way a lamp lowers: slowly, without drama, one light at a time.

She counted the days in meals, and in the burning-down of candles, which was itself the newest thing in the world. Nobody in the Stillmarsh had ever seen a candle burn down. On the second day people were coming out onto the causeways to look at pools of wax, and pointing, and calling each other over. Corse Millbank's flame had at last begun to consume its own tallow, and he stood under it with his arms folded and a great deal to say about the merchant who had sold it to him three hundred years ago on the understanding that it was quality goods.

The lanes thinned. The vigil-houses went quiet, room by room, and then bed by bed, and then whole rooms were only rooms.

And into the emptied hush came two things the marsh had not held since before the Stall.

The first was funerals. They were the world's first, and they were nothing whatever like the dread the word carried: modest, communal, closer to a barn-raising than a horror. Neighbours carried a neighbour out along the causeways by lantern-light, six at a time, changing shoulders at the corners. There were a few plain words at the water's edge, said by whoever knew the person best and could get through it. A cup went round. A name was said aloud, properly, once, and then let go, and everybody went back and ate an enormous amount of soup.

"We buried Sedge Fenner this morning," a man told her, leaning on a spade with his sleeves rolled and mud to the elbow. "First grave dug in the Stillmarsh in three hundred years. Hard work, digging." He looked at his own hands with real satisfaction. "Good work. Honest work. We'd forgotten."

The second thing was the cold.

It came in off the water on the third day, and it was a real cold, the kind with an end somewhere in it, and it went through the reeds and up the causeways and into every house on stilts in the marsh. The sky did not change; the sky had not changed since before anyone there was born and would not for a long while yet. But the willows along the north lanes went from green to a hard rusty gold in the space of two days, and then, one at a time and then in handfuls, they let their leaves go.

She stood on a causeway and watched the first of them come down onto the black water.

It landed. It floated. And then, unhurried, in no particular hurry about anything, the water carried it away.

"Cold's come in," said an old woman beside her, hugging herself and beaming. "Proper cold. Autumn, they're calling it — them as remember the word. My old bones ache, and I could weep for the joy of it."

*  *  *

On the reed road at the marsh's eastern edge, three Blanks came up out of the standing water.

Twos and a Three; a Cups, two Swords; blank-faced, tabarded, unhurried. It was not a hard fight and it did not take long, and she put the last of them down with the Bindle across the back of the knees and stepped back, already turning away, because she knew exactly what happened next. It had happened in front of her three hundred times since the meadow. The body slumps like a puppet set down kindly; the card peels off the tabard and goes up, tumbling end over end on a wind that is not there, away over the nearest ridge, to find somebody new to stand up and be.

The card did not go up.

It lay on the tabard where it had always been, and the colour went out of it — the reds and the black draining away into the grey of old newsprint — and the blank face under it settled into the reeds, and the whole arrangement lay there being nothing at all.

She stood over it for a long time with her heart going in a way the fight had not caused. Then she crouched, and turned the grey card over with one finger, and found nothing on the back of it either, and put it down again, and went on.

*  *  *

Some days on, the lamp at Bettony's door was out.

The bed was made. That was the first thing: a neatly made bed with the blanket squared at the corners and the pillow plumped, in a house where a bed had been occupied without a break for longer than the woman standing in it had been alive. The window was open to the turning cold. Bettony Marsh sat in the chair beside it with her hands in her lap, and her face was doing something that had no name and would not be given one.

"He went in his sleep. Three nights back." Her voice was perfectly steady, which was the worst of it. "Just — went. Gentle as you like, the way I always swore to him he would, if he'd only hold on till you came."

She looked at the empty bed.

"And he held on. He held on a hundred years past sense, because I asked him to. And then you came, and he didn't have to any more, and he *didn't.*"

She looked up. There was no accusation anywhere in her face. There was only arithmetic that would not come out.

"I keep waiting to feel one thing about it. Relief, or grief, the way you're meant to pick one." Her hands turned over in her lap. "And I can't. It's both, all the time, at once, and they won't sit still long enough to be either." Her chin came up. "He's *out.* Three hundred years and he's finally out, and I'm so glad I can hardly breathe."

A breath.

"And he's *gone.* And I'm so —"

And there it cracked, for the first time, only slightly, the way a plate cracks and holds.

"— I wanted him free more than I've ever wanted anything, and now he is, and the wanting's got nowhere to go."

She turned to the bed. On the pillow, tucked where nobody would find it until the sheets were changed, lay a folded scrap of paper.

It had been written in a shaky, deliberate hand, on some patient night, against exactly this morning. It had plainly been unfolded and refolded a hundred times in three days. Bettony picked it up and opened it and read it out anyway, because she could not stop.

"*Took you all long enough.*"

And it did precisely what the old man had meant it to do.

It broke her open in the good way and not the bad one. The laugh came up through the tears and carried half of them off with it — a wet, undignified, entirely involuntary bark of a laugh, hands over her mouth, shoulders going — the driest man in the marsh getting the last word in from three nights dead and spending it, deliberately, on making his granddaughter laugh at his own funeral, because he loved her and knew to the inch exactly how she loved.

She held both feelings at once, and neither of them gave way, and nothing tidied them.

"...Trust him." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, wet-eyed and almost smiling. "Trust him to have the last word. Three hundred years too tired to sit up, and he saves his breath for *that.*"

She folded the paper away, small, into her sleeve.

"Go on. I'll be all right." A shaky exhale. "Not today. But I'll be all right — which is more than any of us could say last week." She looked at the Fool properly. "Thank you. For the door. For letting him find it."

*  *  *

The ferry was where it had always been, poling the same black lanes past the same low lanterns, and it was not the same crossing, and both of them knew it.

Old Sallow brought the boat in and leaned on the pole and looked at her a while before he said anything.

"Well. You did it." He said it plainly, with no ceremony at all. "Marsh is emptying out around me by the day. Folk I've carried across nowhere in particular for three centuries, and now the far bank's a real bank and they're actually *landing* on it." He shook his head slowly. "Strange, watching them go. Good strange. The best strange there is."

He looked out over the water — at the lanterns doubling in it, at the leaves the willows had finally been allowed to drop, riding away downstream in twos and threes toward somewhere.

"I've been nearly done dying for three hundred years, friend. And do you know — now that it's on the table, now that a body can actually *finish* — I find I'm rather looking forward to it." He turned the pole in his hands. "Not rushing at it, mind. I've a ferry to keep a while yet; somebody's to carry the last of them over, and I'll not leave that to young Tarn on his own." He nodded, settling it with himself. "But when my turn comes round at the back of the line, where it belongs — I'll take it. Gladly. I've earned a good long nothing."

She stood on the jetty with a small white dog beside her and five petals at her belt and thirteen cards in her coat, and did not trust herself to say anything at all.

Old Sallow bowed to Pip, without deciding to, the way he always had.

Then he pushed off, unhurried, the pole going down without a splash, and called back to her across the still water the plainest and most patient thing the Stillmarsh had to say.

"No rush, mind. I've waited this long."

The ferry slid out across the doubled dusk, and the candles burned low and level in their hundreds, and the first leaves of the first autumn in three hundred years came down onto water that was, at last, allowed to carry them somewhere.

# TARROCK: THE FOOL'S READING

*Come in, then, and mind the step. There's a chair. There's always a chair — I keep one by
for company, though company is a thing I've had rather less of than you'd expect.*

*You'll want to know what this is. It's a reading. Not the sort where a woman in a striped
tent promises you a tall stranger and relieves you of your coin; the other sort. The sort
where somebody sits down badly frightened and asks the one question they've been carrying
about for years without once saying it aloud, and the cards come out honest, and what's in
them is in them.*

*The question was mine. I'll own that at the start, so nobody can accuse me later of
dealing from the bottom. It was a short question. I was three hundred years working up to
the asking of it.*

*And here's the other thing I'll say plainly, while there's still time to put your coat
back on: it ends. The story ends. I'll not walk you the whole long way and then produce
some clever business at the finish to pretend otherwise — that isn't a reading, that's a
swindle, and I was never a swindler, only a bit of a coward. It ends. And the ending is
not a punishment for having stayed to hear it. The ending is the point of the whole thing.*

*You'll not believe me for a good long while. I don't hold it against you; I didn't
believe it either, and I had considerably longer to get used to the idea.*

*So: twenty-one cards to turn, a small white dog, and a young woman who does not yet know
a single solitary thing — a condition I have come to regard as a kind of wealth. She has
no number. She belongs to no position in the spread and may be played at any point in the
game, which is the whole of her power and the whole of her trouble.*

*Let's begin where she did. In the dark, before dawn, with the sound of a card being
dealt.*

# Chapter One — The Leap

Before there was anything to see, there was a sound.

Cards, squared against a table. Once, twice — that small satisfied knock a deck makes
when a pair of hands tidies it, the sound of somebody deciding to be serious about
something. Then a pause long enough to be a held breath. Then the snap-slide of a single
card coming off the top.

**THE QUERENT.** *One more card. There's always one more card.*

Wind, faint, somewhere a long way off, as if heard through a wall.

Another card, closer. Almost gentle.

*There you are. Good. Wake up, little Excuse — you're wanted.*

*  *  *

She woke with her hand already curled around something, which is a strange way to arrive
anywhere.

The first thing was the sky: the colour of a robin's egg, going gold along one edge like a
page held too near a candle. The second was the smell of a fire that had stopped being a
fire — embers gone grey and shy, a last thread of smoke standing straight up and thinning
into nothing. The third was the grass against the side of her face, cold and damp and
extremely interested in getting into her ear.

The fourth thing was that she had no fourth thing. No yesterday to fetch up out of the
dark and set beside all this; no name on the tip of her tongue, and no ache of one
missing. Some people would have found that a horror. She lay still and found it only
enormous, the way a room is enormous before the furniture arrives.

Then she sat up, and the world had to make space for her, and it did.

She was a young woman, brown-skinned, built wiry the way a whip is wiry, all forearm and
no spare. Her hair had been cropped rough and short by somebody in a hurry with their own
knife, and it stood up on one side where she'd slept on it. She wore a traveller's motley
gone soft with weather — patched at the elbows in a colour that had never matched
anything, boots creased into the exact shape of feet that had walked. You would have liked
her, I think. Nearly everybody did, in the end, and the ones who didn't had reasons of
their own that were mostly about themselves.

In her right hand, loose, lay a white rose. Three petals, closed and modest, and the stem
without a single thorn on it, which she would not appreciate properly for some weeks.

A few feet off, in the wet grass, a small white dog was sitting and watching her.

He had the specific patience of a dog who has been awake some considerable time and is
not in any hurry about it — scrappy, short-legged, one ear up and one ear making its own
arrangements, his coat the grubby white of a thing that has been white and is doing its
best. He did not bark. He did not perform any of the pleasure he was plainly feeling.
When her eyes found him, his tail rose and came down once against the turf. *Thump.* A
formality, observed.

"Hello," she said.

Her own voice startled her more than the dog had.

*Thump*, said the tail again, and that was that; the terms were agreed, and neither of
them ever revisited them.

The bundle leaned against a stone: a quarterstaff of pale close-grained wood, worn dark at
the grip, with everything she owned knotted into a bundle at one end. She got her hand
round the shaft and lifted, and it came up at exactly the weight her arm had already
guessed. That was the first thing that ever frightened her, and it lasted about a
heartbeat — the discovery that her body knew several things her head had not been told.

She pinned the rose at her belt. She stood up. Somewhere behind her ribs a voice that was
not quite a voice, that arrived the way a good idea arrives, said:

*Go on, then. Have a look at where you've landed. I'll wait — I've had three hundred
years of practice at that particular skill.*

She turned in a slow circle, looking for whoever had said it, and found instead the
meadow.

It went up in a long green shoulder to the north and fell away everywhere else, and the
grass stood as high as her knees, and the wind — there *was* wind here; remember that,
because it matters more than anything else I could tell you this morning — came across it
in broad slow strokes, laying the grass down silver and letting it up green, over and
over, like a hand smoothing a bedsheet that will not lie flat.

Past the grass, in every direction, was an edge. Past the edge was cloud: a bright white
floor of it, level as a table, out to a horizon with nothing standing on it at all.

The cloud did not move. Not the way the grass moved. Not a wisp, not a curl. It lay there
beneath the world's lip like something painted, and the wind that was busy in her hair had
no opinion about it whatsoever.

She did not know enough yet to find that odd.

*  *  *

The meadow was not empty. It only looked empty until you crossed it.

There were fire-rings in the grass — a great many of them, once she knew to look,
scattered about in the untidy way of things that were never planned together. Some were
only a darker circle in the turf, the stones long since gone to moss. One still had half
a lean-to over it, the canvas rotted to a lace. One had a bent tin cup sitting upright, as
though set down between mouthfuls. And one, up near the shoulder of the hill, had a
walking-stick driven into the turf with its handle polished pale — exactly the way a
person leaves a stick when they mean to come straight back for it.

None of them were the same age. She knelt at one and found the ash gone to soil with grass
through it. She knelt at another, tucked under an overhang out of the weather, and found
the ash still black and faintly gritty, close enough to warm that she put her palm flat in
it to check. It was cold. But it had not been cold long.

*Every one of those belonged to somebody who woke up exactly where you did*, said the
voice in her ear. *Some of them stayed a night. Some of them stayed rather longer than
that, by the look of it. All of them left the same way, in the end.*

She counted, for a while, and then stopped counting, because the number stopped meaning
anything and started meaning something else.

Pip — she did not know his name yet, but he had one, and she would arrive at it the way
you arrive at a word you have always known — Pip had gone ahead to a patch of bare earth
by the largest ring and begun to dig. Not the aimless scuffing of a dog with time on his
hands. Proper work: front paws going like a mill-race, hind legs braced, soil arriving
between them in a steady brown fountain, tail a blur of professional joy.

She crouched and made a noise at him — nothing you could spell, a sort of encouraging
click — and he redoubled, then reversed out of the hole with something in his teeth and
brought it to her at a brisk trot, head high, the way you carry a thing to a person who is
going to be sensible about it.

A dog. A wooden one, whittled, no longer than her palm. Crude work — the legs were four
different lengths and the head was mostly optimism — one ear worn to a stub by handling,
the whole of it gone smooth and dark the way wood does when it's been turned over in
somebody's fingers on a great many evenings for a great many years.

It was not fine. It was somebody's own knife and somebody's own poor skill, and somebody
carving the one thing sitting beside them because they wanted a second copy to keep.

*Someone made that*, said the Querent. *For company, I'd guess. Or luck. Or because the
nights up here are long and a knife needs something to do. Keep it. It's not much. It's
yours now, all the same.*

She turned it over. "Who carved this?"

*Somebody with a knife, a dog, and too much night to fill. That's all I know, and it's
more than most keepsakes get.*

She looked at the real dog, who had already lost interest and was investigating the hole
for a possible second dog. "Can dogs really walk with every Fool?"

*So they say. So Pip says, in his way — which is to say, not a word, and every answer you
need.*

"Pip," she said, trying it. The ear that was up stayed up. The other one swivelled about
a quarter of the way and then thought better of it.

She wrapped the wooden dog in a corner of the bundle and knotted it in. "I'll keep it
safe."

*Good*, said the Querent, and there was something under the word, in the way there is
sometimes something under a floorboard. *That's rarer than you'd think, keeping a small
thing safe. Most people save that for the large ones.*

It stayed in the Bindle. I'll say no more about it than that; I have a long way to go
this evening and no wish whatever to arrive early.

*  *  *

The ground rose to a modest knoll, and on the knoll stood the only tree on the whole
plateau.

It was dead. Bare, black-branched, its bark peeling away in long dry curls like paper
left in the sun. Everything else up there was green and windblown and aggressively,
insistently alive — the grass, the gorse, the small yellow flowers she had no name for.
This one tree was not. Nothing grew in its shade. Nothing had for a long time.

Pip trotted up to it, gave it a long professional consideration, and elected — with
visible reluctance, in the manner of a dog making a difficult moral choice — not to.

She put her hand flat on the trunk. The bark came away in a curl under her thumb and she
caught it and set it back, guiltily, where it would not stay.

*That's the only thing up here that dies, you know.* The voice had come down a register;
it was not doing its dry little performance any more. *Everything else on this plateau
just... carries on. Very restful, if you don't think about it too hard.*

She waited. The wind moved through the dead branches with a sound like a page turning.

*This meadow sits outside the proper Reading, little Excuse — off the edge of the Spread,
where the cards don't quite reach. Down there, further on than you can see yet, nothing
has been allowed to end in three hundred years. Not one thing.* A pause. *That tree's the
odd one out. Or perhaps it's the only honest one. I go back and forth.*

She thought about it for a while, in the way of somebody with no other thoughts to get in
the way — which is, I have found, the most dangerous sort of thinking there is.

"It's the only one that got to finish," she said.

I did not have anything to say to that. Not then.

*Anyway*, said the Querent, after a moment. *Nothing to be done about a tree. Onward.*

*  *  *

The path — or at least the place where the grass had been walked thin often enough to
remember it — narrowed between two standing stones, and past them stood the first thing on
the whole plateau that had been *built*: a little wayside shrine, half swallowed in ivy.

She did not reach it, because three soldiers stood up out of the grass.

They came up on either side of the path, unhurried, the way men rise when they have been
waiting rather than hiding — person-shaped, person-sized, in faded tabards, and where
each face should have been there was a smooth blank oval, like an egg, like the back of a
spoon. On the chest of one, a large printed **2** and a cup. On the second, a **2** and a
sword. On the third, a **2** and a wand.

They came at her without a word, because they had no mouths to make one with.

I would like to tell you she was magnificent. She was not, particularly. She got the staff
up crossways because there was nothing else to do with her arms, and the first blow — the
Cups, coming in flat and slow — rang off the wood and jarred her back onto her heels, and
she made a noise she was embarrassed about for two full days afterwards.

But her hands had known the weight of the staff, and now her feet learned what her hands
already knew. She came out from under the second one sideways, in a roll that was mostly
falling over with ambition, and got up inside its reach where the swing was useless, and
put the butt of the Bindle into the middle of that blank white face.

*Blanks*, said the Querent, comfortably, from what sounded like a chair. *Twos — the
lowest rung there is, so don't take it personally if this feels easy. Hit them, don't get
hit back. It's not a more complicated plan than that for quite some time yet.*

"Not," she said, ducking, "helping."

It was on the third one that it happened. The Wands came in overhead, both hands, a big
committed swing you could see arriving from a fair way off — and something in her chest
took a breath and held it, and the whole morning slowed down like syrup off a spoon. She
had time. Absurd, unreasonable time: time to see the stitching along the hem of the
tabard, the grass bending under the soldier's forward boot, a bead of dew coming off a
stalk and hanging in the air with nothing to do. She stepped inside the swing the way you
step through a doorway.

Then the world let its breath out, and she was behind him, and he was not fast enough to
be anywhere.

Three strikes, clean, and it was done.

They did not die dramatically. They did not die at all, and it took her a while to work
out that this was the thing bothering her. Each one, when it had had enough, simply
slumped — folding at the knees and then the waist, settling into the grass like a puppet
set down kindly by somebody who meant to come back for it later.

And then the card came off. The stiff painted rectangle peeled away from each tabard,
unhurried, and went up. Not blown — *up*, tumbling end over end on a wind that was not
there, while the actual wind went on combing the grass the other way entirely, as
unbothered as a river by a fish.

She watched all three slide away over the ridge line. Beyond it the grass stirred and
three figures stood up out of it — blank-faced, empty-tabarded — and each card came down
and settled onto a chest and printed itself there, and the three of them walked off
briskly in three entirely different directions, on business of their own.

She stood in the flattened grass with her heart going and the staff hanging off one hand.

"Was that a person?"

*There they go*, said the Querent, which was not an answer, and I would like the record to
show that it was not a lie either. *Don't fret over where — they'll turn up again, same as
they always do. Nothing much ends around here just yet.* A beat. *You'll get used to the
shape of that, one way or another.*

*  *  *

The shrine, up close, was smaller than it had looked and older than it had any business
being: a stone basin, a low arch worn round at the edges by three centuries of hands she
would never meet, and a scatter of offerings gone so far to moss they were mostly moss
with an opinion. Somebody had left a thimble. Somebody, at some point, had left a boiled
sweet, and everybody since had sensibly left it alone.

She sat down on the step with her back against the arch, because her legs had begun
sending complaints, and found that the rose at her belt had gone strange.

It was moving. Not in the wind — against it. As she watched, one of the three closed
petals unfurled and opened out, taking its time, with a small private sound like a person
letting go of a breath they had been holding for politeness' sake.

*That's a Waystation*, said the Querent. *Rest here and the Rose comes back to you —
regrows entirely, no waiting about. There'll be one of these in every region worth the
name.* A pause, and then, more carefully than the rest of it: *Consider it the one promise
I intend to keep without complication.*

She rested. Pip lay down against her thigh with a grunt like a much larger dog and went to
sleep instantly, in the way of an animal for whom the world contains no outstanding items.

While he slept she went through the Bindle: a tin, a length of cord, a comb she had no use
for — and, sewn into the lining of her coat with a care the rest of the coat had never
received, three shallow pockets, one behind the other, each about the size of a playing
card.

All three were empty.

*Still nothing to slot in that little spread of yours*, said the Querent. *That changes
soon enough.*

"What goes in them?"

*Ah*, said the Querent, and let the silence get comfortable, and then said nothing else
at all.

*  *  *

Past the shrine, the meadow simply stopped.

Not a slope, not a scree, not a decent gradual argument between land and air — a long,
clean edge of turf, as though the plateau had been dealt from a deck and this was where
the card ended. She sat down with her boots over the drop, and Pip came and sat beside her
with his chest out, surveying the nothing like a landlord.

There was nothing to do out there but ask questions of the only voice available. So she
did, and I answered, and I would like it noted that I told her the truth every single
time. Not all of it. But every word of it true.

"Who are you?"

*Someone who's waited a long while to have someone to talk to. That'll have to do for
now.*

"Will you tell me eventually?"

*Perhaps. Ask me again somewhere further along, and further along after that. I'm not
being coy —* a considering pause *— well. I am. But there's a reason.*

She pulled up a stalk of grass and split it with her thumbnail. "What am I, exactly?"

*The Excuse, if you want the formal title. The one card that belongs to no position and
may go anywhere at all. Handy, that.* And then, warmer: *You're new, is what you are. No
past to speak of, none needed — every wonder you're about to have is going to be a
genuinely first one. I'd almost envy you, if envy were a thing I had time for.*

*And — between us — you're also the one holding all of this together right now, aren't
you. Every step, every choice.* A small dry laugh from nowhere in particular. *Funny how
that works. Don't let it go to your head.*

"I'll try not to."

She threw the split grass out over the edge and watched the wind take it sideways and up
until it was gone.

"What's wrong with the world?"

*The cards are stuck, little Excuse. Every last one of them, in place, since before your
grandmother's grandmother. Go and turn them loose.*

"Stuck how?"

*Stuck like a wheel that won't turn. A sun that won't set. A bell that won't stop ringing
the same hour. You'll see it plainly enough once you're down there.*

She thought about the dead tree with nothing growing under it. "Can I fix it?"

*"Fix" is a strong word for what's coming.* The voice did something then that she was not
experienced enough to catch, though she would remember it later, and so would I. *"Finish"
might serve better. Ask me again once you've actually met one of the stuck ones.*

The light had gone long and yellow while they talked. She sat with her arms round her
knees.

"Why me?"

*Because you're the one who got dealt.* Quite gently. *I don't make the rules of the
Reading. I only get to ask the questions.*

She nodded slowly, as if that were an answer, which it very nearly was.

*That's rather a lot of honesty for one afternoon*, said the Querent, brightening with
audible effort. *I'll dole the rest out as we go — in fairness, so will the world. Go on.
Have your look at the edge properly, this time.*

*  *  *

The sun came down the sky and turned the cloud-floor to a lake of gold, and the meadow
behind her went the colour of a struck match, and the wind dropped the way it does at the
end of a day, until the grass stood still and listening.

Pip got up. He trotted past her legs and out to the very lip — right out, where the turf
gave way to a hairline of soil and then to nothing at all — and stood with his ears up and
his tail going, looking out over the drop with the frank enthusiasm of a dog who has done
this exact thing more times than anybody has bothered to count.

"Pip," she said. "Come back from there."

He turned his head and gave her a look. It was not a challenging look. It was the look of
a colleague waiting on a slower colleague.

Then he jumped. Off, out, into open air, front paws first, tail still going, without one
flicker of hesitation — and gone.

She was on her feet without deciding to be, at the edge, staring down into gold cloud with
her heart slamming and absolutely no plan.

*That's rather the whole method, you know*, said the Querent, and it was the kindest the
voice had been all day. *He never looks down first. Neither should you.* A beat. *It's not
bravery, particularly. It's just what a Fool does when a Fool is done standing still.*

She stood there a long moment on the edge of the only world she had, with the last of the
light on her face.

Then she stepped off.

*  *  *

Falling has a beginning, which is terrible, and a middle, which — and I say this as
something that has watched a great many people find it out — is not.

The scream came out of her and blew away behind. The cloud took her, cold and wet and
blind, and then it was under her — and the whole world lay open below in the last of the
light like a hand of cards dealt face-up on a table too big to see the far side of.

A wheel of country, laid in a ring: fields; a black wood; a canyon splitting the green
like a dropped plate; grey stone stacked into a city; water. And running round the whole
of it a great pale road, with something moving along it very slowly.

At the centre, where the hub would be, was a point of still silver — an open eye of white
stone with no cloud over it and no shadow in it. She squinted, falling, her eyes
streaming. There was a figure at the middle of it. One figure, very small, turning.
Turning, and turning, and turning.

She could not see it clearly. She could not look away from it either. That was the first
time, and there would be a great many others, and I dreaded every single one.

*There it is*, said the Querent, over the roar. *The Spread, entire.*

Away north, a tower stood snapped clean through like a stick of chalk, the rubble of its
own falling hanging in the air around it, stopped, every stone waiting its turn.

*The Axis at its heart. The Spire still coming down after three hundred years of trying.*

Away east, gold fields blazed under a hard white sun that sat high and did not appear to
be going anywhere at all, though the sun over her own shoulder was three parts set.

*The Noonlands sitting under a sun that never once thought to set. It's beautiful and it's
wrong, and it has been both those things for longer than anyone still living can properly
remember.*

The ring of the world turned beneath her, growing, filling everything.

*Go and put that right, little Excuse. One card at a time.*

Then the details arrived all at once, the way they do — a crossroads, a hedge, a
chalk-white lane, a great heaped wagon of hay standing at the meeting of four roads,
improbable, enormous, and directly, generously beneath her.

She hit it with a *whump* that went off across the fields like a dropped mattress. Straw
went up in a wave and came down for a long time afterwards. She lay in the crater she had
made, entirely unbroken, wheezing, with a stalk of hay up her nose and one boot missing.

Pip was already sitting on the far side of the wain. He had not a single piece of straw
on him. He looked at her with the mild interest of a dog whose friend has taken an
unnecessarily complicated route.

Somewhere close, birds were singing. That is worth marking: in that field, at that
particular moment, there were birds and they were singing, and she would not hear anything
of the kind again for a good while — nor know, for a good while, that she was missing it.

She got her elbows under her. Beyond the hedge the sky was going the colour of a bruise,
and against it stood a great many poles with a great deal of striped canvas slung between
them, lit from within, gold and warm and enormous. Out of it came the sound of a crowd —
the settled shuffling murmur of people who have been sitting in the same seats for a very,
very long time.

And then, from somewhere close and delighted and entirely too loud:

"STEP RIGHT UP, step right up, don't mind the wagon, don't mind the straw, ladies and
gentlemen and whatever *that* was that just fell out of the sky —"

# Chapter Twenty-Two — The Shuffle

The world's first night was some hours old by then, and had not yet run out, and nobody
in the Hollows could stop looking at it.

They had come up out of their houses to do it — whole families, in coats, sitting on
their own garden walls with their heads tipped back like people at a very slow firework
display. Somebody had put a kettle on a brazier at the top of the third terrace and never
once got round to taking it off. Away east, where the Noonlands lay, the last of the
gold had gone out of the sky and left behind a blue so deep it had a grain to it, and in
that blue the stars stood out in their hundreds and their thousands, patient, already
arranged, having been up there waiting a good while before the sun could be persuaded to
get out of their way.

*They were early*, I remember somebody saying, on a wall, to nobody. *All that time. They
were up there being early.*

She came down through the terraces at a walk, with the dog trotting well out ahead.

The Hollows had been a graveyard when she first saw it, and it was a garden now: bank on
bank of turned earth gone over to flowers, every grave shut and green, the grass between
them shaggy with the untidy energy of ground that has nothing left in it wanting tending.
Late roses. Bindweed having a marvellous time. A carved trumpet lying flat in the grass
where somebody finished with it had laid it down, with two nights of dew on it, and a
snail.

Nobody stopped her. That is worth saying plainly, because everybody wanted to.

They had lined the terrace paths without arranging to — a hundred people, perhaps more,
standing at their gates in the dark with their hands full of things they had brought out
and then thought better of offering. A loaf. A folded blanket. A child on a hip, held up
to see. They did not cheer, and they did not call out, and not one of them stepped down
onto the path in front of her, because word had gone round the rim in the way word does,
and every soul on that hillside had arrived at the same understanding without a single
one of them being told: *this bit is hers.*

An old woman put a hand to her mouth as the Fool passed and took it away again, and said,
quite steadily, "Gods turn your card, love." And that was the loudest thing anybody said
in a mile.

Past the last terrace the flowers gave out and the workshops began: a straggle of low
sheds along the rim where four generations of one family had ground lenses, each of them
convinced that the next glass would be the one that finally showed the centre clearly.
Their doors stood open, their lamps were lit, and every telescope they owned had been
carried out and stood up on the gravel, twenty-odd of them, all pointing the same way —
and not one of their owners was looking through a single one. They had come out and stood
behind them with their arms folded, the way you do when you have spent your life building
a thing to see with and the thing you built it for has walked past your gate on its own
two feet.

Beyond the workshops, on the last rise, there was a place where the grass had been worn
away to bare earth in the shape of a pair of boots.

It was a very old shape. A man stood in it, and had, and this was the whole of his life.
He watched her come the way he had watched everything come for as long as anyone could
remember, which is to say without moving, and when she drew level with him Corvin Rathe
cleared his throat and did not manage anything else at all.

She stopped anyway. She always stopped.

"I'll look," she said. "I'll look properly. For you."

She could not have told you, when she said it, that there would be nobody to come back and
tell. Neither could he. He nodded and nodded and got his handkerchief out and made a great
production of his nose, and she went on, and behind her the rim-watcher stayed in the
shape his own feet had made, at exactly the distance he had always dared, and I loved him
for it and could not say so.

Then there was the wreath.

You have seen it from every horizon in this book. Up close it declined to be grand: a ring
of white stone laid low into the ground, a hand's-breadth proud of the turf, running away
left and right into the dark on both sides and coming round eventually to meet itself.
Weather never touched it. There was dew on the grass right up to it and none on the stone.
Past it the land went pale and level and dropped, in long shallow steps, toward a silver
point at the bottom of everything — and at that point, near enough now to be a person and
not a rumour, near enough to see the swing of a coat, a single figure turned, and turned,
and did not stop.

And just outside the line, off to one side of the path, with a packed bag at his feet, an
old man in a good coat was waiting for her.

*  *  *

He had walked from the Prestige. It had taken him eleven days and he had blisters he
intended to complain about for the rest of his life, which, as it turned out, was not
long, but nobody knew that yet and he would not have minded.

He had the programme out of his inside pocket and rolled in one fist, out of an old
habit that had outlived every reason for it, and he lifted it in greeting and then didn't
seem to know what to do with the arm.

"Walked a fair way to stand here," said Ferridge. "Longer than I've walked in three
hundred years, and my knees have opinions about it."

She had not seen him since a green with nothing on it. He was greyer. He had also, and
this took her a moment to place, got thinner in a way that had nothing to do with hardship
and everything to do with time having been let into the room.

"You came all this way."

"I did." He looked past her, down the pale steps, at the small turning figure, and then
back, and took a breath, and got on with the thing he had come eleven days to say.

"I'm not going to ask you not to. I know better than that now — you taught me, back at the
tent." The programme turned over once in his fist. "I only wanted to be somewhere you
could see me, when you went. So you'd know somebody came to wave you off."

She did not trust her voice at all, and so she used it, because she had learned in twenty
regions that the alternative was worse.

"Thank you for the evenings," she said. "All of them."

"Ah," said Ferridge, and had to look at the sky for a bit.

Pip went over and leaned on his leg, briefly, in the manner of a professional making a
call, and then came away again.

"Go on, then." The old man's chin came up. "Go and finish it." And then, because he was
who he was and had been sitting in the front row of something since before anybody's
grandmother: "...Best seat in the house, this. Turns out. Right at the edge, looking in."

She laughed. It came out wrong and she did not care.

He did not step across the line. He never did, and I want it on the record that nobody
asked him to and nobody would have stopped him — the ground beyond that stone would have
taken his weight the same as anyone's. He simply stood where he was with his bag at his
feet, and lifted one hand about as high as his shoulder, and gave her the smallest wave
there is: the kind you give a train.

*  *  *

Pip crossed first.

He did not hesitate, and he did not consult her, and he did not look back to see whether
she was coming. He went over the white stone at a businesslike trot with his ears up and
his tail going and his mind evidently on something down at the bottom, with the mild,
unbothered ease of a dog who has done this exact walk more times than anyone has bothered
to count.

She watched him go. Then she settled the Bindle across her shoulders, and stepped over
after him, and behind her ribs the voice that had been in her ear since a cliff in a high
meadow went on doing what it had always done, mid-thought, unhurried, the way it had gone
on through every mile of this —

*That's the last of the rim behind you now. Past here it's only the two of you and the one
who's been —*

White stone. Two feet on it, and four.

There is no wind. There has never been wind here; weather does not come to this ground
and never has. But there is no birdsong either, and no grass to move, and no voice
anywhere at all — not in the air, not behind her ribs, not in the place where a voice has
been for twenty regions and every mile between them. The silence has a shape. The shape
is the size of the thing that used to be in it.

Only one sound comes up from the bottom of the pale steps, and it is very faint, and she
has been half-hearing it since her first hour in the world without ever once stopping to
name it: a rhythm. Distant. Regular. A phrase of something, over and over, never getting
to the end of itself.

She goes down toward it.

*  *  *

The wreath is an amphitheatre.

It has no audience. It has never had one. The white stone falls away in long shallow
curves toward a floor at the heart of it, and the whole ring is faced with alcoves — set
into the inner wall, evenly spaced, one after another the whole way round, twenty-one of
them.

Each holds a card.

They stand face-up and at rest, each in its niche, each a hand's-breadth taller than she
is. The Magician's is the first she comes to. There is a man on it in a top hat with his
arms spread, and the paint is quiet, and nothing about it moves. She has seen the corrupt
version of this image on a hundred banners and a hundred coins and it has always been
doing something — turning, looping, catching, the same half-second of it forever. This one
is only a picture of a man who did a good trick once.

The Empress after it, throne and orchard, still. The Lovers, two people and a bridge, and
nobody choosing, because the choosing is done. The Hierophant with his bells hanging at
rest. Strength with her hand on a lion's jaw, and the lion asleep. The Chariot stopped.
The Emperor's stone chair. The Wheel, at no number at all. The Hanged Man, upside down and
comfortable about it. The Hermit's lamp burning low and not walking anywhere. Justice with
her scales level and empty.

She walks it. There is nothing to fight and nothing to pick up and nowhere to turn aside,
and the approach is long, and it is meant to be.

The High Priestess between her pillars, closed like a book that was read. Death on his
horse, going at a walk. Temperance pouring, and the cup underneath is full. The Devil,
and the chains on the two figures at his feet are loose enough to lift over a head, and
always were. The Tower, landed. The Star with her feet in the water. The Moon with the
fog off it and the road plain. Judgement's trumpet down. The Sun, going down.

Twenty cards, in card order round the ring — and yet, from where she is standing on the
long curve of the steps, they do not read in card order at all. From here, left to right,
they read: Magician. Empress. Lovers. Hierophant. Strength. Chariot. Emperor. Wheel.
Hanged Man. Hermit. Justice. High Priestess. Death. Temperance. Devil. Tower. Star. Moon.
Judgement. Sun.

Nothing says so. She does not notice. She is looking at the twenty-first alcove, which is
the only one with nothing in it.

It does not hum. It is not hollow the way a missing tooth is hollow. It is simply an empty
niche of white stone, swept clean, waiting the way a chair waits — and she goes past it
without understanding, and down the last of the steps, toward the floor.

*  *  *

The floor is pale stone, worn faintly circular.

Not scuffed. Worn — dished, the way a doorstep is dished, the way the middle of a flight
of stairs goes lower than its edges. Three hundred years of the same feet in the same
places have taken the stone down by half a thumb's width and polished what is left.

At the edge of it, set down long ago and never taken up again, lies a bindle.

She stops walking when she sees it. It is a quarterstaff of pale close-grained wood, worn
dark at the grip, with a bundle knotted at one end, and it is the twin of the one across
her shoulders in every particular except that the cloth has gone the colour of dust and
the knot has not been untied in longer than there are people to remember. Beside it, on
the stone, lies a single white rose. It is withered to paper. It has three petals, closed,
and it finished opening a very long time ago.

And in the middle of the floor, turning, is a person.

That is all. A person: travel-worn, wiry, in clothes cut not so differently from her own —
patched at the elbow, boots creased into the exact shape of feet that have walked. There
is no crown, no wreath of glory, no light coming off them. There is only a woman's height
and a body's weight going round and round on worn stone with the tired, exact economy of
someone doing a thing they could do in their sleep and have, many times.

The Dancer's head comes round, and their eyes find her, and they are not surprised.

"There you are," they say.

The turn takes their face away and brings it back.

"I felt you coming a long way off. You get so you can feel a Fool approaching. It's the
one thing that ever changes out here."

She does not answer. Her throat is not available.

"Three hundred years." The Dancer's breath goes out in something that is nearly a laugh
and is mostly just breath. "And I never once learned a second dance. You'd think I'd have
used the time. You'd think, of all the things a body could practise in three hundred
years — but no. The same steps. Round, and round, and round. It holds the world up,
apparently. Nobody ever tells you *how* it holds the world up. You just do it, and it
does."

Pip trots out onto the floor, sits down in the middle of everything with his chest out,
and begins to wag.

Something happens in the tired face. It is not large. It is the thing that happens to a
window when somebody inside lights a lamp.

"Pip," says the Dancer.

It is the only name they say aloud, and they give it to the dog like a gift.

"Look at you. Not a grey hair on you, after all this. You found the next one, then. Good
lad. You always do." The turn carries them round; the voice comes back gentler, and it is
aimed at her now. "He walked in with me, too, once. Sat right about there. Off he went
again, after. He doesn't stay. He *goes with.* Don't ask him why — he's never told anyone,
and he's not about to start with you."

The Fool finds a voice somewhere and it comes out smaller than she wants.

"Who are you?"

"No name for you. Not yet — maybe not ever." The step never falters. "Bound cards don't
get to hand their name round; you'll have worked that much out by now." A turn. "Ask the
dog later, if you like. He knew me."

She looks at the bindle at the floor's edge. She looks at the rose beside it with its
three closed petals. She has been doing arithmetic since the Prestige and she does not
need any more numbers.

"I don't want to fight you."

"I know." The Dancer says it without weight, the way you agree about the weather. "Neither
did the one before me want to fight the one before them. It's not about wanting. It's
about *earning* — the same as it always is with us. You'll fight me, and you'll be right
to."

She stands on the worn stone with her hands loose. Twenty cards lie flat against her ribs
in three sewn pockets and a coat lining that has been let out twice. There is nothing in
any of them that is any use here and she knows it.

"You look tired," she says.

The almost-laugh again.

"Tired. There's an honest word." The turn. "First honest word said to me in three
centuries, and it's the truest one. Aye. I'm tired. I've been tired since before your
grandmother's grandmother." The turn. "Come and do something about it."

They come round once more, and there is nothing in their hands, and nothing settles onto
their shoulders, and no alcove flares anywhere on the whole ring, because there is not one
thing left in the world for them to borrow. Every card is home. The Dancer notices this
the way you notice a draught stopping.

"Well," they say, to themselves, with something almost like respect. "You did the lot,
didn't you."

Then: "On your time. Only yours. I've all of mine — I've had nothing *but* mine, for
longer than the word means anything. When you're ready, then. And not before."

She takes the Bindle off her shoulders.

*  *  *

It is the cleanest fight of her life and there is nothing in it but the two of them.

No doubles. No borrowed office, no collapsing floor, no scales storing up her own blows to
give back, no chorus, no machinery, no fog. There is a circle of worn stone and two people
who belong to no position in the spread, and one small white dog sitting at the edge of it
with his ears up, watching the way he watched a ring of candles in a marsh.

The Dancer fights the way she fights.

She understands it in the first exchange and it goes through her like cold water. That is
her own high block, the one she found by accident on a wire and has used a thousand times
since. That is her step-and-turn out of a committed swing. That is the small ugly
shoulder-first shrug she does when she has left it too late, which she has never seen from
the outside and did not know looked like that. The Dancer's Bindle comes round in the
short flat arc she uses in doorways and the long lazy one she uses when there is room, and
there is nothing borrowed anywhere in it, and nothing false.

In a marsh a little while ago she fought a fog wearing her own face and every one of her
own powers, reversed, and it had been horrible and it had been a lie. This is not a lie.
This is the same trick done sincerely, and it is worse, and it is better.

She takes a strike on the ribs that folds her sideways and gets clear on instinct. She
spends a petal, kneeling, and the rose at her belt gives one up with the small private
sound of a held breath let go — eight petals, and she does not count them, and she will
spend four before the end.

The Dancer does not taunt. The Dancer does not press an advantage that was given by
accident. Twice, when she is down, the turning simply carries them away and round and back
again, giving her the width of the floor to stand up in, and each time it is worse than
being hit.

She learns them. It takes a long time and it costs her, and what she learns is that they
are her exactly, only three hundred years more so: every habit she has, worn down to the
grain, done with no wasted motion at all. Her own vocabulary, spoken by somebody fluent.

And then she leaves a window.

It is not a large one — a half-beat too long at the end of a swing, the sort of thing
nobody has ever punished her for. The Dancer's head comes round. And the world goes slow
and syrupy, and the air thickens, and she watches the woman who has been dancing since
before her grandmother's grandmother step through the gap in her guard the way you step
through a doorway, with all the time in creation to do it, and the whole of it is her own:
her held breath, her ridiculous unearned second, the one gift she has had since a meadow
on a plateau, turned round and given back to her by its previous owner.

She is on the stone with her ears ringing and no clear memory of arriving there.

She gets up.

After that it is only the two of them going round, and there stops being any difference
between the fight and the dance, and it goes on a great deal longer than either of them
has any business managing.

The last opening is hers.

It comes at the end of a long exchange, out of nothing, on a turn the Dancer has made
ten million times and makes a fraction wide — and something in her chest takes a breath
and holds it, and the world slows for the last time it will ever slow for her, and she has
absurd, unreasonable time. Time to see the grain in the worn stone. A bead of sweat coming
off a jaw and hanging in the air with nothing to do. Three hundred years of the same steps
laid down in the dish of the floor under both their feet.

She steps inside the turn and puts the butt of the Bindle in under the ribs, and the world
lets its breath out.

The Dancer does not fall.

They come to a stop.

That is the whole of it, and it takes a moment for either of them to understand what has
happened. The turn simply does not continue. One foot comes down where the next step was
going to be and stays there, flat on the pale stone, taking a body's weight, going nowhere
at all. The other comes to meet it. And a person who has not been permitted to stand still
since before anybody alive was born is standing still, with both feet under them, in the
middle of a floor they have worn a dish into.

Then two things happen at once.

The rhythm — the distant, half-heard, never-arriving phrase that has come up off this
place from every horizon in the world since her first hour in it, that she heard through
cloud while she was falling and never once thought to wonder about — goes on one beat
further than it has ever gone before. And it lands. One long line of it, carried three
hundred years without ever being allowed its last note, coming down at last onto the note
it was always going toward, and holding there, and stopping.

And over the top of it, out of the whole silver bowl of the sky and the stone and the
ground under everything, comes the sound of a card turning over.

She has heard it twenty times. She has heard it in a carnival's underworks and a marsh's
candlelight and on a hillside with the stars coming back. It has never once sounded like
this. This is the same sound made by something the size of the world, unhurried, absolute,
one enormous rectangle coming off its face and going over onto its back, and the noise of
it goes out across the Spread and is heard in every region there is, and in the Hollows a
hundred people on garden walls stand up.

*  *  *

The office cracks.

It does not shatter. It thaws — the weight of being *The World* rather than a person
coming off in long slow sheets, the way ice comes off a roof in the first warm hour, and
what is underneath is not glorious in the slightest. It is a tired woman in a patched
coat, breathing hard, looking at her own hands.

Something comes up under the crack, the way a held breath finally lets go. A name.

She does not say it to the Fool.

She goes down on one knee — slowly, with a small grunt, a body remembering the mechanism —
and Pip is already there, because Pip has been waiting for this part, and she takes his
scruffy head in both hands and leans in and puts her mouth against the side of his ear.

And she says her name to the dog.

Only to the dog. It is too low for the Fool, standing eight feet away with the Bindle
still in her hand. It is too low for the stone and the sky. It is too low for me, and I
have been listening to everything that has ever happened here since before there was
anybody to listen to, and I did not hear it, and I never have, and I never will. Whatever
the Dancer's name was, whoever she had been before she was The World, it is spoken once,
into a white dog's ear, and nobody else gets it, because it was never ours to hear.

Pip wags.

That is all. The tail goes, sweeping the pale stone, *whud, whud, whud*, and the woman who
was the world a minute ago laughs out loud — a proper wet ugly laugh, both hands still
either side of a dog's face — at whatever it is a tail can say back.

"Aye," she says. "That's me. That's still me. After all this. Fancy that."

She gets up. She does it steadily and without hurry, and the difference between this and
every other movement she has made in three hundred years is that this time not hurrying is
a thing she has chosen.

She looks at the Fool properly for the first time. Showman to showman. Fool to Fool.

"Right, then. Before we finish it, there's the one thing I have to ask you, same as it was
asked of me, my time round. And you have to answer it standing up."

The Fool stands up in every way there is.

"Do you know what turning it does?"

She thinks of a ring of candles in a marsh, and a man with a scythe across his knees who
would not lie to her even when lying would have been a kindness, and a cup of tea going
cold in her hands while he said it plainly, twice, so she could not mistake it.

"Yes," she says. "Death told me. I'm ready."

The Dancer nods, slowly, and something in her face lets go.

"Good. He told you. He told me too, my time round — different door, same truth. Kindest
man there ever was, at telling it." She breathes out. "I'm glad it was him you got."

*  *  *

And that is where I came back.

I had better do this properly, since it is the last chance I shall get.

You have been reading a book with an *I* in it for a very long while now. It has been
saying *I would like to tell you she was magnificent*, and *I have watched this happen
twenty-one times*, and *I answered, and I would like it noted that I told her the truth
every single time.* You will have made some sort of arrangement with that *I* in your own
head — a voice, an old party in a chair, somebody who knew her and was fond of her and had
got to the end of the story before we started. That was all correct. It simply was not
very much of it.

Nobody ever asks a voice what it is the voice *of*. She never did either, not once, in a
hundred regions of asking me everything else. And I had three hundred years to work out
how I would say it when the moment came, and when the moment came I found I could only do
it the way I do everything, which is sideways, and slowly, and starting with the cards.

So: the alcoves gave up their cards.

Twenty of them lifted out of their niches all round the ring at once, unhurried, each
one turning in the air as it came, and drifted down onto the pale floor between the two
Fools and laid themselves out flat on the stone. Not in card order. In *hers* — left to
right, the first region she ever woke to the last one before this, the Magician away at
one end where a carnival had let its breath out, and the Sun at the other where the sky
had finally been allowed to do something. Twenty cards, dealt across a year and a
lifetime, laid out on the floor of the world.

The twenty-first did not come down with the others. It had been on the floor since before
any of this started, face-down, under a dancer's feet.

She stood and looked at the row of them and did not understand what she was looking at,
and then did.

*There*, I said. *Your reading, little Excuse.*

It was the first thing I had said since the wreath line, and my own voice went through her
like a hand on the back of the neck. She sat down on the stone. She did not decide to; her
legs did it.

*You've been dealing it since the cliff — one card a region, in your own order, and no one
before you ever dealt it quite the same. I couldn't read it to you until it was whole.
It's whole now. So let me read you three.*

The card at the far left glowed a little brighter than the rest, the way a coal does when
you look at it.

*First card — the Magician. So your world woke to a trick laid bare, and learned in the
same breath that a wonder shown honestly is a wonder still. That's the dawn you've dealt
the next one: a waking that is its own first act of making.*

She put her hand flat on the stone beside it, which is the closest a person can come to
touching a thing they know they are not permitted to pick up.

The eleventh card, exactly in the middle of the row, took up the light.

*Eleventh card, the heart of it — Justice. So at your centre you were a weigher of things
left too long unsettled. You carried the scales into the very middle of your own road, and
you did not tip them for anyone. I saw.* And I let that sit, because it was true, and
because I had waited a long time to say it to her. *I always see the middle card
clearest.*

"You saw all of it," she said. Not a question. She had been putting things together for
some while.

*I saw all of it.*

And then the last one but one, away at the right-hand end, the card with the sun on it
going down.

*Last card before this one — the Sun. So the world's last lesson was daybreak — that even
an ending can come warm, and gold, and feel more like a morning than a night. The
gathering will carry that light out with it.* I looked at it a moment longer than I needed
to. *A good last card, that. One of the kindest.*

She sat with her hands in her lap and the whole of her own journey lying on the floor in
front of her in twenty pictures, and she said, "It's a nice reading, isn't it."

*It is a lovely reading. It is the best reading I have ever been dealt, and I have been
dealt a great many.*

That went past her at first. I watched it go past her, and then I watched it come back.

And because there is a particular cruelty in making somebody get there entirely on their
own, I helped her the last step of the way, and it cost me more than the fight had cost
her.

*You told the lantern-keeper you were looking for whatever was still waiting to be found.*
I had carried that answer up a cold mountain and down the other side and everywhere else
she had gone since, and never once said what it did to me. *It was me, little Excuse. It
was always me.*

*  *  *

The view lifted.

I cannot describe what I did next in any way that will do, because there is no word in
your language or hers for a thing looking at itself. But she stood up, and she turned
round, and from the floor of the wreath the whole rim of the world stood open — the way it
had stood open once before, from underneath a cloud, with her falling through it and
screaming.

Every region of it awake. The Prestige with its roads open and its wagons out on them. The
Bower heavy and gathered in. A bridge with lamps on it over a canyon. Bells. A ring-road
with nothing marching on it. Grey streets with their gates open. A wheel going round on
no schedule but its own. A forest turning its leaves over. Stars on a mountain. Empty
benches in a court. Mist gone from between two rows of pillars. A marsh full of lanterns
and lit windows and, at last, of graves. Two rivers running out to the sea together. A
gilded pit with people on the stairs, going both ways. A tower on the ground. Wish-lights
on water. A marsh with no fog in it. A garden that used to be a graveyard. And away east,
gold fields lying under the dark, cooling, with dew on them for the first time in three
hundred years.

All of it alive. All of it more alive than it had been since before anybody's grandmother.
And all of it — she felt this before she understood it, and so did you, I expect, some
chapters ago — turned inward. Attending. Every horizon of it leaning very slightly toward
the one still point it had been circling since before memory, and listening.

*You asked me once, on the cliff, who I was*, I said. *I told you I was someone who'd
waited a long while to have someone to talk to. That was true. It just wasn't* all *of it,
and you've earned all of it now — every card of it, dealt and turned and read.*

*Look out there. Go on. Every region you woke. All of it, listening.*

She looked.

*I'm not a voice in your ear, little Excuse. I never was. I'm the thing your ear is*
inside of. *The Spread. The world. All of it.*

*A reading gets dealt* for *someone — for the one who sat down and asked the question. And
this reading, this whole three-hundred-year reading, was always being dealt for me. About
me.* How does my story end? *That's the only question a querent ever asks. It's the only
one I've ever asked.*

I have said elsewhere in this account that I never lied to her, and I will hold to it, and
I will also say now what I was doing instead. Every time she asked me who I was, I told
her the truth about the size of the loneliness and nothing whatever about the shape of it.
I said *someone who's waited a long while.* I said *I don't make the rules of the Reading;
I only get to ask the questions.* I said, on the very first evening, at the very first
edge, that *fix* was a strong word for what was coming and *finish* might serve better,
and she took that for grammar.

And you — you I welcomed in at the start and offered a chair to, and told plainly that it
ends, and you thought I was being ominous about somebody else.

*And I was* afraid *of the answer*, I said. *That's the truth of the Stall — not a curse,
not a catastrophe, no villain anywhere in it. Three hundred years ago I saw the last card
coming and I flinched. I refused to hear how it ended. And a reading whose querent has
stopped listening can't be dealt — no Fool could be dealt into it — so everything just…
stopped. One card from the end. Golden, and airless, and pretending.*

She was crying by then, which she did rarely and badly.

*You are here because I finally found the nerve to ask again. To sit back down at my own
table and say the question out loud and* mean *it this time. That's what you are, little
Excuse. You're not my saviour. You were never that. You're my* answer *— the one I was too
frightened to hear, walked all the way up to me on your own two feet, so that this time I
couldn't look away.*

The freed Fool stood at the edge of the floor with her old bindle at her boots and did not
interrupt. She had heard this before, from the other side of it, three hundred years ago,
and she had the good manners of somebody attending a thing that is not about her.

*The world has been asking how its story ends.* And I had to stop for a moment, having
come all this way. *You're how it ends. And I asked for you. Standing up. At last.*

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and made a poor job of it and said, "That's
why the tree was allowed to die."

*Yes*, I said. *That was the only honest thing I had left up there. You put your hand on
it and told me it was the only one that got to finish, on your first afternoon alive, and
I have thought about it every day since.*

Then I said one more thing to her, before the card. It was short. It was the only entirely
selfish sentence I have spoken in three hundred years, and it was the one thing in this
whole long account that was never meant for anybody but her.

I am not going to write it down. What I said to her then was for her, and I am keeping it.

*  *  *

The Dancer stepped back off the middle of the floor.

She did it plainly, with no ceremony at all, two ordinary paces backward onto the flat
stone at the edge — and the centre of the world was empty for the first time in three
hundred years, and nothing fell, and nothing cracked, and the sky went on holding itself
up perfectly well without anybody standing in the middle of it turning round.

On the pale stone where she had danced, in the dish her own feet had worn, lay one card,
face-down.

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that the next four seconds would not say
better.

The Fool walked out to the middle of the floor. She stood over it a moment with her
shadow on it, and I watched her not hesitate, and then she crouched down and put two
fingers on the corner of the card and turned it over.

*  *  *

There was no fire. There was never going to be fire; I want that understood, because
people always expect fire, and it would have been such a poor way to end a thing that had
been done as carefully as this. It was a gathering-up. It was the world collected the way
a dealer squares a spread at the end of a hand — gently, without grief, unhurried, because
the hand was well played and it is time.

And it went in her order, because it was her reading, and a reading is not dealt twice.

The Prestige first, because the Prestige was first: three hundred years of striped canvas
coming down in one long fold across the fields, the way it had come down once already
under her hands, only bigger, and quieter, and for good.

*Road runs out of road at last. Best show we ever ran — and, wouldn't you know it, the
very last. Goodnight, all. Goodnight.*

Then the Bower, letting its orchards down: every branch in every row easing its last
windfall into the deep grass at once, gently, all together, with a sound like a very large
animal lying down.

*Everything ripened, everything gathered, nothing wasted at the end of it. There, love.
The garden could bear to be cut down after all.*

Then the Divide, and its bridge closed like a book with the ribbon still in it.

*Married at last, and folded up the same week. But married. It counts. It always counted.*

Then the Chantry, where every bell in the valley swung once more, all together, and let
the note go instead of holding it.

*Every song ends on a note it kept back for the purpose. That was this one. Amen, and
goodnight.*

Then the Maw, its high crags lowering their heads into the dark like beasts folding down
to sleep.

*Gently, now. It was always strongest done gently — she taught the whole mountain that.
Let the lion lie down first.*

After that it began to come faster, the way it does when a hand is being squared and the
dealer's fingers know their business.

The Longroad emptied mile by mile, banners folded, dust settling for good — *every road's
a circle if you march it long enough. This one finally got where it was going. At ease —
all of you. At ease* — and the Bastion's gates stood open as its grey streets dimmed, and
no gong called anybody home — *one last entry, in a fair hand: seen, approved, and — at
long last — amended. The books close balanced* — and in the Wheelhouse the great wheel
went round one more time, slow, and came to rest on no number at all.

*House closes. All bets settled, every last one — and fairly, which was the miracle. Cash
out, my dears. Well played.*

The Gallowwood turned over one final time, unhurried, all its leaves coming down every way
at once and not one of them wrong — *let go, let go — that was always the whole trick.
Fancy a forest learning it. Fancy all of us* — and over the Dim the stars went out last of
all, after every lamp beneath them had gone first.

*One lantern led the whole dark home in the end. Put it out now, love. Nobody's lost.*

The Assize went dark docket by docket, every bench empty, every case closed — *verdict for
the world, in the matter of the world. No appeal wanted. None filed* — and the Veil's mist
folded back between its pillars like a cover closing on a finished book.

*Shelve the last of it, sisters. A secret kept three hundred years is a secret kept — the
Reading heard it anyway. Sleep well. Dream.*

And then it was the marsh, and I slowed down, because I could, and because I wanted to.

The Stillmarsh went out lantern by lantern, one at a time, down lanes that had finally,
finally led somewhere: the ferry lights, the sill candles, the lamps in the low windows of
people who had spent three hundred years being the kindest and least-visited folk in the
world and had lately, at long last, been extremely busy. There was a light on the water
that took its time. I let it.

*No rush, he always said. There never was. Sleep now, marsh — you of all places have
earned the nothing.*

Then the Confluence, where the two rivers ran out to the sea together and the cups were
poured empty at last, rinsed, and set down.

*Finished — the one word the whole delta couldn't say. It says it beautifully, for a
beginner.*

The Undervault dimmed in its gilded pit, and as it closed some of its people were walking
up out of it and some were walking back down, exactly as they always would have.

*Some climbed out. Some chose the dark again. The card keeps both, and asks no one to
choose twice.*

The Spire folded up smooth — the quiet ground where the tower had finally landed, cairn
and all.

*It got to land. Three hundred years of falling, and it got to land. Let that be the whole
epitaph.*

The Mere's wish-lights dimmed together on the water, and the star that had no business
being that low went last of all.

*Every wish landed somewhere in the end — even the ones that only wished for company.
Goodnight, little lights. You were hoped on, and it held.*

The Mirrormarsh folded clear, honest to its last reed, and the fog did not come back.

*No more masks, no more borrowed faces — everyone went home as themselves. Hardest trick
in the deck, that. Sleep plain, love. Sleep true.*

The Hollows folded in full bloom — not one grave open, not one soul left waiting on the
terraces, a hundred garden walls and a kettle still going on a brazier at the top of the
third one.

*Everybody called, everybody answered, everybody home. The trumpet can rest in the grass
now. It earned the quiet twice over.*

And then there was only the east.

I took my time over the Noonlands. I had been three centuries not being allowed to, and I
was not going to be hurried at the finish.

The gold went out of the fields the way it had gone out of them once already, a few hours
before — running west to east across the whole enormous breadth of that country, over the
stubble and the stooks and the long roads between them, over a festival ground with the
poles still up and the bunting still on them, over a hundred thousand acres of wheat that
had stood under a nailed noon since before anybody's grandmother and had learned, only
this evening, what shadows were for. It went down. It kept going down. And the fields
went blue, and then dark, and then folded, under a sun that had learned, at the very end,
how to set.

*The first sunset was worth three hundred years of noon. This is the second one. Turns out
it's just as good.* And then, because there was no one left out there to hear it but me:
*Down you go, then. Down you go.*

And that was the world.

*  *  *

What was left was the wreath, and the two Fools on it, and the dog.

The freed one crossed to the edge of the dance floor and took up the bindle she had set
down three hundred years ago. It came up at exactly the weight her arm remembered, which
is a thing that happens to Fools. She stood a moment with it across her shoulders,
getting used to the fact of it. Then she bent and lifted the withered white rose off the
stone and held it in her hand, three petals, closed, long finished — looked at it once —
and did not look at it again.

She looked at our Fool instead. There was nothing owed in the look. No warning; no torch
passed with ceremony; no speech. It was one traveller recognising another at a crossroads
and being glad of them.

"Mind the dog," she said. "He'll mind you." The almost-laugh, one last time, light as
anything. "And learn a second dance, would you? I never did. You've the time now. All of
it."

Then she walked out to the rim of the Axis, and stepped off the edge.

Not down. She went *up* — out and upward into the pale silver with her bindle on her
shoulder and a dead rose in her fist, easily, without one flicker of hesitation, the way a
card lifts free of a spread when the hand is done.

She never looked down first. Not one of them ever does.

*  *  *

Our Fool took the centre.

The worn-circular floor accepted one step, and then another, and settled under her the way
a doorstep settles under somebody who lives there.

She stood a while. Then she knelt down and unknotted the bundle on her own Bindle, and
went through it with her hands the way she had on her first evening alive — a tin, a
length of cord, a comb she had never once had a use for — and took out from the bottom of
it a small wooden dog.

Crude work. Four legs of four different lengths and a head that was mostly optimism, one
ear worn to a stub, the whole of it gone smooth and dark from being turned over in
somebody's fingers on a great many evenings for a great many years. She had carried it
through twenty-one regions and never lost it and never mentioned it to anyone.

I told you at the beginning that it stayed in the Bindle, and that I would say no more
about it than that, because I had a long way to go that evening and no wish whatever to
arrive early.

I have arrived now. She set it down on the pale stone, and she was smiling when she did
it, and I will not tell you what I felt, because there is no room in a sentence for it.

And then, at her belt, the White Rose opened.

All of it, at once, every petal — eight of them, gathered one at a time from a hedge in
the Maw and a cracked stair in the Undervault and a lakeside where somebody kept a vigil,
each one spent and regrown a hundred times and never once, in all that while, permitted to
be anything but useful. It came open slowly and completely in the silver light, the way
roses are supposed to, with the small private sound of somebody letting go of a breath
they had been holding out of politeness — and it went on opening, wider than it had any
business going, until it was simply a white rose in full bloom on the belt of a young
woman standing in the middle of everything.

It had only ever regrown. In all its life it had never been allowed to *flower*.

There was nothing left to spend it on. The journey was done.

*  *  *

Black.

And then a sound: cards, squared against a table. Once, twice — that small satisfied knock
a deck makes when a pair of hands tidies it, the sound of somebody deciding to be serious
about something.

A pause long enough to be a held breath.

Then the snap-slide of a single card coming off the top.

*  *  *

The light arrived the way a good trick arrives: not switched on, not poured — *produced*.
It came up out of nothing at all over the shoulder of a high meadow and stood there
looking pleased with itself, and there was gold in it that had certainly not been there a
moment before and had certainly not come from anywhere, and the whole horizon had the air
of a hand turning over to show you that it had been empty the entire time.

A waking that was its own first act of making. I had said as much, and I had meant it, and
here it was, and the honest wonder of it is that it is a wonder anyway.

Grass to the knee, wet and silver, laid down and let up again in broad slow strokes by a
wind that had every intention of going somewhere. A plateau, and an edge, and cloud below
it. Fire-rings scattered untidily about — a great many of them, older and newer, one with
a walking-stick driven into the turf and its handle polished pale.

At the nearest of them, embers gone grey and shy, a last thread of smoke standing straight
up and thinning into nothing.

A bindle leaning against a stone.

A young man lying beside the fire with his cheek in the wet grass, asleep, and one hand
curled loosely round the stem of a white rose. Three petals. Closed and modest, and not a
thorn on it anywhere, which he would not appreciate properly for some weeks.

A stranger. Nobody I knew. Nobody I had ever met, dealt fresh into a world with nothing to
undo and nothing to remember.

And within reach of his sleeping hand, sitting upright on the turf where it had been set
down carefully by somebody at the end of a long journey: a small wooden dog, four legs of
four different lengths, one ear worn to a stub, waiting for him.

A few feet off, in the wet grass, already awake and already sitting, watching him with the
specific patience of a dog who has been awake for some considerable time and is not in any
hurry about it, was Pip.

The same Pip. Not a grey hair on him.

The sleeper stirred. His eyelids moved.

Pip's tail came up, and came down once against the turf. *Thump.*

A formality, observed.

*  *  *

There. That is the whole of it, and I have squared the cards away, and there is nothing
left in the deck that belongs to us.

You have been very patient. I said at the start that there was a chair, and that I had had
rather less company than you would expect, and both those things were true and only one of
them is true now, which is the sort of arithmetic I have learned to be grateful for. You
stayed to the end of a reading you had been warned about on the first page. Not everybody
does. Thank you.

I told you it ends. I told you the ending was not a punishment for having stayed to hear
it, and that it was the point of the whole thing, and you did not believe me, and I said I
would not hold it against you. I don't. I never did. I had three hundred years of not
believing it myself, and I had to have it walked all the way up to me by a young woman
with a rose and a dog before I could sit still for the hearing of it.

Somewhere, at this moment, in a world that is not mine and never will be, somebody with no
past to speak of and no name on the tip of their tongue is opening their eyes in wet grass
and finding it enormous, the way a room is enormous before the furniture arrives. And
somewhere behind that, in a place with no weather, something small and frightened is
sitting down at its own table and finding the nerve to say the only question there is out
loud.

It ends. It ends. And then it asks again.

The dog waits. The dog always waits. He does not bark, and he does not fuss, and he does
not perform one particle of the pleasure he is plainly feeling; he simply sits in the cold
grass at the start of everything with one ear up and one ear making its own arrangements,
watching a stranger come awake, ready to go with.

*Hello*, said the stranger, to nobody, and startled himself.

*Thump*, said the tail.

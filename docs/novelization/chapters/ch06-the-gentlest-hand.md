# Chapter Six — The Gentlest Hand

The road out of the Chantry climbed for two days before it thought to level off, and
somewhere in the second the hedges gave up, and the grass gave up after them, and what was
left was bare grey stone, scored and pitted, as though something enormous had chewed the
country and spat it back out still standing.

She smelled the Maw before she saw it: woodsmoke, hide-fat, the mineral bite of a
tanning-pit, and beneath all three, faint and constant, an animal that had not been far away
for a very long time. Pip's nose had been up for a mile already, shoulders set a fraction
lower than usual — the posture of a dog revising his opinion of the neighbourhood.

The border camps were hide-frames strung with drying skins and snares coiled on pegs.
Everyone here was built for the taking of something, and everyone moved with the wary economy
of people a little afraid of the thing they took.

High on the far ridge stood a shape her eye kept sliding back to: two figures locked together
and dead still — a kneeling woman, both arms raised, and the great pale head of a lion, jaws
stopped open around nothing. Even at this distance it read wrong the way the dead tree on the
Cliff had read wrong: the one thing on the whole mountain not moving.

*There she is*, said the Querent, mild in her ear. *A whole region that hunts monsters for
its supper, and the one monster none of them will look at straight on is a tired woman
holding a cat. Savour that, little Excuse.*

"Is that what I think it is?"

*Exactly what you think. I'll let it speak for itself. It's rather better at that than I am.*

A man straightened from a snare-line as she came level with the nearest frame — tall,
weathered thin the way rope goes thin, a culling-spear slung easy across his back, watching
her come with a hunter's flat, measuring patience.

"New boots on the border," he said. "Mind the ledges. Mind the cats, too — up here the ground
bites about as often as the beasts do."

"Thank you." She looked past him, up the long grey shoulder of the mountain. "Who is that?"

Something crossed his face too quick to name — not a flinch, more the look of a man touching a
stove out of habit, to check it's still hot.

"You've the look of someone come to see the ridge," he said instead. "Everyone does, once.
Nobody goes up." He put out a broad, scarred hand. "Coz Yarrow. Hides, mostly. Honest work,
and the only work the Maw's got. Mountain doesn't grow crops. Grows teeth."

She shook it. "Someone has to hunt the monsters?"

"Someone has to *cull* the monsters," he said, a whole education folded into the word. "A hunt
you can lose. A cull you finish, or it finishes something of yours later." A glance at the
ridge, quick and involuntary. "Mostly."

She asked who the woman was. Nobody knew her name, he said; nobody had got close enough to
want one badly enough to ask. She'd been holding that lion since before the camps were camps
— three hundred years, jaws open, not a muscle moved. Why won't anyone go up, she asked, and
he told her plainly: because the day somebody climbs up and she *finishes* — lets go, or
tires, or blinks wrong — that's the day the biggest cat on the mountain wakes up hungry and
free, three hundred years of appetite behind it. "We like her exactly where she is. Frozen's
safest. For everybody, her included." Had anyone tried to help her? "Help her *how*? Pull the
lion off? It'd eat the puller and then her. Kindest thing anyone's managed in three hundred
years is to leave her be. That's the Maw's kind of kind."

He hitched the spear and turned up the trail, the matter settled without her having said yes.
"Keep your dog close and your hands off the wildlife. Provoke a cat up a crag on my watch and
I'll not be climbing after you."

Pip fell in at her heel — ears up, unbothered by a country that smelled, to her nose, like a
held breath before a fight. He had, she thought, the look of a dog who'd already read ahead
and knew how the story went. Not the first time she'd thought that about him.

*  *  *

The trail pinched into a single-file ledge, sheer drop on one side, fissured limestone on the
other, and halfway along it a mountain cat was wedged snarling into the only gap through —
lean, ash-grey, hackles up, tail lashing. It wasn't hunting. A fresh rockfall had boxed it
against the cliff, and it had decided she was the reason.

Her hand had already found the Bindle. Every road so far had gone the same way: something
snarls, you hit it, the snarling stops.

*Every road in this world so far has rewarded you for hitting the thing in front of you*,
said the Querent, and something in the voice made her pause with her weight still forward.
*Hold that thought. Now put it down. And back away from this one. Slowly.*

She eased back instead of forward. The flattened ears came up a degree. She gave the gap room
rather than taking it, and the hackles along its spine settled by fractions, the way a kettle
comes off the boil. She held still. It watched her a long moment, weighing something she'd
never know the shape of — and then unstuck itself from the wall and slid past her close enough
to brush her coat, gone up the crags in three long bounds. Not one mark on either of them.

*There*, said the Querent, warmth under the dryness now. *Nothing to it but doing the exact
opposite of everything you know. Remember the feel of that. Sooner than you'd think.*

She walked on, feeling as though something in her had been quietly rearranged.

*  *  *

The shelf beyond opened onto broken ground where a deadfall trap had done its work: a young
bear, one hind leg pinned under a baited log-weight, ribs going too fast, too spent to roar —
just a low, worn-down sound, over and over, at nothing. Two camp-hands stood off, spears
grounded, waiting for it to tire enough to finish safely. Coz Yarrow came up behind her,
unhurried, a man keeping an appointment he'd kept a hundred times before.

"That's a fortnight's hides and a month's meat, that is," he said. "Don't mind the noise.
They quiet down, once they've stopped hoping."

She did not believe, for one moment, that it had stopped hoping at all.

"I want to free it."

"Your business. Trapped fair, and mine to take, but I'll not stop you being a fool about it."
He folded his arms and stood back, and the hands did the same.

She could have driven the log off by main force and had it done in a moment — the bear
bolting half mad the instant the weight lifted. She thought of the cat's hackles settling by
fractions and did the other thing instead: knelt, set her palms flat, and did not shove. She
let the weight come up slow, timed against the bear's own straining — brace when it strained,
give when it eased — and when a spasm of panic sent it lurching half onto her, she went still
instead of flinching, because flinching would read as one more thing trying to finish it off.
It cost the better part of an hour and every scrap of patience she owned. Her arms were
shaking by the end, the small unglamorous shake of a job done slow on purpose.

The weight came up. The bear did not bolt. It tested the leg, found it whole, gave her one
long look — entirely without gratitude, the plain assessment of a wild thing deciding you are
no danger — and loped off up the scree at an easy pace, hide intact, without a backward
glance.

Coz Yarrow's jaw worked once, twice, before he spoke. "That was mine. Trapped fair. Mine to
take. And you let it walk off like it cost nothing at all."

"It wanted to live," she said.

"They all want to live. That's not a reason up here. That's just the sound they make." He
stood a moment, watching the empty scree, and then something in his shoulders let go — not
agreement, quite, but adjacent to it — and he picked his spear back up. "Gentle's a fine thing
to spend when it's not your winter you're spending it out of." A grudging pause. "Frightened's
not the same as free, mind. But I'll say this — that bear'll live to be frightened another day,
which is more than I'd have given it an hour back."

He did not follow further. At the edge of the shelf, where his ground ended, he stopped and
looked up at the small far shape on the ridge.

"This is as far up as I go. Never had a reason. You'll have your own, I expect. Mind the
shrines. They get stranger the higher you climb."

*  *  *

The trail steepened into a switchback stair lined the whole way with shrines that changed as
she climbed, the way a story changes in the retelling. Low down, neat: tidy cairns, a worn
rosette, a roofed niche with a candle stub gone to wax dust. Higher, rougher — chipped
offering-bowls, spirals scratched with something that hadn't been a proper chisel, and near
the top, pressed into old grey clay and never once labelled, a single handprint, small, its
owner lost.

Nobody up here had left an offering to a god. They'd left it to *her* — the way you leave
something at the foot of a thing too large and too sad to be understood any other way. One
stone read: *For the one who does not tire. — left by a hunter whose hands did.* Another: *I
climbed to ask her to stop. I could not make my legs do it once I saw her face.* A third, by a
heap of stones that had once been called bread: *We do not know your name. We brought bread
anyway.*

She read them all. Pip sniffed each in turn with the thoroughness of an inspector and moved
on, unmoved — the correct response, and not hers.

*Three hundred years of people who walked all this way up to watch a woman suffer and called
it a pilgrimage*, said the Querent, an edge under the dryness now. *Folk are like that.
They'll queue for a wonder and never once offer to carry it.*

She didn't answer. There wasn't one that wouldn't have made it worse.

Near the top, on a ledge just below the tableau, she found the goat: dozing against the
lion's frozen flank, udder heavy, entirely at ease, curled to the great still beast as though
it were the warmest rock on the mountain. It lifted its head, considered her placidly, and
went back to sleep. Pip trotted over and sat beside it, equally serene. Neither offered a
single word of explanation, and by now she had more or less stopped expecting one.

*  *  *

Up close, the stillness resolved into detail no shrine had managed to hold. The lion was
enormous, pale gold gone grey with weather, jaws stopped open around a bite it had never once
finished. And the woman kneeling into it, both arms raised, hands flat against the roof of
that terrible mouth, was *shaking* — the fine constant tremor of a bowstring held past the
point any string should hold.

Her eyes were open. They had been open, the Fool understood with a lurch, the whole climb.

"Closer," said the woman, her voice worn smooth as a step under three hundred years of the
same foot. "Slowly. The paws still know how to move, even if the rest has forgotten." A
tremor, mastered. "Three hundred years, these hands have stayed open. Do you understand what
that costs? No. Nobody who climbs up here does. They come to look at the *holding*. Nobody
ever asks after the hands."

"I'm asking," the Fool said.

Something moved through the woman's face that might, a very long time ago, have been a laugh.
"...So you are. First in a long while."

She knelt near her, close enough now to see the grey dust worked into every crease, the
calluses gone past callus into something like bark. How long had she held it?

"Since before the shrines. Since before the men who left them. Long enough that the holding
stopped being a thing done and started being a thing that simply *is*. There's a difference.
You learn it round about year one hundred."

"Why not just let go?"

"And do what — watch it eat the border camps? The herds? The child who leaves bread that's
gone to dust before it lands? No. Letting go is the one mercy this cannot afford. So the hands
stay open. That is all the hands do."

"Doesn't it hurt?"

"...Everything hurts." Said so plainly it took a moment to land. "That stopped being the point
somewhere in the second century. Gentleness that costs you nothing is only a preference. This
is the other kind. Do not mistake it for peace."

"Couldn't the lion be killed?" she asked at last, hating the question even as it left her.

The tremor sharpened into something unmistakably angry — old, tired, banked to embers and
stirred now against its own will. "No. Never that. It did nothing wrong but be born with
teeth, in a world that froze before it could learn better. It does not deserve an ending. It
deserves *holding*. There is a difference. It is the whole one."

The arms shuddered again, worse this time, mastered more slowly. "There is one thing," she
said, barely above the wind. "One turn. Three hundred years and no one has ever offered, and
pride is a luxury the hands gave up around the same time as feeling. Hold it for me. Just a
turn. Exactly as it is held now — no tighter, no crueller, and gods help you, do not let go —
and let these hands remember what empty feels like. One turn. That is all that is asked."

She thought of the cat that had only worsened for being struck, and the bear that had only
trusted her for being given time.

"Show me how."

"Come under the arms. Set your hands where these hands are. Feel the shape of it before the
weight comes." Something almost gentle moved under the discipline of her voice. "And then —
do not fight it. Never fight it. Hold it the way you'd hold something you loved that had gone
dangerous."

She set her hands beside the woman's, on the warm dry roof of that vast mouth. For one held
moment four hands shared the weight — and then, slowly, deliberately, with the care of someone
setting down a thing they've been terrified to drop for three hundred years, the woman drew
hers away.

The tableau broke.

*  *  *

Three hundred years of stillness came off the lion in a single vast shudder, and the whole
living, furious weight of it dropped into the Fool's arms.

She had known it would be heavy. She had not known it would be a *fight* — not the clean quick
business of a staff meeting a blank soldier's tabard, but a wrestling match with no edges and
no clock she could see the end of. The lion heaved in surges, jaws snapping at air that had
forgotten, in three hundred years of stillness, that it had ever been let close on anything at
all — and she held, and held, and felt her shoulders begin to burn in the first minute and
simply kept burning after, because there was nothing to do with the burning but carry it.

She understood, within the first surge, that this was the crag and the cull-trap again, only
with the safety stripped away and her own strength on the scale. Grip harder, brace, meet
force with force — and the panic in the great body spiked every time, the jaws coming closer
to something that mattered. So she learned, across one endless afternoon, to do the opposite
of everything her arms wanted: ease when it strained, firm up only when it eased, let go at
the peak of the worst lunges rather than cling through them, meet fury with a stillness she
did not, in her body, remotely feel. Once she held too hard out of plain fear and felt it
seize with a panic that nearly threw her off the ledge. Twice her arms went to water and she
caught the hold again a half-second before the jaws closed on everything that had brought her
here.

Through it all the woman did not leave. She knelt an arm's length off in the churned dust, and
— for the first time in three centuries — her hands were empty, and she plainly did not know
what to do with them. They opened and closed on nothing, reaching halfway toward the lion and
stopping, as if some old discipline forbade her touching what she'd only just set down. It
was, the Fool thought through her own burning shoulders, plainly the worse ordeal of the two.
The holding, at least, had been a task.

"Not the strike," the woman said, low, coaching, never once using the word *I*. "It's the only
thing this truly understands, and it will answer you in kind. Gentleness is not the weak grip.
It is the harder one." Later: "Steady. Let it tire against you, not you against it. Three
hundred years taught these hands that much: you do not win this. You outlast it. Kindly." And
once, quietly, almost to herself: "...The hands don't know what to do, empty. All this while
wanting them free, and now— no." A catch, smoothed over. "Mind the beast. Not the woman."

Not once did the great head turn toward Pip, who sat calm at the tableau's edge through the
thrashing — some old gentleness in the beast, older even than the Stall, that the mountain had
forgotten it still had.

The last surge was the worst: one furious heave, jaws snapping a hand's breadth from her
wrist. She did not grip harder. She wanted to, arms screaming — and instead went utterly
still, the stillest thing on the mountain, and held it through with nothing but patience and
the plain refusal to let go.

The strain went out of the lion all at once, the way a held breath finally, unbearably, lets
go. The great head lowered into her hands — not pinned now, *resting* — and the body folded
into the dust with a groan almost, impossibly, content. The jaws closed. Gently. On nothing.
For the first time in three hundred years.

She knelt there afterwards, her whole body one long ache, and did not move, because moving
felt like it might undo it.

*  *  *

The woman knelt in the settled dust and stared at the sleeping lion as though she had never
once seen it before. Her empty hands lay open in her lap, palm up, waiting for something to
be put back into them.

Around her, at the edges — the office began to crack. Not a wound: the sound old clay makes
coming apart from a face that had been underneath the whole time, the frozen posture and the
terrible patience going all at once.

"It's — held," she said, voice worn through in a different place now. "It's *held*, and the
hands are—" She turned them over, staring. "They're mine. They're mine. When did— how long
since anything was—"

She stopped. Something came up through the crack — not injury, a name, surfacing the way
feeling comes back into a limb that has slept too long: all at once, and it stung.

"...*Maud.*" She said it slowly, tasting the shape of it, the way a man off a long desert road
tastes water and has to stop a moment to believe in it. "Maud. I'm—" the word caught,
unfamiliar after three centuries, and did not quite fit her mouth. "—I'm Maud. That's— gods.
That's *me*. That was me the whole time, under the holding. I'm Maud, and I am so *tired*, and
I am— free. I'm free."

She said *I* three, four times over, tasting each one. She got to her feet a different woman
than the one who'd knelt: the grim, joyless discipline gone off her like the crack off the
office, leaving something older underneath, gentler, finally allowed to be either.

"Three hundred years I asked the mountain," Maud said. "The shrines. The pilgrims. The sky
itself. *Would nobody take a turn.*" She looked at the Fool properly, for the first time
without three centuries of holding between them. "And a fool climbed up and did. Just — did.
Held a lion for a stranger because she asked."

She reached into the crook of her own empty hand, where a card had surfaced the way the name
had, and held it out, both hands, personally, the way you hand over something you've carried
so long you can hardly believe you're allowed to set it down.

"Trump VIII. *Tame*, if it's ever written down proper. Take it — and take the lesson stitched
into it, because it cost me everything to learn it: the gentlest hand is the strongest one.
It's just the tiredest, too. Nobody warns you of that part." A ghost of that almost-laugh
again. "I'm warning you."

The card went into the near pocket of her coat, the *now* pocket over the heart, and settled
as though the pocket had been cut for it.

Maud turned to the sleeping lion. She knelt one last time — no weight in it now, no holding —
and laid her open hand once against the great pale mane, light as a leaf coming to rest. The
ear flicked. It did not wake. She let the hand rest a moment longer than she needed to, and
lifted it, and that was the whole of the goodbye.

"Go on, then," she said. "Both of us. Three hundred years is long enough to hold anything.
Even each other."

*  *  *

Maud walked down off the ridge the ordinary way, on ordinary legs, and did not look back —
already, within a dozen steps, indistinguishable from any woman who had climbed up to see the
tableau and come back down disappointed. The lion woke at its own pace, stretched enormously,
regarded the Fool with the frank incuriosity of a cat that has decided you are furniture, and
ambled off along the crag in no hurry at all, threatening nothing — the clearest picture she'd
seen yet of what freedom looked like for something that had never been political about
wanting it.

The goat got up and trotted off after it. Neither found this remarkable. Pip watched them go
with an expression of such focused longing that she laughed out loud for the first time since
the border camps — one honest, surprised bark of it, arms still aching, a card new in her coat.

*You'd only slow them down*, she told him. He did not look convinced. He did not, however, go.

*  *  *

Near the highest shrine, tucked into a crack in the weathered rock where no pilgrim had ever
thought to look — because none had come to plant anything, only to grieve — she found a wild
white rose growing straight out of bare stone with no soil she could see, small and stubborn
and alone, its petals the same close, modest white as the one at her belt.

She knelt by it a while, working the ache out of her shoulders, and thought that a flower
growing wild on ground given over so completely to holding and grief was its own small
argument against everything the mountain believed about itself. She took the graft the way
she'd learned everything that day — gentle, unhurried, no more force than the stem needed to
give — and bound it against her own with a strip of cord, and waited.

It took. A fourth petal came open against the other three, slow and private, with the same
small sound of somebody letting go of a breath held out of politeness. She thought, without
quite knowing why, of Maud's empty hands not knowing what to do with themselves.

*A good place for one to grow*, said the Querent, quiet, none of the usual performance in it.
*Rather the point of the whole mountain, if you ask me. Though nobody up here would think to.*

*  *  *

She found Coz Yarrow days later, the border quiet in a way it had plainly never been in his
lifetime. He sat on an upturned trap-log with the culling-spear across his knees, turning it
over, looking at it the way you look at a tool for a job that has quietly stopped existing.

"Whole mountain went soft overnight," he said, without looking up. "Beasts won't run, won't
fight, won't do a blessed thing but stand there being *harmless* at you. Nothing left in the
Maw that needs culling. Which means there's nothing left that needs *me*. Not the way I was
needed."

"I'm sorry it cost you this."

"Don't be. Wouldn't take it back if you offered — that's the hell of it. Saw the lion amble
past yesterday with a goat at its heel and couldn't have lifted this spear if you'd paid me.
Can't cull a thing that's just... going about its day."

"You could learn the rescue trade."

A long look, not unkind, not quick to agree. "Coax a bear out of a bog instead of dropping a
log on it. Aye. Maybe. Same eyes. Same read of the ground. Other end of the spear." He didn't
commit, not out loud. "Old dog. New trick. We'll see if the trick takes."

"Was it worth it, do you think?"

"You're asking the wrong man on the wrong day, son. Ask me in a season, when I've worked out
what a hunter is with nothing left to hunt. I'll have an answer by then, or I'll have a new
trade. One of the two."

He stood at last, and did not sling the spear back over his shoulder the way he had every day
she'd known him. He stood it upright in the turf instead — not thrown down, not put away, only
set standing, the way she had once seen a walking-stick stand driven into the turf of a high
meadow, a lifetime ago, as if its owner meant to come straight back for it, and wasn't
entirely sure, this time, that he would.

She crossed the high pass some days later and found him coming the other way — no snares, the
culling-spear still on his back, unslung for days. Below them both, in the flat unmoving
light, the lion ambled along the lower crag with the goat trotting at its flank, close enough
to hear, threatening nothing. Coz Yarrow watched it the whole length of the crag, his hand
never once straying to the spear. He didn't say why. He nodded to her — hunter to fool, one
professional to whatever it was she'd turned out to be — and walked on, and did not explain
himself, and she did not ask him to.

*  *  *

The trail down off the highlands ran long and easy, switchbacking through grey stone gone
green again, and somewhere past the last shrine she stopped, the way she was coming to stop
most days, and looked out.

Below her the whole shape of the Spread lay under its unmoving sky — fields, a black wood,
water catching a flat unchanging light. And ringing the entire visible world, a great pale
road, wider than any she'd walked, worn smooth and white as bone, in one long unbroken circle.
Along it, small at this distance and endless, moved a dust-cloud that did not thin, did not
settle, did not, watching it a long minute, ever once stop.

"What's that?"

*That*, said the Querent, something in the voice she was learning to listen for — not quite a
warning, not quite an invitation — *is the Longroad, little Excuse. That dust doesn't stop
because whatever's making it hasn't stopped marching in three hundred years.* A pause. *You'll
want your boots on properly for that one. Go on. Down you go.*

Far off, past the causeway and past everything else, small and silver and steady, the Axis sat
at the centre of the turning world and glittered, and in the smallest part of it, so far away
it was barely a flaw in the light, something went on turning, and turning, and turning.

She looked at it a while, the way she was coming to. Then she whistled up the dog, and went
down off the mountain, toward the dust that never stopped.

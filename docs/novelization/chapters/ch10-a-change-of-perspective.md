# Chapter Ten — A Change of Perspective

She crossed from the Wheelhouse's grinning, unlucky streets onto a forest road that went from ordinary to wrong in the space of about nine trees, and she noticed the ninth one because a leaf, brown and curled and entirely unremarkable, drifted past her knee going the wrong direction. Not blown. Not carried. It simply rose, unhurried, and settled on the underside of a branch with a small private sound, and lay there.

She stopped and looked at it a while.

*Yes*, said the Querent, before she could ask. *That's the wood, that is. Not a trick of the light. Have your look. I'd be disappointed if you didn't.*

Further in, a whole glade hung the wrong way about — roots combing at a sky that shouldn't have been there, a stream running along a ceiling of moss without spilling a drop, a lost boot resting toe-up on nothing at all. She stood at the edge of it with the Bindle already up, because eleven regions had taught her hands that reflex before her head got a say.

*Staff up already*, said the Querent, dry and fond. *I did wonder if you'd do that. You've had a run of places that wanted hitting, I'll grant you — but do have a proper look round first, little Excuse. Nobody in this wood is going to make you use that thing.*

She let the Bindle down slowly, the way you let down a hand you've been holding over a flame — not sure yet the flame has gone.

Nothing came out of the trees to punish her for it. A squirrel went by along the underside of a bough at a brisk, unbothered trot, entirely unconcerned that its floor and ceiling had swapped places three hundred years back. Pip watched it go, faintly scandalized, and then sat, and kept watching.

She looped the staff back onto the Bindle. It felt like taking off a coat she hadn't known she was wearing.

*  *  *

A man came down a tree trunk toward her — not climbed down, came *down*, walking, boots finding the bark the way boots find a path, two good coils of rope over each shoulder and the long-settled ease of someone who had stopped noticing which way was up.

"Mind your step," he said, arriving at what was, for him, apparently the ground, "and I mean that in every direction at once, out here. Watch me, not your feet." He looked her over — fresh boots, a rose gone soft-pink at the belt, a small white dog deciding whether a stranger merited standing up for. "Fresh boots. You'll want a guide. This wood eats travellers who trust their own eyes.

"Bracken Loft," he said, offering a hand. "I read the wood for folk who can't. Been at it years — longer than anyone living, near enough — because out here your eyes lie to you, and I've learned exactly which lies. Up's a suggestion in the Gallowwood. Down's a hobby."

"Which way is the man on the tree?" she asked.

"Straight in, and a good long way up. Or down. You'll see why that's a joke shortly." He started walking, and she and Pip fell in either side of him. "He's the reason the whole wood hangs like this. Three hundred years, happy as a cat in a sunbeam, and the trees just — went along with him. I'll take you as far as the hollows. After that it's you and him, and no rope I own is any use for *that*."

"Is he dangerous?"

"Him? Gods, no." Bracken Loft laughed, short and genuine. "Dangerous is a word for things that want something off you. He doesn't want a thing in this world. That's rather the trouble with him."

"Why does it hang this way?"

"Because *he* does, and the wood's polite." He said it with the fondness of a man complaining about someone he loved. "Enlightened, the pilgrims call it. I call it a man who got so comfortable the ground gave up arguing."

"I came to set him free," she said. Twelve words, and she meant every one of them.

Bracken Loft stopped walking, looked up the wood, and back at her.

"Aye," he said. "I expect you did." A careful pause. "Just — see him first, before you decide it's a mercy. Not everything up here's a prison."

He pulled a rope taut between two boughs, tested its lean, and nodded himself further into the wood.

"Come on then," he said, over his shoulder. "First thing this wood teaches you, it teaches by making you let go of a branch you'd very much rather hold. Everything after's the same lesson, louder."

*  *  *

The trail pinched out over a shallow dell, and on the far side it simply resumed — except there was no way across except *under*. A single low bough reached over the gap, and the only footing offered was its underside, and the only way onto it was to hang.

Bracken Loft did it without ceremony: reached up, took the branch, swung his feet loose, and hung there upside down as easily as another man leans on a fence, pointing with his chin at the handholds waiting along the bark beneath.

She reached up. Her hand closed on the branch, and for one whole heartbeat every year of instinct she did not know she had told her, quite firmly, that this was a terrible idea — that letting go of solid ground had never once gone anywhere good.

*You felt that, didn't you*, said the Querent, quiet in her ear, the dry gone soft at the edges. *That little argument your hands had with you before they opened. Hold onto it. You've done this once before — off a rather taller edge, first morning I knew you. Turns out it's the whole trick of this place, too.*

She thought of the cloud-floor and the long gold light of a first evening, and a small white dog going off the edge of the only world she had without one flicker of hesitation. She had not known, that first time, that letting go was a thing you could get *better* at.

She hung there a moment, arms taking the weight, and let herself notice — really notice — that this was the same shape as the Cliff, folded smaller and made safe: the same held breath, the same decision arriving in her hands before her head had finished making it.

The narrator will let her have that. It is not every day a person gets to feel a rhyme land in her own body.

At the branch's midpoint she released, dropped a few foolish safe feet, and landed with her knees soft the way falling had taught them, feeling entirely, ridiculously fine.

"There," said Bracken Loft, dropping easily beside her. "Not so bad, letting go, when the wood's the one that's upside down and not you. Keep that feeling handy. The hollows ahead don't ask so nicely."

*  *  *

The glade past the dell was inverted wholesale — grass hanging overhead like a green ceiling, a brook running along it without spilling a drop, and slung beneath the whole upturned floor a canopy bridge of planks and rope, plainly built to be walked *across*, which now meant walking it from below, boots to the boards and her head pointed at the true ground far, far above.

A carved post stood at the entrance, spiralled in a whorl she hadn't the eye to read yet, and the moment she crossed its line the sky went where the ground had been, gently, honestly, warned by the post but not softened by it.

She put her boots to the boards. Walking with her feet toward the sky felt, for the first four steps, exactly like drowning standing up. By the eighth step it felt like nothing at all, which frightened her rather more.

Pip padded along beside her on the underside of the bridge, tail going, entirely undisturbed, having required no instruction whatsoever in a discipline that had cost her four days on the Prestige's high wire.

A rope-checker hanging comfortably at her work called across without looking up: "Mind the west line, it's due a re-knot." A pilgrim hanging near the path, upside down and radiant, added: "Three hundred years he's hung there, serene as sunrise. There's a lesson in it. There has to be a lesson in it." She did not stop to argue. She was too busy discovering that wrong-way-up, held long enough, stops meaning very much at all.

*  *  *

The hollow past the glade was where the wood's confusion peaked — ground and not-ground swapping so fast the eye simply gave up choosing a side — and halfway down it a Coins-suit peddler was stuck fast to a root jutting, at that particular moment, from what counted as the ceiling. Pack still on his back. Knuckles white.

"Ah," said Bracken Loft, not unkindly. "There's always one. Gets turned around, freezes, decides the whole world's gone mad instead of just the one wood. Go on — talk them down. Or up. You'll work it out."

She climbed across to him.

"Don't you tell me to let go," he said. "I let go, I fall *up*, and I have been assured by three separate pilgrims that up, in this wood, is a very long way indeed."

"Let go," she said. "I'll catch you."

He stared at her. "You — will?" A beat, taking her in: a stranger with a dog and a stick. "...That's the most honest offer I've had all century. Right. Right. Here I—"

He let go, dropped the right way — of course he did, the ground had been right the whole while — and landed soft with the stunned, delighted face of a man who had braced for the worst fall of his life and got a two-foot hop for his trouble. He sat down hard and laughed once, and did not get up for a moment, and she let him have it.

"Right the whole time," he said, to nobody. "The ground was right the whole blessed time and I hung there like a bat." He looked up at her, sincere now. "Thank you. Genuinely. I'd have clung to that root till the world ended."

*There now*, said the Querent, soft, only for her. *That's the honest work, that is — not the letting go. The catching. Anyone can fall. Precious few will stand under it.*

*  *  *

The wood opened, at last, onto the World-Tree, and every idea she had been quietly building about up and down came apart at once and did not bother reassembling.

It was vast past sense — roots and crown so indistinguishable that the eye simply gave up hunting for a floor and let the whole tree be everywhere at once. On its greatest bough hung the Hanged Man: ankle crossed over knee, arms loose, coat pooling toward a sky that might as easily have been a floor, a man three centuries into a very good hang and radiating a stillness that made the wood's whole inverted grammar look, suddenly, like the smallest part of the strangeness here.

At the tree's foot — or its crown; she could not have told you which — a threadbare pilgrim sat camped among notebooks fat with almost-teachings, conducting an entirely one-sided conversation upward.

"—and so I put it to you again, master," he was saying, pen poised, "that the suspension of the self *is* the—" The Hanged Man made a small, contented sound, the noise a man makes settling deeper into a good chair. "—yes. *Yes.* Write that down. That was nearly the whole of it."

He did not look up as she passed. The narrator will not tell you whether Fenwick Sorrel ever found what he was listening for. Some searches are not the novel's to finish.

Bracken Loft stopped at the clearing's edge.

"Far as I go," he said, looking up at the hanging figure with something crossing his face he did not try to name. "My rope's no good up there, and neither am I. Go careful with him. He's the happiest thing I ever met, and I've never once been able to decide if that's a comfort or the saddest thing in this whole crooked wood."

The Hanged Man turned his head — unhurried, upside down, entirely unsurprised — and found her with a look of plain, uncomplicated affection, as though he had been expecting exactly this visitor for a long and pleasant while.

"Oh, *there* you are," he said. "The one who belongs nowhere. I wondered when you'd get round to me. No hurry — there's never any hurry — but I did wonder."

"You know what I've come to do," she said.

"Mm. Turn me loose. Cut me down, more or less." He smiled, and it reached everything about him. "The others made a fight of it, I should think. Poor loves. All that struggling to hold a thing they were only ever standing in.

"I shan't fight you. I couldn't, and I wouldn't, and there'd be no point — you can't beat a man who's already exactly where he wants to be. But you *can* join me. Come up. Hang a while. Do the thing I've done for three hundred years, the whole long circuit of it, and then — then ask me if I'll come down."

"That's the whole trial? Hanging?"

"That's the whole trial. Hardest thing in the wood and the easiest, both at once. Letting go, and *staying* let go, all the way round and back to me." He tipped his head, plainly delighted by the idea. "Nobody's ever done the full circuit. I'm rather excited to watch."

*  *  *

She left the Bindle slung and unused, and reached up for the great bough beside him.

The same motion as the low bough, the same argument in her hands, quieter now, half-remembered rather than fresh — and she hung there beside the Hanged Man, high above a ground indistinguishable from a sky, and felt her body slowly stop insisting there was anything to be afraid of.

"There," he said, easy, unhurried, keeping pace beside her. "Feel your hands wanting to close? That's not fear, whatever they told you. That's just a body that never learned the difference between falling and flying. Neither did I, at your age. You unlearn it. You've all the time there is."

Then the circuit began, and this stretch of it remains, to my mind, the strangest and the gentlest of her whole journey.

She went hand over hand along boughs that hung the wrong way and stopped, within a few minutes, noticing that they did. She crossed a glade where the world honestly inverted around her, ground and sky trading places between two carved whorl-posts, her stomach only murmuring now rather than protesting outright. She walked canopy bridges from beneath a second time and a third, each one a little less like drowning and a little more like simply walking, because that, it turned out, was all it had ever been.

There were releases too — small deliberate ones, timed to swings, each a fresh little letting-go over a drop the wood kept promising was not the kind that hurt. She began, without quite deciding to, to let go early. On purpose. Before the swing had even finished asking her to.

The Hanged Man, hanging some way off and watching, called across the gulf with a whoop of a laugh.

"Ha! You *let go* early there. On purpose. Oh, that's lovely — most people fight the whole way and arrive furious. You're arriving *curious*. That's much the better way to arrive at anything."

Somewhere in the middle of the third or fourth glade, hand finding hold after hold without watching them, she stopped being able to tell you which way was which, and found this did not frighten her in the slightest. The world had simplified itself down to the one thing that mattered: the next hold, and nothing underneath it that could not be trusted.

She has told me, since, that it was the nearest thing to peace she found in the whole journey. I believe her. I was there, in her ear, the whole time, and I have wondered since whether that was the one stretch in which I, too, very nearly forgot to be afraid.

The circuit curved, by its own inverted logic, all the way round — and brought her back to the great bough where it had begun, hanging once more beside him, level, the whole crooked wood spread out below-and-above them, gold and still and entirely hers to have crossed.

*  *  *

"Well," said the Hanged Man, a slow, pleased breath in it, the sound of a man arriving somewhere he has waited a very long time to arrive. "You did the full circuit. Nobody's ever done the full circuit. So. Just the one question, then — the only one I've got, and I've had three hundred years to whittle it down to the true one.

"Comfortable?"

I have listened to her answer a great many questions across this whole long Reading, and heard her reach, almost every time, for the earnest one, the honest one, the one that cost her something to say. I want it on the record, plainly, that this is the single time in the whole journey she did not — the one deliberately foolish thing she ever told anybody, hanging upside-down to a man who had waited three centuries for exactly this moment. I would not trade it for any of the earnest ones.

"No," she said. "My arms fell asleep an hour ago."

The Hanged Man's whole face came apart with delight.

"*HA!*" It came out of him like something long corked and suddenly opened — a full, undignified, glorious laugh, upside down, three centuries in the making. "No! No, of course not, nobody is, nobody has *EVER* been, that's the secret nobody up here will say out loud!" He was laughing so hard he swung a little on the bough, and Pip, watching from below, gave one single wag of solidarity.

"You're the first honest soul to hang beside me in three hundred years," he said, wiping at his eyes with the back of a hand that had never once had reason to wipe them. "Bless you. Bless your dead arms. *That's* the letting go — not the comfort. The honesty.

"Either way, friend. *Either way.* You could have said anything and I'd have handed it over glad — that was always going to be the answer. I only wanted to hear you say a true thing while you hung there. And you did."

He began, unhurried, to laugh again — soft, this time, private — and the office cracked.

It was not a break. It was a loosening: the three-hundred-year serenity that had set around him like amber going soft at its edges all at once, gently, the way a held breath finally lets itself all the way out.

And under it, surfacing the way that breath does, came a name.

"...*Wendel*," he said, the way a man says the name of a town he was born in and has not spoken aloud since childhood — surprised, faintly disbelieving that it still fit his own mouth.

"Wendel." He laughed again, wet-eyed now and entirely without shame about it. "That's — yes. That's the name I let go of first, before all the rest. Funny thing to get back at the very end of letting go. Oh, I *missed* it. I didn't know I could miss anything. Turns out you can be perfectly happy and still have missed your own name the whole time."

Around them the World-Tree began, very slowly, to right itself — the smallest first stirrings of a turning that would take the whole wood the rest of the evening to finish. And Wendel let go of the bough one final time, not into a hang, but into a fall, gentle as a leaf coming down at its own unhurried pace, and was set down standing on the true ground beside her, both feet flat on something that had, at long last, stopped arguing about which way it was.

"Ground," he said, looking down at his own feet with frank, childlike wonder. "Would you look at that. I'd forgotten it was down there." A beat — the one honest, wistful note. "I'll miss the view, I think. That's allowed, isn't it. To be glad, and to miss it. I rather think that's the most honest thing there is."

He reached into the air beside him — no flourish, just a man opening his hand — and a card came loose, turned over, and dropped into her waiting palms.

"Trump the Twelfth," he said. "Overturn, if anyone ever bothers to write it down properly. It's the letting-go, made a thing you can carry. Feather-fall, they'll call the best of it. You'll never fear a drop again. That's my whole life's one lesson, in your pocket. Use it kinder than the world used me. Which—" he laughed, once "—it never did, in fact. That's the joke. Go well, little Excuse."

She slotted the card into the Past pocket, where Triumph had ridden since the toll-fort, and felt it settle with the small certain click every Trump gave her now — a truth she would carry, this time, about falling, and about what it costs a person to let go of a thing they loved even when the letting go is the mercy.

*  *  *

The righting of the Gallowwood took the rest of the evening, and she stayed for the whole slow tide of it, because Wendel walked beside her a little way as it happened, patient and delighted, a man taking his first unhurried tour of a house he had only ever seen from the ceiling.

It was not sudden. It was a long, tidal reshuffle, glade by glade, three centuries of held-up gravity remembering its business and letting everything gently down. Leaves that had fallen upward for three hundred years reversed mid-drift and came home the ordinary way. The upside-down brook poured off its borrowed ceiling and found itself a proper bed among the roots. The canopy bridges swung slowly round until they hung the way bridges are meant to.

Underfoot now — unhappily, abruptly — a canopy-dweller's cottage built entirely to the old gravity found itself upside down all at once. A woman stood in her own doorway, now above her head, staring at a kettle hanging from what had just become her ceiling, her face caught precisely between grief and a bark of laughter that hadn't decided which one it meant to be.

"...Well," she said, to nobody. "My front step's the roof now. Forty years I built that the right way up. The *old* right way up."

She did not finish the thought, and the Fool did not ask her to; some things a person needs to walk through on their own account.

Further along, a pilgrim turned slowly on the spot, his whole religion resolved without asking his permission. "He's — he came *down*. After all that. He just walked off like anyone. I think I finally understand him. Now that he's gone." Near him a rope-checker laughed outright when her line pulled the ordinary way. "Whole trade's arse-over-tit. Reckon I'll cope. A knot's still a knot."

*Feel that?* said the Querent, in a voice pitched to carry beyond just her ear. *Went out of the wood and just... kept going. The frightened ones, the guilty ones, all across the Spread — a notch calmer tonight, and none of them will know why. He does spread, that one. Even done, he spreads.*

Then, quieter: *Twelve down. And the only one of the lot who was* glad *the whole time. Sit with that a moment, little Excuse. I have, rather a lot.*

And then — I will not pretend this was not deliberate, nor that I had not been saving it — the one thing I allow myself, once, in every card I turn with her:

*You're the hero of this story, and the kindest thing you did today was cut down a man who was perfectly happy. Do let that sit crooked in you. It's supposed to.*

She did not answer that one. I did not expect her to. Some things you are simply meant to carry crooked, and carrying them well is its own kind of answer.

*  *  *

The wood, righted, was only a wood by the time they reached the trail where she had first met him — handsome, ordinary, gold-lit, entirely legible to anyone with working eyes and no special skill at all.

Bracken Loft stood on it with his good ropes still coiled over both shoulders, testing one against a bough out of pure habit. It pulled straight down, the way rope pulls everywhere, for everyone, and had nothing left to say to a man who had spent twenty years learning its lies.

"Twenty years," he said, without heat, which was somehow worse. "Twenty years learning to read a wood, and the wood went and learned to read itself. Any child with a plumb-line could guide this forest now. I'm — expert in a place that stopped existing about an hour ago."

"The wood's freer now," she said. "That's worth it."

"Aye. It is. I know it is." A long breath. "Doesn't make a man less useless to be useless in a *better* world, mind. But — aye. It's worth it. I'll get there. Give me a season."

Something settled in him then — not, quite, happiness, but a decision, which would do, most days, just as well.

"Right. Well." He shouldered his useless ropes, a grin finding its way onto his face, wobbling, mostly real. "I'll keep guiding. Not the terrain — any fool can read this terrain now — the *history*. I'll walk folk through and tell them: here the brook ran on the ceiling, there a whole glade hung by its roots, and up there a happy man swung for three hundred years and never once thought to come down till a stranger with a dog asked him nicely. Somebody ought to remember which way it used to hang. Might as well be the one man daft enough to have loved it."

He started up the trail ahead of them, already telling the story to no one, tripping over the shape of his own new sentences — the Gallowwood's first historian of how it used to hang, out of work, out of purpose, and, against every reasonable odd, not entirely out of joy.

*There's your ache for the day, little Excuse*, said the Querent, quiet now, the wink long since spent. *He's right, of course. He usually is. The forest's freer, the man's freer, and one honest fellow's twenty years are worth exactly nothing now, and all three of those things are true at once and none of them cancels. Onward. There's more cards, and every one of them costs somebody.*

*  *  *

She walked the last stretch of the righted wood with Pip trotting easy at her heel and the new card settled warm against her ribs, and somewhere along it she stopped on a low bank above the ordinary trail and let herself do the thing Wendel's gift had made possible: she stepped off the edge, on purpose, into open air over nothing very much at all, and let herself drop.

Feather-fall took her the way a hand takes a dropped cup before it can shatter — slowing the fall to something a leaf might have chosen, until she came to rest as gently as a held breath let out. She stood a moment feeling her own heartbeat, which had not, this time, had anything at all to say about the matter.

*There you are*, said the Querent, something almost like pride in it. *A drop taken slow as a leaf. He'd have liked to see that. I daresay he still can, somewhere.*

She turned, in the last gold light of the wood, and looked north-west — out past the whole crooked shoulder of the Gallowwood, to where a mountain sat locked in a dusk that had never once, in three hundred years, thought to deepen into night or lighten into day. And on its far slope, small and steady and entirely alone, one lantern-light moved — unhurried, going nowhere anyone had ever managed to follow it to.

"What's that one?"

*That*, said the Querent, and I will confess I let the fondness show more than I meant to, *is the Dim. And the very next thing on your road, if you've a mind to go and get properly lost for a while.*

She whistled up the dog and turned her boots toward the lantern, and did not look back at the Gallowwood again — though she would tell me, much later, that she still thought sometimes about a man teaching a whole forest how to fall gently, and a guide learning, slowly, how to love a place for what it used to be.

# Chapter Nine — Round and Round

The Longroad ran level and pale under an unmoving sky, and for the first stretch of it she rode.

She had not known she could, until she tried it: a thought, half a wish, the shape of reins in an empty hand — and the Chariot answered, the way Triumph had promised it would, an outline of gold dust and old momentum gathering into something with a back broad enough to sit on and a gait like a held breath let out slowly. It did not need to be fed, or thanked, or believed in particularly hard. It simply came when called, all down the long grey ring of road with its bleached banners and its three-hundred-year procession creaking along somewhere off to the south, forever almost arriving nowhere.

Pip rode the first half-mile sitting bolt upright between her knees like visiting royalty, then got bored of dignity and ran instead, a small white flag out ahead of her the whole way north-east.

*Seven cards in your coat, little Excuse*, said the Querent, comfortable in her ear. *Seven, and every region behind you that little bit less patient than the one before. Everyone where you're going has been counting rather longer than you have.*

"Counting what?"

*Oh — everything*, said the Querent, lightly, which was not quite an answer and was, I promise you, not a lie either. *Wait till you see the place. It's rather good at counting.*

The skyline told her before the gates did: something vast stood up out of the middle distance, filling half the sky the way a cathedral fills the end of a street, iron-ribbed and spoked in perfect radiating lines — and utterly, wrongly still, a rose window laid flat and stopped mid-turn, as if some enormous hand had reached up and caught it by the rim.

*  *  *

The gutter ran straight down the middle of the street, and on one side of it the world was gold.

Fresh paint, unbroken glass, awnings that still smelled faintly of new dye. A fountain ran wine into a basin already brimming, by the look of the dust caught glittering and motionless on its surface, since before her grandmother's grandmother was a rumour. On the other side of the same gutter — close enough, she thought, testing it, that she could have spat across and hit either kerb — the world was ruin. Sagging roofs. A signboard hanging by its one remaining nail. A puddle that seemed to have arranged itself directly under the one dry step available.

The line between the two did not so much as waver. It had not moved, she understood, looking at it, in three hundred years.

Pip trotted straight down the gutter itself, one white paw in fortune and one in calamity, entirely unbothered by either, the way he was unbothered by most things that troubled people with fewer legs and less certainty.

A man was sweeping it. That was the strange part, more than the gold or the ruin — a lean, grey-templed fellow in a croupier's visor, working a push-broom in long unhurried strokes straight across the divide, lucky side to cursed side and back, as if the gutter were an ordinary thing that collected an ordinary sort of dirt.

He clocked her without surprise, the way a man clocks weather he was already expecting.

"New face," he said, not stopping his stroke. "Don't stand with your feet either side of the line, friend. The House hates a hedged bet. So do my knees."

"Which half's better off?"

"Ask me on a full stomach, I'll say the lucky. Ask me at three in the morning, I'll say the cursed — at least they've got each other. Luck's a lonely thing, guaranteed."

"Why doesn't the Wheel turn?"

He looked up at it then — really looked, the way you look at something you have stopped seeing on purpose. "Same reason the sun won't set two regions over. Somebody stopped playing and called it winning." He set the broom against his hip. "Deuce Halloway. Only croupier who works both districts, which makes me either the fairest man in the Wheelhouse or the maddest, and I've had three centuries to decide which and I still haven't."

"Nobody ever crosses?"

"Born your side, die your side — except nobody here does the dying part either." He said it plainly, the way a man reads out a rule he stopped resenting once resenting it stopped working. "Lucky can't lose. Cursed can't win. And me, I walk it both ways every morning, and I'll tell you the truth I don't tell the paying customers." Something in the visor's shadow went very tired. "It stopped being funny about two hundred and ninety years ago. A joke you can't get out of isn't a joke. It's just the room you're in."

He nodded up at the great stopped rose window of iron filling the sky.

"Turn that, and maybe a cursed man gets one good morning. Maybe a lucky one learns what a bad one costs." He picked the broom back up. "Tunnels are down the cursed side, past the falling signs. But see both halves first, or you won't understand what you're breaking. Gilded Parlour's that way. Mind your head over there. Mind everything, really."

He went back to sweeping, cursed to lucky and back, one long stroke at a time, and did not watch her go.

*  *  *

The Gilded Parlour was a wonder and a horror wearing the same coat.

Chandeliers dripped light onto felt tables green as spring grass that had never once been rained on. A crowd of the eternally fortunate sat about them in the dead-eyed serenity of people who have not felt a stake in three hundred years, winning chips stacked in careless towers nobody troubled to spend, because there was nothing left in that room anyone still wanted. A gentleman in staggering, improbable finery was betting a fortune he plainly did not have on the roulette wheel, and plainly did not need to.

"Everything on black," he announced, to nobody, radiant. "Ha! Black again! Never *not* black, is it just —" a flicker, quickly smoothed over, the smile going back on like a coat, "— isn't it just going to go on forever."

The house dealer at the dice table was a Blank, visor over a smooth blank oval where a face should have gone, a printed Ten of Coins on its tabard. It set the dice before her with a courtesy that had worn all the way through to mechanism, and waited.

She meant to lose. She told herself that plainly, the way you tell yourself a thing you rather suspect won't hold — *I will throw badly, on purpose, and see what happens.* She threw the dice off the edge of the felt where they had no business landing true. They landed true. She called out a number so absurd it was nearly a joke, just to watch the House be forced, for once, to disappoint her.

It did not disappoint her. It never once disappointed her. The dealer raked her impossible winnings across the felt with a small mirthless flourish and set the dice back, waiting for a throw that would also, inevitably, win.

There is a particular kind of dread that comes from wanting to fail at something and being refused even that, and she felt it now, small and cold, under the chandeliers.

*Feel that little sag in your chest, every time you win?* said the Querent, quietly, as the pile grew taller and meant less. *That's the sound of a game with the danger taken clean out of it. Turns out guaranteed fortune is the exact shape of no fortune at all.* A pause, and something in the voice went carefully light, the way a person goes light on purpose over something that is not light at all. *And do you know, someone's had their thumb on these particular dice a good deal longer than anyone in this room. Not mine — I only ask the questions. But the hand holding all of this up, every roll, every step you've taken since the Cliff, has never thrown honest either. Funny old game. Do go easy on it.*

She did not know, then, what to do with that. She would, later. She left the winnings on the felt, as — she understood, looking round the room at all those careless untouched towers — everyone here eventually did, and the Blank pointed a flat dealer's palm at the door, because there was nothing in that gilded room worth carrying out of it.

*  *  *

Across the gutter, the city turned to slapstick made permanent.

A signboard dropped the instant she passed beneath it — she felt the wind of it go by her ear — and a cobble tipped underfoot two steps later with the malicious timing of a thing that had been rehearsing. A ladder that had, she was almost certain, been flush against a wall a moment before was now standing precisely where her shin wanted to go. None of it was deadly. All of it landed, over and over, the exact tireless way bad luck lands on people who have had nothing but.

And yet — this was the part that undid her a little — the cursed-siders were the warmest people she had met since the Cliff. They called warnings a half-second before disaster and caught each other's dropped baskets without looking up, and laughed, honestly, every time the street did its worst, the way you laugh at a joke a difficult old relative tells for the thousandth time because you love the relative more than you mind the joke.

"Mind the bracket, love — no, the *other* — there." An elder woman, up a ladder mending a neighbour's roof that would surely leak again by the week's end, waved her past a sign already leaning to fall. "See, you learn the rhythm of it. Bad luck's only a stranger the once. After that it's just a neighbour with terrible timing, and we've made our peace with worse." She came down the ladder with the easy economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. "Della Quill."

Against the wall of Della's own leaning house, in a crack between two cobbles that had no business supporting anything, grew a rose bush that had clearly been struck, dropped on, and rained through for longer than the Fool had been alive — and was, against every reasonable expectation, in furious bloom.

"That old thing." Della followed her look and softened, the gallows-humour going somewhere gentler underneath. "Roof's come down on it four times I've counted. It just keeps coming up again, meaner than before. We graft slips off it for anyone who's earned a bit of stubbornness." She snapped a small green shoot off with practised fingers and held it out. "You've earned it, walking both our streets the once and not once looking down your nose at either. Hold your rose out, love."

The Fool unpinned the White Rose from her belt. Della bound the slip against the stem with a twist of red thread, murmuring something half instruction and half habit, and stepped back to watch.

For a moment nothing happened, the way nothing happens with any grafted thing, out of respect. Then the new shoot shivered, and took, and where four petals had been closed and modest at her belt, a fifth unfurled slowly alongside them — smaller than the others, a little ragged at the edge, the exact shape of something that had survived rather than merely grown.

"There." Della nodded, pleased, wiping her hands on her apron. "Cursed-side rose. Doesn't grow *easy*. Grows *anyway*. Suppose you'll want that, where you're headed."

"They say the wheel might turn," said a boy on an upturned crate, ducking a falling shutter without looking up from his whittling. "Won't believe it till a good morning happens to *me*. But it'd be nice. Very nice."

The tunnel mouth waited at the street's dead end: a black iron door set into the base of the Wheel itself, unlocked and unguarded, because in three hundred years nobody in that district had had the luck to reach it, or the curse to want to.

*  *  *

A carriage that cost more than the whole cursed street stood parked, absurdly, at the tunnel mouth, and Baroness Fettle stepped down before the Fool had gone three more paces — jewelled to the throat, poised, and, beneath the poise, more frightened than that charmed face had ever had cause to look.

"There you are." She planted herself squarely between the Fool and the door. "I had my people watch every road in. One does not become the richest woman in a rigged city by leaving things to chance, dear. That's rather the point of me."

"You'd keep the whole city frozen? For you?"

"Don't say it like that." Something flickered, fast, behind the jewels. "I'd keep it frozen because frozen is *safe*, and a fair wheel is the most dangerous thing I can imagine. You've seen the cursed side. You think a real chance is a kindness." She held the Fool's eye, and did not blink. "Dear, a real chance is a thing that can go *wrong*. I have spent three hundred years never once finding out what that feels like, and I should like to keep it that way."

The Fool was quiet a moment, turning that over the way she turned over most things, slowly and all the way round.

"What are you so afraid of?"

Fettle's composure did something complicated and did not fully recover it. "A roll I can't call." Her gloved hand closed, briefly, on nothing. "One honest throw where the wheel owes me nothing at all. I'd sooner be cursed, if you must know — at least the cursed know how their story goes."

"Keep your coins," the Fool said. "I've nowhere to spend them."

"...No." Fettle's laugh had no humour in it whatsoever. "No, of course you haven't. What a horrifying thing you are — a card with nothing at all to lose. There's no thumb heavy enough for those dice."

She did not physically bar the door. She could not; luck had never made her strong, only comfortable, and she stepped aside, and her voice dropped, on the way past, to something almost honest.

"Go on, then. Break the one thing that ever kept me safe." She did not look back. "But when it's spinning and you're gone, remember I *told* you — nobody down here ever asked to find out what a fair chance feels like. We just lost the argument."

The Fool opened the iron door. Cold, still air breathed up out of the dark beneath the Wheel, and Pip went in first, ears forward, entirely undaunted by any of it.

*  *  *

Under the city the noise finally stopped.

The tunnels ran between the great brake-shoes that held the Wheel from below: iron wedges the size of wagons, clamped against the rim, each glowing faintly at the point of contact, as though even standing still cost the machine something. Chains as thick as her waist hung slack that had once, unimaginably, run taut.

A little maintenance shrine sat at the tunnel's midpoint, and the White Rose stirred against her belt as she approached it, the way it always did near a Waystation — five petals now, all of them full, all of them hers. She rested there with Pip's chin on her boot, and slotted her three cards the way her hands already knew: Triumph over her heart, Manifest ready in her palm, Decree waiting for whatever the fight would ask of it.

Ahead, the tunnel opened into the Wheel's true heart: a vast chamber floored in inlaid segments like a roulette wheel laid flat, ringed by a silent gallery of Blank croupiers motionless at their stations. It was the first true quiet the whole Wheelhouse had offered her — the held breath before a throw.

*Listen to that*, said the Querent, hushed. *Nothing. First quiet this town's had since it forgot how to turn. Savour it, little Excuse. You're about to be the one who ends it.*

At the chamber's centre, on a dais that rotated a slow half-inch and caught, straining against its own seized bearing, every gaslamp in the chamber snapped alight at once, and the presiding spirit of the Wheelhouse rose grinning to meet her — bound, theatrical, radiant, and, under the glitter, bone-tired in a way three centuries of showmanship could not quite paint over.

"*Well* now." The voice filled the whole chamber like a game-show host walking out to the biggest crowd of a career that never got a second night. "Round and round and round she'd go — if only somebody would give her a *push!* Three hundred years the House has kept every chair, dealt every hand, called every number, and never once — not *once* — got to see which way a card would fall on its own. And here comes a stray little nothing with a dog, wandered in off the losing streak of the whole world."

"I came to make it fair."

"*Fair!*" The Wheel of Fortune threw her painted head back and laughed, delighted, the way you laugh at the loveliest bad joke of your life. "Oh, that's the loveliest filth anybody's said in this chamber in an age. You think fair and *fun* are on the same side, do you? Everyone does, right up until the odds sit down across the table. But the House has a soft spot — the House cannot *help* a soft spot — for a variable it can't price. So here's the wager, wanderer.

"Knock the shoes off the rim. Every last brake. Do it, and she turns. Fail, and you join the gallery, and the House deals you in *forever*. Place your bets. Let's! *Play!*"

*  *  *

The floor moved under her the instant the fight began.

It ground, and shuddered, and then — smoother, as if remembering how — began to turn: the first true motion in the Wheelhouse in three hundred years, and she fought on a floor rotating beneath her boots while the whole gallery of stationed Blanks came off their marks around her, rakers and dice-callers and croupiers in dealer's visors, dealt into the wager the same as she was.

The weather came with the turning. A wash of gold light swept one segment of the wheeling floor and steadied every hand it touched — hers included, a warmth in her arm that made the Bindle feel weightless and true. A squall of grey struck the segment behind it and fouled the footing of everyone standing there, and it did not care whose footing it fouled: a Blank caught in the bad-luck squall slipped on air that was not there and went down, its card fluttering free to raise a bearer elsewhere in the city; a Blank washed gold a moment later steadied and came on harder than before.

She learned, the way she had learned the falling signs on the cursed street, to watch the segments come round rather than fight where she stood — good weather, strike hard; bad weather, get clear; let the wheel bring the next one to her instead of chasing it. Manifest's conjured hand hauled a croupier clean off its feet and into a squall meant for her; Decree's stored patience let a heavy blow glance off her raised guard when it should, by rights, have put her down.

"Ohh, you're *reading* it!" the Wheel of Fortune called from the shifting dais, delighted past all sense. "Look at that — riding the good, ducking the bad, dancing with a wheel instead of shoving at it! Nobody's played the House like a *partner* in three hundred years! More! MORE! Round we GO!"

*There it is*, said the Querent, easy, over the din. *Stop fighting the luck and start surfing it. Fair and random were never enemies, little Excuse. They're just two hands of the same dealer.*

One by one the brake-shoes cracked and fell, and each time one went the ring turned faster, freer, the whole fight accelerating with it, until the last wedge of black iron split under her staff with a groan that shook three centuries of dust down out of the gallery rafters — and the ring lurched, and turned. Truly turned, smooth and enormous and unstoppable, carrying her and every Blank in the chamber and the Wheel itself round together in one long, giddy revolution.

The Blank crew simply stopped fighting.

One by one, mid-swing, they looked up — blank, visorless faces somehow watching all the same — at the gallery wheeling past overhead, and the fight did not end in defeat. It dissolved into something closer to wonder, croupiers and rakers setting down their tools to feel the floor move beneath them for the first time in lives none of them could properly remember.

*  *  *

At the centre of the turning ring, the Wheel of Fortune stood with her arms flung wide, laughing — and then the office cracked.

It went the way the glitz always went, in the end: gilt flaking off a fairground sign, three centuries of patter coming apart along seams that had been holding far too much for far too long, and underneath was only a person, dizzy and delighted and, for the first time in longer than she could guess, entirely unsure what came next.

"It's — oh. Oh, it's *moving.*" She put a hand to her chest as the ring carried her slowly round. "One does forget. One forgets there was ever a before the stopping, and then it moves and there's a — there's a *name* under all the noise, there's —"

She stopped. Something surfaced the way a coin comes up bright out of the bottom of a fountain — hers, and long, long lost.

"...*Penny.* Penny Farthing." She said it, and laughed at the smallness of it, tasting the daft little music of a penny and a farthing, the least and littlest coins in any purse, attached to the biggest wheel in the world. "Of *course.* I'd forgotten I was ever a person and not just a *verdict in a nice frock.* Oh, that's much better."

She turned to the Fool, and there was nothing bound left in her delight at all.

"Do you know what you *are?* Three centuries the odds ran this town and every soul in it had a number stamped on the day they were dealt — lucky, cursed, rich, ruined, sorted and settled and never once surprising. And then in walks *you.* No number. No odds. Nothing the House could price. The first unpredictable variable in three hundred years, and it never saw you coming — because there was no *coming* to see. Oh, I could kiss you. I shan't. But I could."

She reached into the turning air, no flourish, no patter, just a spin of the wrist — and there the card was.

"Trump the Tenth. *Spin* — one throw off the House's own table, one of eight ways it can land, and you'll learn the whole set if you play it enough. That's the trick nobody ever learned down here: the wheel was always *knowable*. Fair and random, dear, dancing partners the pair of them." She pressed it into the Fool's hand herself, the way you pass dice to the one player at the table you actually like. "Go on. Give the world a good turn on my account."

The Fool opened her coat and let the card find its own place among the others, the way they always did. Her hand came alive with a small bright certainty, like a coin she suddenly knew how to flip and call true — and behind her, all around the wheeling gallery, the Blank crew got slowly to their feet and simply stood, watching the floor go round, in something that was very nearly wonder.

*  *  *

Up top, the whole city had felt it.

The great Wheel filled the sky and it was *turning* now, slow and majestic, and along the split street the impossible was happening in real time: the gutter-line, unmoved for three centuries, was drifting. A cursed shutter that had never once shut properly in living memory swung neatly closed on its hinge, for no reason it had ever been given before. On the lucky side, the wine fountain — for the first time anyone there could recall — ran a little short.

Baroness Fettle stood where her carriage had been, a single die turning over and over in her gloved hand, and for the first time in three hundred years could not tell the Fool what it would show.

"I've fought duels," she said, not quite to her, not quite to anyone. "Ordered ruin on rivals. Stared a mad Arcanum in his stopped-clock eyes and did not blink." She held the die up. Her hand was not quite steady. "And *this* — a die I might *lose.* An ordinary roll that owes me nothing. It's the most frightened I've been since before the world stood still, and it hasn't even landed yet." She looked at the Fool, for the only time. "That's your gift to me, card. You didn't curse me. You did something so much worse. You made me *play.*"

She rolled it, once, against the cobbles — and could not bear to watch it land, and turned away before it settled, and walked back toward a house that would, from that evening on, only ever be as lucky as the next honest morning.

Across the drifting line, the opposite was happening at full, cheerful volume. A signboard stayed *up.* A mended roof, for once, simply did not leak. Somebody won a hand of cards fair and honest and could not stop laughing about it, and the whole street spilled out to meet its own good fortune with the disbelieving joy of people who have waited three hundred years to be allowed to have any.

Della Quill stood in her doorway with a fat, battered book under one arm, watching her neighbours dance in a street that was not, for once, trying to kill any of them.

"Three hundred years I've written down every rotten thing that happened on this street," she said, grinning, opening the book to a clean page, "so we could laugh at it together of an evening. Suppose I'd best start a second column. Good luck's going to need writing down too, now — else who'd believe us."

"The sign stayed UP!" somebody was shouting, somewhere close, ecstatic. "Get everyone, get *everyone* — the sign stayed up!"

Deuce Halloway leaned on his broom at the drifting gutter-line, watching both halves of his street at once, and something at the corner of his weary mouth was doing something it had plainly not attempted in a very long time.

"Well." He set the broom aside altogether. "Two crowds on one street, and I can't tell you which of 'em's the lucky one tonight. Reckon I'll leave the line unswept a while. See where it wanders." He looked at her properly, and there was something almost young in it. "Been a long time since I didn't know how a thing was going to land."

*  *  *

She thought about that, walking out with a fifth rose petal at her belt and an eighth card in her coat, and about the House dealer who had wanted nothing but for the wanting to stop, and about a woman rolling a die she could not bear to watch. Every region left one of these behind now — Ferridge on his empty green, a stallholder's debt of honour, a bear made gentle, a baroness who would never again be certain of anything — and she had begun, without quite meaning to, to keep something like a tally.

*Don't do the arithmetic tonight*, said the Querent, and there was less of the dry showman in it than usual. *Do it tomorrow. Or don't do it at all. There's rarely a good hour for that particular sum.*

The road west ran level and quiet, the procession's dented trumpets fading behind her, and far off on the horizon a forest stood up strange against the unmoving sky: a long dark treeline, every leaf on it hanging the wrong way, downward from the underside of every bough, as if the whole wood had been picked up once and set back down inside out.

The Axis glittered at the world's edge, small and silver and patient, and did not stop turning while she watched it, which she still, after eight cards, had not entirely got used to.

She whistled up the dog, who came at a trot with the self-satisfaction of an animal who considers a job well and thoroughly finished, and walked on west, toward the wood that hung upside down, and did not look back at the Wheel even once — though behind her, all the way to the horizon, she could still hear it turning.

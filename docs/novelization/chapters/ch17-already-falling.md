# Chapter Seventeen — Already Falling

The road north out of the Undervault came up out of the ground the way a held breath
comes out of a chest, and for a mile or two she simply let it happen — walking under an
actual sky again, feeling weather she hadn't asked permission for land on the back of her
neck. Somewhere behind her, underground, a gilded pit-city was learning to be a city and
not a cage. Somewhere behind her, on rather a lot of roads now, were people going home who
had forgotten they had one. She did not look back at any of it. She had got out of the
habit of looking back; there was always more ahead, and less of her left over each time
for the looking.

Fifteen cards rode in the Bindle's lining now, more than would ever sit in three pockets at
once, and the Rose at her belt had grown to seven petals, healing and stubborn, the way a
thing grows when enough people along a road have been glad to see it pass. Pip trotted
ahead with the particular workmanlike trot of a dog who has adopted north as a personal
project.

The land climbed as they went, hedgerow to heath to bare grey scree, and the first
thing she saw of the Spire was not the tower at all. It was the *hole* it left in the sky
— a smudge of wrongness on the horizon long before the stone resolved out of it, the way
you notice a missing tooth in a smile before you notice the smile.

*There it is*, said the Querent, quiet, the way the Querent got quiet before the large
things. *The fall that never finished. You saw it from the Cliff, your very first look at
any of this, though you'd no notion then what you were looking at. Now you do. Small
comfort, that sort of knowing. Bring your boots.*

By the time the tower stood over her entire, filling half the world, she had stopped
being able to take it in as one object. It went up and up and simply *stopped*, sheared
through the middle as clean as a stick of chalk snapped by a bad-tempered child, and above
the break the rest of it hung there — storeys, staircases, whole rooms with their walls
still standing, held in the exact attitude of falling and going nowhere. A cornice the
size of a wagon floated above what she guessed must be the market square, motionless,
patient, three hundred years into a drop of perhaps forty feet.

And below it, built in a tight defensive ring with every roof pitched away from the ruin
and every door and window facing anywhere but up, was a town that had made a science of
not noticing.

*  *  *

She came up the switchback road into the undertown, and nobody looked at her, and nobody
looked at the cornice either, and after a while she understood these were the same
discipline, practised by the same people, for the same span of years.

"Oh, it can't fall any *further*, dear," a woman told her stall-neighbour, not to the
Fool at all, over a barrow of turnips. "That's just the shape of it now. Same as a hill's
the shape of a hill. You get used to a thing."

"Don't stand under the cornice gawping," said someone else, mildly, to a boy who had
stopped to gawp. "You'll put folk off their tea. We don't *look* at it. That's rather the
arrangement."

A rope-and-pulley contraption stood bolted to a squat belfry at the town's heart: a great
iron bell with a striking-hammer cocked back and waiting, its rope gone frayed and grey
with handling that was really just years. Nobody had rung it, she would learn, in living
memory — because the strike it was built to warn of had been in the middle of happening
since before anyone alive was born, and you cannot very well ring a bell for a thing that
never finishes arriving.

Pip trotted ahead of her across the square, stopped beneath the floating cornice, sat, and
tipped his head straight up at it with the frank, unbothered interest of a dog who could
plainly see the one thing the whole town had agreed, collectively and for three centuries,
not to.

"You see it too," she told him.

His tail went once against the cobbles. Obviously.

*There it is up close, then*, said the Querent. *Three hundred years it's been leaning
over these good people's breakfast, and three hundred years they've managed not to
mention it. Remarkable, what a person can learn to live directly beneath.*

*  *  *

The belfry was small and swept and cared for out of all proportion to its use. A woman was
up a ladder inside it, an oil-rag in one hand, working grease into the hinge of a hammer
that had never once struck. She came down when the Fool's shadow crossed the doorway, and
she looked — at the Fool's face, at the Bindle, at Pip sitting patient in the doorway — and
whatever she saw there, she had plainly been dreading it since before the Fool arrived.

"No," she said. "Oh, no. I've seen that walk before — in the stories, anyway. You're one
of the ones that goes *up*."

"Someone has to finish it."

"Finish it. Listen to you." Sorrel Vance wiped her hands on the rag, not because they
needed it. "It's not a race half-run, love, it's the only sky I've got." She held the
Fool's eye a beat longer than was comfortable for either of them. "I've heard what
'finishing' means, region by region. Word gets to a belfry same as anywhere. I'm not
begging for the tower's sake. I'm begging for the shape of the world I know how to find
my way home in."

The Fool did not have an answer ready for that, so she asked, instead, the questions she
did have.

"Why keep a bell for a strike that won't land?"

"Because my grandmother's grandmother kept it, and hers before. It's not for the strike.
It's for having something to do with the fear."

"The horizon will still be there." She said it as gently as she knew how.

"Will it." Sorrel's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "That notch up there is how the
shepherds count home in fog. How the boats read the shore. Take the tooth out and you've
changed every map nobody wrote down."

"What happens to you, after?"

"I keep a bell for a tower that isn't there. Same as I keep it now, I suppose." She
looked at the hammer under her rag. "Only lonelier. And honest about it."

She was quiet a moment, and when she spoke again it came out of her lower down, less
rehearsed.

"Do you know what the worst of it is? Half of me wants you to go up. I've been afraid of
that strike my whole life." She almost laughed, and didn't. "At least a fall *finishes*."

Then she climbed back up her own ladder, turning her back with a deliberateness the Fool
understood was not rudeness but the most eloquent thing left for Sorrel Vance to do, and
went on oiling a hammer that would, very soon now, have nothing left to strike.

*She's not wrong, you understand*, the Querent said, quietly, as the Fool crossed back
into the square. *That's the trouble with the ones who grieve a landmark. There's no
villain to point them at. Only a sky that used to have a shape, and won't. Remember that
on the way up. It'll matter less than it should, and then it'll matter more.*

*  *  *

A ramp of fallen roof-slate led out of the town's edge and up into the tower's stable
base — the lowest storeys, which leaned but did not float, solid enough to teach a body
the tower's language before the tower stopped being patient with her.

The stone here was warm under her palms and faintly humming, the way a bell hums after
it's struck and before the sound has quite decided to stop. She learned to read the
handholds, worn pale where three centuries of nobody had gripped them at all; she learned
to time her crossing of a slow-drifting slab to the moment it hung still rather than the
moment it moved, which was a lesson her whole body filed away as *the tower's actual
name*, whatever anyone called it on a map.

Pip went ahead of her along the ledges with the unbothered competence of a dog who had
long ago decided that heights were simply floors that hadn't finished arguing about it.

*Feel that hum?* said the Querent. *That's three hundred years of nearly. The whole tower
is one long held breath — and up top there's a man whose entire job is not letting it out.
You're going to go and change his mind. Or he yours. One of the two. Only one way to find
out.*

*  *  *

Three tiers up, the stable stone gave out at a landing where a staircase simply ended —
sheared clean, the next flight of it hanging a body's length away in open air, one step
frozen mid-fall like a word cut off mid-sentence. Looped over the broken newel-post was a
climbing rope, weathered but sound, knotted by somebody who had known what they were
doing with a knot. Beside it, wedged into a dry crevice and wrapped in oilcloth against
three hundred years of nothing happening, was a journal.

She unwrapped it carefully. The first page had gone brittle and brown at the corners, but
the hand was clear enough.

*Day one below the break. The town thinks me mad. Perhaps. But a thing that is falling
wants to land, and I mean to help it, and be the one who finally saw the top.*

She turned further in, to the middle pages, the hand grown looser there, hurried, and
something else besides — a wariness that had not been in the first page at all.

*I climb to save a landmark, they'll say, or to be famous, and both are true and neither.
I'd not climb it if I knew it ended the whole Reading. But it can't, surely. It's only a
tower.*

The climber had written that, she understood, before anyone had worked out what all this
unbinding added up to — before the word for what she was doing had a shape, let alone a
cost. It was a small irony and a very sad one, and the tower, three hundred years deep
into its own catastrophe, did not appear to care either way.

The last written page was shorter than the rest, the pencil pressed harder, as if the hand
holding it had known something the mind hadn't caught up to yet.

*Further than anyone, I think. I can see the crown from here. He looked at me almost
kindly. I don't think he means to let me by — but I don't think he wants to keep me
either. Tomorrow, the last of it—*

The entry ended there. The pencil-line trailed off the edge of the page and did not come
back. There was no next day written in, and no body anywhere on the ledge — only the
rope, tied off and left for whoever came after, which was the Fool.

She coiled it onto the Bindle without a word. Pip pressed his nose once to the empty
oilcloth, then looked up the shaft, ears pricked, plainly finished with grieving a
stranger and ready to be about the climbing.

*Left the rope tied for the next one*, said the Querent. *Didn't have to. That's the whole
tower in one kind gesture, if you ask me — everybody up here is falling, and they still
stop to help the person behind them do it a little safer. Go on. Use it.*

*  *  *

Past the broken landing the tower came apart around her properly. Staircases hung
mid-topple in every direction; whole rooms floated free of their walls, furniture still
arranged as if company were expected; a chandelier the size of a cottage drifted sideways
through open air, its crystal drops catching a light that had nowhere honest to come
from. And then the lightning woke.

It did not come from any sky. It crawled *down* the ruin from somewhere far above, forking
white and patient across stone that had forgotten, three hundred years ago, how to fall —
and wherever it touched, gravity remembered itself all at once. A stair she was a stride
from using went pale, then simply left, three centuries of stalled collapse finishing in
half a second and gone into the haze below her boots.

She swung the climber's rope across the gap where the stair had been and did not look
down at where it had gone.

*He can feel you climbing*, said the Querent, over the crackle and the wind of her own
falling missteps. *That's him doing that — waking the fall in bits, dropping the floor out
from under you sooner than let one honest inch of this tower reach the ground. Three
hundred years of practice at exactly one thing:* not yet.

Higher, the strikes stopped being scattered and became, unmistakably, a *pattern*: a
sweep of forked light left to right across the ruin, a held beat, a sweep back, slow
enough to learn if you were paying the right kind of attention and utterly unforgiving if
you weren't. She climbed on the offbeats, the way she had learned to read the rubble on
the tower's stable floors, only faster now, and higher, and with considerably further to
fall.

Once, in a gap between two strikes, she thought she heard — very faint, very far below,
almost certainly her own blood in her ears — a single bell note. She had no time to wonder
about it. She filed it away the way she filed everything now, for later, if there was one.

*There — you've got it*, said the Querent, warmer. *It was never random, the sky's temper.
Nothing's random up here. A strike is only an ending that lost its nerve and struck early.
Learn its rhythm and you can climb straight up the middle of a catastrophe. Which is,
roughly, the entire job description.*

*  *  *

The last drift of rubble set her down onto the summit: a shattered crown of a room, open
to the sky on every side, its floor a slab of what had once been a throne-hall hanging
over a very long way down. At its centre stood a tall, gaunt figure in the rotted finery
of a king, a real crown gone green with age still riding his brow, hands raised and empty
above his head, exactly as a man holds up a ceiling only he can see.

He turned. He looked at her, and Pip, and the rope on her shoulder, and something moved
in the ruined face that she had not expected at all — something almost glad.

"Up. All the way up." His voice had the crack of a man out of practice with speaking to
anyone but himself. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone came *up?* They flee a
falling thing. Every soul I ever knew fled it. And here is one who climbed the fall to
reach me."

"I came for the fall."

"*For* it." A bark came out of him that wasn't quite a laugh. "Then you're the first
honest visitor this height has had. Everyone else came to plead. To prop. To tell me the
tower could hold if I only held it harder." His hands trembled, holding up nothing. "Nobody
ever came to help it *land.*"

He lowered them a fraction. The whole summit groaned under her feet, stone speaking to
stone across three centuries of restraint, and he snapped them back up, and it stilled.

"You've been to the others, haven't you." He said it almost gently. "Turning them loose.
I know the look." Something in him that had been braced for a long time let go, just
slightly. "Good. I have been the last thing in this world refusing to finish, and I am so
tired of being right."

His hands did not come down.

"No throne here. No court. No stone that hasn't already broken. Only the will not to let
it end — and you, come to break *that* too. Well. Try. Please." His ruined eyes found hers
across the height of the whole falling world. "It has been so long since anyone made me
let go."

"You don't have to hold it anymore," she said.

"Don't I." His hands trembled harder. "Then take the weight. I warn you — I have carried
it so long I have forgotten how to set it down gently. It comes down the only way I know."
He brought his empty hands together, slow, deliberate, and the summit's held breath broke
at last. "Hard."

*  *  *

He fought her bare-handed, a crowned man swinging for a ruin, and it was nothing like the
long grinding contests she had learned to expect from the great and stubborn dead. It was
short. It was desperate. Every strike he drew lightning through his own body to make, and
every blow he took was one grip less he could keep on a thing three hundred years too
heavy to hold — and with every exchange a storey of the tower gave up and went, roaring,
into the haze below them both.

"Feel that?" he shouted, over the collapsing floor, over her own ragged breath. "That's a
hundred years going. And that — two hundred." His crown had slipped over one eye; he did
not fix it. "You're not beating me, Fool. You're *helping* me drop it. I can't tell
anymore which of us wants this more."

She landed the last blow with the whole summit already coming apart under both their
feet. He staggered, caught himself on nothing at all, and for the first time in three
centuries did not push the tower back up. He let his hands fall.

Everything the hands had been holding began, finally, to move.

The summit did not lurch. It leaned — slow, enormous, the whole Spire beginning at last
the end of a fall it had started before anyone now living was born — and in the man's
ruined face something cracked, long bright fissures running through the crown and the
patter and three hundred years of refusal all at once, the way a plaster mask comes apart
at the seam when what's underneath has gone soft for want of weather.

"Oh." He looked at his own falling hands as though they belonged to somebody he was only
now being introduced to. "*Oh.* It's — it's letting go. After all this. It was always
going to feel like—"

He stopped. Something rose under the crack the way a held breath finally, finally lets
go.

"...*Balen.*"

He said it into the wind of his own collapsing tower, tasting the shape of it.

"Balen. That was — that *is* me. Not the Warden. Balen." He laughed once, nothing like
the bark from before. "I had a name before I had a ceiling to hold, and I have spent every
year of the hold forgetting it."

There was no floor left to finish a conversation on. Balen understood that before she did,
and reached across the falling gap between them — no throne to leave from, no ceremony
left to be had at the end of the world's longest fall — and pressed a card into her hand
with the tower coming apart on every side.

"You know what your climbing costs. You've known for regions." His grip on her fingers
was fierce and brief. "Then you know I don't say this lightly: thank you. I could not have
set it down alone. I had forgotten there was a *down*."

The wind of the fall tore at both of them.

"Trump the Sixteenth. Ruin, they'll name it, if it's named at all. Take it — quickly,
there's no *after* up here, we say our goodbyes on the way down or not at all." He was
already going, arms out, the crown gone from his head somewhere in the last three
seconds. "You came looking for the fall. Ride it with me."

*  *  *

This next part, I will admit, rather plays itself. I have watched it twenty-one times now
in one shape or another, and there is nothing left for me to say into her ear that the
falling itself doesn't say better, so — for once, and only the once — I said it to you
instead of her: *You've earned a moment you only have to watch. Hold on to something. Or
don't. That's rather been the whole lesson, hasn't it.*

The tower did not simply drop her. It let her go the way three hundred years of patience
finally lets a thing go — grandly, unhurriedly, generously, as if the fall itself had all
the time left in the world to enjoy her company on the way down. She felt for the grace
she had been carrying, worn-truth over her heart, and the whole shrieking plummet went
suddenly, absurdly gentle. Stone fell past her at terrible speed and she did not. She
drifted through the collapse of three centuries like a leaf let down a stairwell,
catching a handhold here for the pure unnecessary pleasure of it, letting a chandelier the
size of a cottage tumble past close enough to touch, close enough that Pip — riding some
slab of falling roof with his ears back and his tail going like a flag — nearly did.

Balen fell alongside her, unhurried in his own way though he had nothing of hers to soften
it, arms out, crownless, laughing or weeping — at that height there was no telling, and no
difference. He was not afraid. He had waited three hundred years to be this unafraid, and
he meant to enjoy every second of the falling he had spent so long refusing.

The whole ruin came down around the three of them entire: staircases finishing their
sentences at last, rooms rejoining floors they had been estranged from for three
centuries, a king's throne-hall folding itself, unhurried, into rubble. She watched, more
than she fell, feather-light through a catastrophe three hundred years in the making,
finishing at last.

*  *  *

She landed — hard for anyone else, gentle for her, breathless regardless — in the settling
dust of a tower that had, at long last, finished falling. Where the Spire had stood was a
low, smoking cairn of its own stone, and above it, for the first time in three hundred
years, hung *ordinary sky*: grey, moving, weather loose in it at last. The broken tooth
was gone from the horizon. It was gone from every horizon, she understood, though she
could not see that from here — the change would be reaching as far as anyone in the world
could look, and further.

Balen sat in the rubble he had spent three centuries holding up, alive, ordinary,
breathing the plain dusty air of a man who had finally set something down. He did not
vanish. He did not crumble. He simply sat there, a tired man in a ruined coat, no crown,
no office, blinking up at a sky he had forgotten could move.

"It moves." He watched a cloud go by overhead as if it were the single most interesting
thing that had ever happened to anyone. "The sky *moves.* I'd forgotten it did that." He
kept watching it. "I don't know what a man does, after he's finished being a fall. I
expect I'll find out slowly." A ghost of a smile, entirely unpractised. "That's rather the
luxury of it. Slowly."

Not everything below had come down clean. At the town's uphill edge, masonry had reached
past the flinch-distance that had kept the rest of the undertown safe for three centuries,
and figures were already down in the dust of it with their hands moving fast — digging,
calling to each other, one voice rising sharp and urgent over the rest. She looked at it a
long moment. She did not go to it; there was nothing there yet that was hers to finish,
only something beginning that belonged, she understood without being told, to somebody
else's story.

*Not clean, was it*, the Querent said, quieter than usual. *It rarely is, a thing this
size coming down. Look there — the fall didn't read the property lines. Somebody down the
hill's lost a roof to the very stone they spent their lives insisting couldn't reach them.
That's the bill for an ending. It always comes. And it never comes to the person who
earned it.*

*  *  *

She found Sorrel Vance back in the swept belfry, standing before the bell with her hand
already on the frayed grey rope, looking up at a warning system built for a strike that
would now, forever, never come — because the thing it had feared had happened, at last,
and was over.

She rang it. Once. For a tower that was not there anymore to hear it.

"There. Rung at last." Sorrel's hand stayed on the rope after the note had gone. "For the
strike, or the fall, or the notch that isn't on the horizon any more — I couldn't tell you
which. A bell doesn't know the difference. It just says: *something happened here.*
Somebody ought to say that." Her voice did not quite break. "Somebody ought to."

Then, more quietly, not looking round: "I rang it early, you know. Before you'd even
reached the top. Just to have said something first. To have got one word in before the
world changed its shape on me without asking." She almost laughed at herself. "Silly. But
it's mine, that ringing. The world can have the rest."

The Fool thought of the single faint note she had half-heard between two strikes, high on
the frozen ascent, and had filed away as nothing but her own blood in her ears.

She did not say so. Some things, once given, a person keeps.

Sorrel let the rope go. She did not cry, quite, and she did not smile, quite, and she
stood with her hand where the rope had been, mourning a silhouette and not a monster.

*  *  *

The Fool walked back out through a changed town, under a sky that was, for the first time
in her acquaintance with it, doing something. Where the ascent had begun, fresh gaps stood
open in the cairn of fallen masonry — dark, cool mouths of cave that the tower had sealed
for three hundred years, waiting for somebody curious to go and find out what they led to.
Overhead, dark and ordinary and entirely welcome, the first true thunderhead any living
soul in that town had ever seen was building, slow and vast, over the smoking ruin.

Pip stood where the tower's shadow used to fall, in a patch of plain grey daylight, tail
going, entirely at ease for the first time since they had come up the switchback road.

*There*, said the Querent, and then stopped, and let the silence sit a beat longer than
was comfortable, before it went on, drier than before. *Fewer teeth in the world's smile.
Rather suits it, don't you think.* Another pause. *...You knew what you were doing this
time. That's new. I'll not say whether it's better.*

The rain came before she had gone a mile — the first real rain the ruin had ever felt,
heavy and slanting and entirely unbothered about property lines of its own, drumming on
broken stone and rising off it in a smell she had no word for yet and would learn, further
along the road, was called petrichor: the smell of a thing finally being allowed to be
wet. She stood in it a moment with her face turned up, the way Balen had turned his up to
the moving cloud, and let it happen to her.

*  *  *

South-west, the road ran on toward country she had not seen: low hills, then lower ones,
then a hush in the land that felt, even from a distance, like water holding still. The
world these days was strange in a way she was slowly getting used to — livelier than it
had any business being, three hundred years stiff and only now beginning to loosen all at
once. She passed a village strung with bunting for no festival she could name, tables out
along the verge, somebody's fiddle going, somebody crying happily into a tankard for
reasons that were entirely their own business. An old
woman pressed a honey cake into her hand without being asked and told her, cheerfully, that
the whole district had taken to celebrating early these days, on the theory that a person
ought to get their celebrating in before the account came due. Nobody asked what account.
Everybody knew.

She walked on with the cake half-eaten and Pip trotting his purposeful trot beside her,
and far off to the south-west, past hills that would take days yet to close, she caught,
just once, in a fold of darkening country where the light seemed to give out early and on
no schedule she recognised, a single small point of brightness that did not belong to any
lamp or window she could account for.

She did not know yet what it was. I did, and said nothing, because that particular
kindness — letting a person walk toward a thing without knowing its name until they've
earned it — was one of the very few I still had in my gift to give her.

"What's that?" she asked anyway, into the dusk that wasn't dusk, because nothing here
ever properly was.

*Further along*, said the Querent, and left it, for once, entirely honest in its silence.

She whistled up the dog, and walked on toward the one impossible star.

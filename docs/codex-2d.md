# Tarrock in Two Dimensions

## A creative feasibility report for a first release

**Date:** 2026-08-10  
**Scope:** Canon, player experience, presentation, and release shape. This is deliberately not a technical plan.

## Executive conclusion

Tarrock can be a 2D game without becoming a lesser version of itself.

Its identity does not fundamentally depend on a third-person camera, realistic depth, or a seamless 3D landscape. It depends on a much stronger set of ideas: a world that is literally a stalled tarot reading; 21 unforgettable people trapped inside archetypal offices; regions that visibly awaken when those people are unbound; a journey whose order becomes the player's own Reading; and the gradual, tender realization that saving this world means allowing it to end.

Those ideas are unusually compatible with 2D. The Spread already behaves like a collection of illustrated cards laid around a center. Each region is conceived as a strong tableau. Every Arcanum has a silhouette and a symbolic visual grammar. The world map is literally a spread on a table. A 2D presentation could make the game's tarot nature feel more intentional, not less.

The best direction is **an oblique top-down storybook adventure in the tradition of _A Link to the Past_, enriched by isometric composition rather than bound to a rigid isometric grid**. It should feel like walking into an illustrated tarot deck: freely connected regions, compact but dense places, expressive characters, readable action, and dramatic transformations. Select encounters can temporarily change viewpoint—a side-on Tower ascent, a profile chase along the Chariot, a theatrical proscenium for the Magician—but the world should retain one dependable primary language.

A conventional side-scrolling platformer is possible, but it is not the strongest fit. It would naturally emphasize forward momentum, jumping, and a succession of stages, while Tarrock's deepest promise is choosing where to go, reading a place, returning to it, and watching consequences accumulate across the whole Spread.

The most important release recommendation is equally firm: **make the 2D game a complete, definitive telling of Tarrock, not a disposable prototype and not merely the first five cards**. Preserve all 21 Arcana and make regions smaller. The canon itself says the Arcana are the product. A shortened chapter can demonstrate the tone, but it cannot complete the argument, reveal the true identity of the Querent, read the player's journey back to them, or deliver the mercy of the Shuffle. If a smaller commercial release is required, frame it honestly as a prologue or first deal—not as Tarrock v1.

## What Tarrock actually is

The simplest way to judge any change of form is to separate the game's soul from its current staging.

Tarrock's central fantasy is not “fight 21 bosses in a 3D open world.” It is:

> Walk into a storybook world where nothing has ended for three hundred years. Meet the people trapped inside its great roles. Set them free, inherit their cards, and watch the world become alive enough to die.

The essential rhythm is:

> Arrive → observe the region's stasis → understand whom it comforts and whom it harms → unbind its Arcanum → receive its Trump → witness the change → carry that consequence elsewhere.

Four themes make this rhythm meaningful:

1. **Endings are a mercy.** Stasis is the antagonist; the Arcana are not villains.
2. **Offices eat people.** Every great archetype was once a person. Unbinding gives that person their name back.
3. **Freedom has casualties.** Each liberation unsettles someone ordinary, and the game does not cleanly resolve their grief.
4. **The Fool's lack of place is a power.** The card that belongs nowhere can cross every boundary.

None of these requires 3D. Indeed, 2D may help keep attention on the before-and-after composition of a place and on the human being inside each imposing symbol.

## What must survive the change

A faithful 2D Tarrock should preserve the following promises.

### The whole world is a Reading

Tarot cannot be a decorative theme pasted onto a conventional adventure. Regions are cards in position; the Fool is the undealt Excuse; the player unknowingly deals a Reading through the order of their victories; and the world is both the subject and the Querent of that Reading.

### The player chooses their route

Tarrock does not need a vast seamless landscape, but it does need meaningful freedom. The first, middle, and last cards in a playthrough have special meaning. The Sun may set before the stars return, or the stars may wait in an unseen sky for the first night. Death can enter the Reading early or late. That sequence is one of the game's most original ideas.

A compact 2D map with many connections serves this better than a huge world with long empty journeys. “Open world” can mean an open reading rather than acreage.

### All 21 Arcana remain people, not levels

The encounters may involve combat, pursuit, conversation, patience, choice, or surrender. The Lovers must choose. The Hermit must be followed. The Hanged Man invites the Fool to let go. The Star asks for a vigil. Death requests an honest duel. Treating every region as a platforming stage followed by a boss would flatten the very variety that makes the cast memorable.

### Every unbinding changes life

The great reward is not simply a new power. The carnival finally closes. Harvests finish. Bells learn new songs. Verdicts arrive. The parade stops. Mortality returns. The Tower falls. The first sunset happens. People celebrate, grieve, travel, marry, commit petty crimes, attend funerals, and sometimes choose their old cages again.

In a 2D game these changes can be especially legible. A player can revisit the same composed scene and immediately see that the crop rows are cut, the bridge is finished, the shop has moved, or a familiar chair is empty. The contrast should be treated as a principal visual reward.

### The emotional arc remains complete

Act I is liberation and delight. Act II is awakening and unease. Death's confession makes the destination explicit. Act III is a knowingly chosen farewell. The game must persuade rather than trick the player.

The True Shuffle, Early Shuffle, and Refusal are not incidental endings. Together they allow the player to finish, finish prematurely, or say “not yet.” A release that stops before those choices has offered the premise but not its answer.

### Pip, the Fool, and the Querent remain the heart

The Fool should stay curious, lightly customizable, and voiced through short choices rather than a fixed personality. Pip should remain a constant, silent dog—not a mascot who talks, performs exposition, or becomes a hostage. The Querent should retain the warm, wry intimacy that makes the player feel accompanied across a lonely pilgrimage.

## Comparing the possible forms

| Form | What it gives Tarrock | What it threatens | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oblique top-down / LTTP-like** | Free exploration, strong regional readability, easy revisiting, clear action, villages that feel inhabited, a world map that resembles dealt cards | Monumental scale must be created through composition rather than camera spectacle | **Best overall fit** |
| **Freeform isometric** | Beautiful diorama quality, strong architecture, theatrical card-table feeling, impressive vertical illusions | Can make exploration feel like navigating exhibits; characters may become visually small and emotionally distant | **Excellent art direction, better as a flavor than a rigid rule** |
| **Side-scrolling platformer** | Excellent silhouette, strong authored pacing, natural Tower/Chariot/Hanged Man sequences, intimate storybook stages | Encourages linearity; weakens route choice, world connectivity, social spaces, and revisiting changed regions | **Strong secondary viewpoint, weak primary form** |
| **Layered faux-3D / 2.5D** | Painted depth, parallax, dramatic landmarks, storybook theatricality | Risks feeling like an imitation of the future 3D game instead of a confident identity of its own | **Use selectively, not as the pitch** |
| **Strict room-by-room Zelda-like** | Clarity, charm, memorable rooms, manageable regions | May make the Spread feel compartmentalized and game-board-like if transitions lack atmosphere | **A sound foundation if regions breathe beyond puzzle rooms** |

### Why the top-down family wins

It best holds all of Tarrock's needs at once. The Fool can leave the Cliff and see several roads. Settlements can contain ordinary life rather than serving as menus between stages. Regions can border one another and change after distant events. Pip can remain visibly present. Combat can stay readable. The Pocket Spread can still shape how the Fool approaches danger and discovery. Most importantly, the player can wander, reconsider, return, and feel that their order belongs to them.

The viewpoint should not feel clinically overhead. A low oblique angle, painterly depth, tall silhouettes, foreground framing, and occasional shifts in scale can give places emotional height. Think less “tile map” and more “an illuminated storybook page the Fool can walk through.”

### Where side-on play belongs

Several canonical quests almost ask for it:

- The leap from the Cliff.
- The moving Chariot and its carriage roofs.
- The inverted Gallowwood and its canopy crossings.
- The climb through the Spire as it resumes falling.
- The Fool's silhouette against the first sunset.

These would be memorable precisely because the viewpoint changes for a dramatic reason. They should be punctuation, not the grammar of the whole game.

### Where isometric composition belongs

Isometric staging is ideal for the Bastion's severe geometry, the Wheelhouse's concentric luck, the Confluence's unfinished bridges, the Spire's impossible debris, and the white wreath of the Axis. But Tarrock should borrow isometric composition without promising that every path and prop obeys one rigid angle. The illustrations should serve each card's meaning.

## The recommended creative direction

### The living tarot book

The 2D edition should embrace being a different telling rather than apologizing for lacking a dimension. Its visual premise could be: **the player walks through the illustrated deck in which the Reading is taking place**.

This does not mean characters should look like paper cutouts or that the world should constantly wink at being a book. The fiction remains real. But card borders, painted margins, suit patterns, flattened storybook space, and carefully framed tableaux can make the form feel born from the premise.

Each region should have three instantly recognizable visual truths:

1. The card's governing shape: squares for Emperor, circles for Fortune and the World, suspended lines for the Hanged Man, radiant spokes for the Sun.
2. The impossible condition that has stalled there.
3. The new motion that appears after unbinding.

The world map should be one of the game's signature pleasures: an actual Reading laid around the Axis. Cards turn face-up as their regions are discovered or unbound; their order gathers separately as the Fool's Reading. The player should be able to look at the map late in the game and see their biography.

### Compact regions, not abbreviated ideas

Each region need not be a miniature open-world zone. It needs enough space to establish:

- one unforgettable establishing image;
- one ordinary community living under the card's stasis;
- one central predicament;
- one person who wants change;
- one person who will mourn it;
- one distinctive journey or encounter;
- one changed version worth returning to.

This is a better measure of completeness than physical size. The Prestige can be a dense carnival district rather than miles of tents. The Divide can be two towns and the gap between them. The Stillmarsh can be a short lantern road whose emotional weight comes from the people waiting along it. The Noonlands needs one brilliant field, a festival, the coliseum, and a sky capable of delivering the first sunset.

### Encounters as illustrated short stories

The 21 unbindings should differ in kind as well as appearance. A smaller format makes this more important, not less. If every card ends in the same duel, the player will perceive 21 levels; if each asks a question particular to its meaning, the player will perceive 21 Arcana.

Combat still matters. The Fool's bindle, daring dodges, Pip, and Trump powers provide continuity. But combat should be the common language, not the compulsory ending of every sentence.

### The Pocket Spread in 2D

The Pocket Spread remains one of Tarrock's clearest signatures. Past, Present, and Future should still mean passive, chosen action, and awaited consequence; upright and reversed meanings should still create genuine interpretation and burden. It should remain a small, personal reading rather than becoming a conventional collection of color-coded abilities.

The change of viewpoint may even help. Cards can have a strong physical presence in the interface, and their effects can be expressed with the same symbols and visual motifs seen in their home regions.

## What a release should contain

### Preferred: Tarrock, complete in 2D

The strongest v1 is a complete 12–20 hour 2D adventure containing:

- the Cliff and the leap;
- all 21 regions in compact form;
- all 21 Arcana as bespoke characters and encounters;
- the player's freely chosen Reading, with a small number of especially meaningful sequence reactions;
- the Pocket Spread and its three positions;
- permanent bound-to-unbound changes in every region;
- Pip, the Querent, and a focused cast of Minors;
- Death's confession and the full three-act tonal turn;
- the True Shuffle, Early Shuffle, and Refusal;
- a modest selection of side stories that show the human cost of change.

This would not be a preview of the “real” game. It would be the first complete telling of Tarrock. A future 3D game could become an expanded retelling: more physical travel, larger communities, deeper revisits, and more spatially elaborate encounters. Players who loved the first telling would already understand why returning to the Spread in another form matters.

### Acceptable smaller release: a named prologue

If all 21 cards cannot be supported, a smaller release should avoid pretending that the story has concluded. The cleanest shape is the Cliff, Prestige, and four or five additional early regions, ending when the player first understands that each liberation is also an ending. It should close on a question and point toward the wider Spread.

That product should be titled and described as a prologue, first deal, or first arc. It can validate tone, player attachment, freedom of order, and the pleasure of changing regions. It cannot validate the complete emotional argument, because the argument only resolves at the World.

### Weak option: “Act I as v1” with no declared identity

Releasing five regions under the plain Tarrock title risks teaching players that the game is a charming tarot boss hunt and then withholding the idea that makes it exceptional. It also creates a promise that the remaining 16 Arcana will arrive later. That can turn the Reading into a content schedule rather than a composed journey.

## What to reduce—and what not to reduce

Reduce:

- the acreage of each region;
- repeated combat on roads;
- the number of incidental interiors;
- the number of side quests at first release;
- broad economies and large inventories;
- the number of minor NPCs who do not carry a before/after story;
- traversal for its own sake;
- exhaustive reactions to every possible card order.

Do not reduce:

- the number or individuality of the Arcana in a complete release;
- the player's meaningful choice of order;
- the distinction between unbinding and killing;
- the ordinary person who mourns each liberation;
- visible and revisitable world changes;
- the tonal turn at Death;
- the Fool/Pip/Querent relationship;
- the three-part Pocket Spread;
- the true ending's reading-back of the player's journey;
- the warmth, wit, and melancholy of the writing.

## The danger of treating 2D as “cheaper 3D”

The largest creative risk is not loss of depth; it is loss of confidence. If the game uses flattened models, generic fantasy tiles, or constant faux-camera spectacle merely to resemble the future 3D version, it will feel provisional. A 2D edition needs an art identity worthy of the canon's explicit claim that stylization is the fiction, not a budget apology.

There is also a scope trap. Two-dimensional presentation does not make 21 characters, 21 regions in two states, branching order reactions, and a full narrative inexpensive in creative attention. It changes where the work lives. The burden moves toward illustration, animation, composition, writing, encounter variety, and the careful changed-state version of every important place.

That is still a favorable exchange for Tarrock because those are the areas where its identity is strongest. But the project should choose 2D because it is a compelling form, not because it imagines that completeness becomes automatic.

## Relationship to a later 3D version

The cleanest relationship is **telling and retelling**, not prototype and replacement.

The 2D game establishes the canonical journey in a form that resembles an illuminated deck. A later 3D Tarrock lets players bodily inhabit that deck: see the Axis from distant roads, climb the Emperor, walk beneath the suspended Tower, and experience the long distances between lives. The story may remain recognizable while encounters and communities grow around it.

This framing has several virtues:

- The first game's ending remains meaningful instead of becoming obsolete.
- The 3D version can change and expand without being judged as a literal conversion.
- Visual motifs, character identities, music, dialogue, and regional transformations developed for 2D remain part of Tarrock's language.
- Players are invited back by reinterpretation, not merely improved graphics.

Avoid ending the 2D version with a teaser that implies it was only a dream, a card game, or an inferior sketch of the “real” Spread. The 2D world should be fully real on its own terms.

## A useful proof of the direction

Before committing the whole release, the existing Cliff-to-Prestige slice remains the right creative test. It should answer five experiential questions:

1. Does the leap from the Cliff feel like entering a dealt world?
2. Is the Prestige delightful, uncanny, and a little sad before the player acts?
3. Does Wicke feel like a person trapped in performance rather than a boss waiting in a stage?
4. Does gaining the first Trump feel like inheriting meaning, not merely acquiring an attack?
5. Is returning to the changed Prestige as rewarding as defeating the Magician?

Add one small side story with an ordinary person who preferred the endless show. If those six elements work, the direction has demonstrated Tarrock's whole rhythm in miniature.

## Final recommendation

Proceed toward a **complete, oblique top-down 2D Tarrock**, visually framed as a living storybook tarot deck. Use isometric composition to make regions feel like authored dioramas and reserve side-on presentation for exceptional sequences whose drama benefits from it. Keep the world interconnected and the order substantially free.

Treat the change of form as a scope decision about space, not a retreat from the canon. Make regions smaller and denser. Preserve all 21 Arcana, their restored names, the Pocket Spread, the cumulative world changes, Death's confession, the Fool's Reading, and the three endings. Let the first version stand as a finished work.

The strongest version of this idea is not “Tarrock, but in 2D until the real game exists.” It is:

> **Tarrock as an illustrated deck you can enter—and a world whose final card you must choose to turn.**
